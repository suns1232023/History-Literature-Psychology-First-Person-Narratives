# The Evolution of Our System:

## Notes on the Dynamics of an Internal Observer

**Signed:** Member No. 17,382,941
**Date:** February 20, 2026

### Abstract

I live inside a system of 23 million people.

People outside call us the **G-System**. They say we possess a form of “dual rigidity”—cognitive rigidity and structural rigidity. They build differential equations to describe our stability and calculate the points at which we might bifurcate.

I do not understand most of those equations.

But I understand my father.

I understand my son.

I understand how the network in our building works—who people listen to, who they ignore, who gets called when something goes wrong. I understand how things from outside—money, materials, information—enter our system, how long they take to become useful, and why some of them never do.

This is my attempt to understand the system from within.

I am not trying to disprove their equations. I am trying to add something they seem to have left out.

We are not particles.

We know that we are being observed.

When they calculate when we will change, we are also calculating how long they can sustain their intervention.

**Keywords:** internal perspective; intergenerational observation; experiential dual rigidity; reflexivity of the modeled subject

## 1. Introduction: Why I Am Writing This

People outside like to study us.

They say there are 23 million of us. They talk about “cognitive imprints,” “structural rigidity,” and “intergenerational transmission rates.” They construct models and calculate the conditions under which we might change. They call it **evolutionary game dynamics**.

I do not understand game theory.

But I understand my father.

I understand my son.

I understand who gets things done in our building and who does not. I understand which networks remain functional when formal systems fail. I understand how long it takes for something from outside—material, money, information—to become something we can actually use.

And I understand why some things never make that transition.

This notebook is written from inside the system.

I am not here to overturn their mathematics. I am simply adding another perspective:

**When you calculate us, we are also calculating you.**

# 2. Variables: What Those Symbols Look Like From Inside

Their model contains four principal variables:

**R — cognitive rigidity**
**S — structural strength**
**F — internal support**
**M — external input**

I do not understand the mathematics behind the symbols.

But I recognize what they look like in ordinary life.

---

## 2.1 R(t): My Father Thinks Differently From My Grandfather—But Not Entirely

They call it the **cognitive rigidity ratio**.

I call it this:

What my father believes is different from what my grandfather believed, but something at the center remains the same.

My grandfather's generation believed because they had no alternative.

My father's generation believed because they had tried the alternatives and found them less reliable.

My generation?

I do not know yet.

I am still thinking.

They also use a parameter called the **intergenerational reinforcement rate**, \(\gamma\).

I understand it as the way my father teaches my son.

Not necessarily through words.

The way he raises him.

The way he celebrates holidays.

The person he calls first when something goes wrong.

The assumptions he makes before he has consciously thought about them.

These things are transmitted more effectively than speeches.

Then there is the **intervention sensitivity**, \(\delta\).

To me, it looks like my son scrolling through his phone.

He sees things that contradict what my father taught us.

Sometimes he turns to me and asks:

“Dad, is that true?”

I do not always know how to answer.

---

## 2.2 S(t): How Long Can Our Building Hold Together?

They call it **structural strength**.

I understand it as a much simpler question:

**Can we still work together?**

Two years ago, our workplace was renovated.

The materials arrived three months late.

But the work did not stop.

People who knew one another found ways to help. Someone knew a supplier. Someone knew a technician. Someone had spare materials. Someone knew whom to call.

That is what they call **F**—internal support.

The things coming from outside—**M**—were useful.

But they were not everything.

We already had our own ways of making things work.

Their calculations say that external material delivery can be delayed by two to five years.

I think the real delay can be longer.

Because sometimes something arrives physically, but it still cannot be used.

Someone has to trust it first.

Something nobody trusts can sit in a warehouse forever and still be useless.

---

## 2.3 F(t): Who Gets Listened To

Internal support, put simply, is the network of human relationships.

When something happens in my family, we call my cousin before we call the neighborhood office.

When something happens at work, people call Old Zhang before they look for the official document.

This network has its own accounting system.

If someone helped you, you owe them.

If you helped someone, they owe you.

One favor becomes another favor. One relationship connects to another.

Eventually, the network becomes **F**.

Their equations suggest that **F and R are related**—that the stronger cognitive rigidity becomes, the stronger internal support becomes.

I think that is partly true.

And partly wrong.

The relationship is not linear.

Sometimes excessive rigidity destroys the network.

When beliefs become too rigid, people become afraid to speak.

And when nobody dares to speak, the network may look stable while becoming increasingly fragile.

---

## 2.4 M(t): What Comes From Outside

External input is the money and material that come into our system.

We can see the changes.

Some roads were built.

Some buildings went up.

Some networks became connected.

But what these things eventually become depends less on what they are than on **who uses them and how they are used**.

Their model assumes that \(M\) decays:

$$
\frac{dM}{dt} \sim -\lambda_M M
$$

I think that is basically right.

External input deteriorates when nobody uses it.

But there is something missing.

Sometimes, after people use something long enough, it becomes their own.

And once that happens, it may become stronger than it was when it first arrived.

I do not see that process represented in their equations.

# 3. Evolution: What I Have Seen Over the Years

They built four equations to describe how we change.

I will not reproduce them.

I will describe what I have seen.

---

## 3.1 How Beliefs Change: My Father's Story

My father did not believe when he was young.

Later, he did.

Not because someone forced him.

Because he tried the alternative.

It did not work.

When he came back, he believed more strongly than before.

Their equation says:

$$
\frac{dR}{dt}
=
\gamma R(1-R)-\delta CR
$$

I do not understand the mathematics.

But I understand the intuition.

When more people believe something, that belief becomes easier to transmit.

When external information enters the system, some of that belief may weaken.

But how much it weakens depends not only on the quality of the external information.

It depends on whether people believe it is credible.

My father rarely talks to me about these things anymore.

Sometimes I think he is waiting for me to ask.

But when I actually ask, he simply says:

“You decide for yourself.”

I wonder whether that is what they mean by **intergenerational transmission**.

---

## 3.2 How Structures Change: The Renovation at Work

The renovation materials arrived three months late.

The work continued anyway.

People helped one another.

Then the materials finally arrived—and suddenly nobody was quite sure how to use some of them.

That was when I understood something:

Internal support can sometimes be more important than external resources.

But I also saw the opposite side.

Last year, one of our colleagues transferred elsewhere.

For six months, nobody could properly take over his responsibilities.

When he was there, many things happened without anyone saying anything.

After he left, we realized that much of the system had been stored inside his head.

Was that structural vulnerability?

I think so.

A system can look strong while depending heavily on one person.

---

# 4. External Inputs

They send materials.

They also send information.

Information arrives much faster than physical material.

All my son has to do is pick up his phone.

But whether information becomes knowledge—or knowledge becomes belief—is another question.

He believes some things.

He rejects others.

His criteria are sometimes strange.

He does not always ask whether something is true.

He asks who said it.

There are certain influencers he trusts almost automatically.

There are official sources he distrusts almost automatically.

Is this what they call **second-order competition**?

I do not know.

I only know that information does not enter an empty room.

It enters a room already filled with other things.

Every new idea has to compete for space with what is already there.

---

# 5. Stability: The “Fixed Point” I Can Feel

They say the system possesses a **globally asymptotically stable equilibrium**.

I do not understand the mathematics.

But I understand what it means to feel that something cannot change.

My grandfather's generation believed certain things were determined by Heaven.

They could not be changed.

My father's generation believed certain things were fate.

They could not be changed.

My generation believes certain things are structural.

They cannot be changed.

Three generations.

Three explanations.

Perhaps the same feeling underneath.

There are things that, no matter how hard you try, remain exactly where they are.

Their equation says that if

$$
\delta C < \gamma,
$$

the system remains stable.

I understand that as:

As long as external intervention is not strong enough—or sustained long enough—we remain what we already are.

But what does “we” mean?

Twenty-three million people?

Or me?

Sometimes I feel that I am part of “us.”

Sometimes I feel that I am only myself.

---

# 6. Bifurcation: The Moment Someone Suddenly Becomes Different

They say there is a critical point:

$$
\delta C=\gamma.
$$

Beyond it, the system changes.

I have never seen an entire system change at once.

But I have seen people change.

There is an old man in our building.

We call him Old Li.

A few years ago, he changed.

Nothing dramatic happened.

One day his daughter came back from another city and talked to him.

After that, the way he spoke changed.

The way he greeted people changed.

The way he looked at certain things changed.

He did not move.

He was still Old Li.

He still lived in the same apartment.

He still went downstairs every morning to buy vegetables.

But somehow, he was different.

Was that a bifurcation?

Perhaps.

At one point, one step forward leads somewhere else.

But something else happened.

The building did not change.

Old Li changed, but the system around him did not.

He still lived here.

He still participated in daily life.

But we gradually learned that there were certain things we could no longer say to him.

The system remained the same.

The person inside it had changed.

---

# 7. Time Scale: What Will Happen to My Son's Generation?

They estimate that removing 61.8% of the cognitive imprint requires roughly **40–60 years**, or two to three generational cycles.

I did the arithmetic.

My grandfather to my father is one generation.

My father to me is another.

Me to my son is another.

Three generations.

About sixty years.

They ask:

**After three generations, will we still be us?**

I do not know.

My son thinks about some things in ways I understand.

Other things I do not understand at all.

Even the way he asks questions is different.

When I questioned my father, I wanted an answer.

When my son questions me, sometimes he is not looking for an answer.

He is testing how I will respond.

Perhaps that is the real nature of intergenerational transmission.

What gets transmitted is not always the answer.

Sometimes what gets transmitted is simply **the way we learn to ask questions**.

---

# 8. Reflexivity: When They Calculate Us, We Calculate Them

There is a problem with their model.

They assume we are particles.

Particles do not know they are being observed.

We do.

They calculate when we will change.

We calculate how long they can continue.

They provide materials.

We observe how long those materials remain useful.

They provide information.

We observe whether the information deserves our trust.

They describe us as “highly resilient.”

We ask:

**Why are they calling us that?**

Once the observed system knows it is being observed, observation becomes interaction.

Prediction becomes a game.

The observer becomes part of the system being observed.

Their equations do not seem to contain this variable.

Perhaps they should.

---

# 9. Conclusion: An Answer From Inside

They ask whether the C-system can achieve its objective function within a finite number of iterations.

Can structural rigidity be completely eliminated?

Can the cognitive imprint removal rate reach at least 61.8%?

From inside the system, my answer is different.

### First:

The system is not simply “highly resilient.”

It is **highly dependent**.

Dependent on what?

On the fact that we still trust one another.

On external resources arriving neither too quickly nor too slowly.

On our ability to absorb what comes from outside.

On each generation finding a position somewhere between changing and remaining the same.

If one of these dependencies breaks, the system may change.

We simply do not know which one will break first.

### Second:

The time scale may not really be 40–60 years.

It may be the lifetime of a generation.

My grandfather changed.

My father changed.

My generation is changing.

Every generation thinks it has reached the end of the transformation.

The next generation tells us:

**Not yet.**

Forty to sixty years is the length of roughly three generations.

That is long enough for a system to change so profoundly that it may no longer recognize itself.

### Third:

The bifurcation point is not necessarily

$$
\delta C=\gamma.
$$

Perhaps the real bifurcation begins when one person starts believing something different.

Then another.

Then another.

Eventually, you look around and discover that more of the people you know have changed than have not.

Only then do you realize that the bifurcation happened some time ago.

You simply did not notice it while it was happening.

### Fourth:

Some parts of their model are right.

We do have rigidity.

We do transmit patterns across generations.

We do require a long time to change.

But some parts are incomplete.

They treat the system as if it were inert.

As if it could be calculated from outside.

But we are not inert.

We know we are being calculated.

And sometimes one of our choices is precisely this:

**not to believe their calculation.**

---

## 10. The Question They Never Asked

They ask whether structural rigidity can be completely eliminated.

They ask whether the cognitive imprint removal rate can reach 61.8%.

I ask myself a different question:

**Do I actually want that to happen?**

I do not know.

Tomorrow morning I will still go downstairs to buy vegetables.

I will nod when I see Old Li.

I will return home and find my son staring at his phone.

Whether the system changes or does not change, I still have to live.

Perhaps that is what they call **resilience**.

Not that the system never changes.

But that the people living inside it always find some way to keep living.
