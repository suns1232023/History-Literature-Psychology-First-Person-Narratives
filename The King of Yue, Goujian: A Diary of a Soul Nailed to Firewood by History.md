The King of Yue, Goujian: A Diary of a Soul Nailed to Firewood by History

The First-Person Testament of a Ghost Condemned by Posterity

Autumn, 491 BCE — Stables, Gusu, Wu

It rained again today.

The autumn rain of Wu is colder than that of Yue. It falls across my back like the repeated blows of a blunt blade. The ragged clothes I am wearing have not been changed for three months—not because I refuse to change them, but because there is nothing else to wear.

When I came here, Wenzhong searched through the treasuries of Yue and managed to gather three hundred bolts of silk, twenty pairs of jade discs, and a retinue of women. Only then did Fuchai agree to let me come to Wu “as a slave,” together with my wife and Fan Li.

Of the three hundred people who followed me here, more than half have already died—of disease, starvation, or the lashes of Wu soldiers.

I remember the day we left.

The people of Yue knelt along the mountain road to Kuaiji, weeping. I sat in the ox cart and refused to look back.

It was not that I was afraid of their tears.

I was afraid that I would be the one to cry first.

I was the king of Yue. A king could not weep before his subjects.

Yet the tears kept rising into my throat all the way down the road, choking me until I could barely breathe.

Today, once again, I fed Fuchai's horses.

To call it “feeding horses” would be generous.

In truth, I am the horse.

Fuchai's horses have the finest hay, the cleanest troughs, and attendants who spread fresh straw beneath them.

And I?

I eat the leftovers discarded by Wu prisoners. I sleep on moldy straw beside piles of horse manure.

Before dawn each morning, the guards come and kick my ribs, shouting in Yue:

“Goujian! Get up!”

They deliberately pronounce my name as though they were calling a dog.

I crawl to my feet, lower my head, bend my back, and brush the hair of Fuchai's horses, fill their troughs, and clean the mud from their hooves.

I am worth less than a horse now.

But I cannot die.

I must live.

I must live long enough to return to Kuaiji.

Tonight Fan Li secretly came to the stable.

He is thinner than when we arrived. His beard has grown tangled, and his eyes are filled with broken veins.

He whispered:

“My King, the Queen is ill.”

My heart tightened.

“What illness?”

“She had a high fever last night. The Wu officials refused to summon a physician. She has been doing hard labor in the palace and may have collapsed from exhaustion.”

I bit my lip and said nothing.

Fan Li continued:

“Wenzhong has sent word secretly. He has already made arrangements in Yue and is trying to bribe Bo Pi, the Grand Steward of Wu. He is greedy and lustful. If we can win him over, there may be a chance for Your Majesty to return home.”

I nodded.

Yet inside I thought:

Wenzhong can still send messages from Yue.

I have spent three years as a slave in Wu without daring to write even a single line.

Not because I am unwilling.

Because I cannot.

Fuchai has spies watching me day and night. If I reveal even the slightest sign of defiance, tomorrow there will be one more head on the execution ground outside Gusu.

I remember when I first arrived in Wu.

I knelt before Fuchai, pressing my forehead against the ground until it bruised purple.

Again and again I said:

“Your servant Goujian deserves death. I ask only to serve Your Majesty like a dog or a horse.”

Fuchai sat high upon his throne, one leg crossed over the other.

Beside him stood Wu Zixu.

He looked at me as one might look at a dying fish—contemptuous, detached, already certain that I was a dead man.

Bo Pi, however, stood nearby smiling.

His smile was gentle.

Almost friendly.

And somehow that frightened me more.

That was when I understood something:

When a person ceases to be a person in the eyes of others and becomes merely an object, he can finally see everyone clearly.

Wu Zixu repeatedly urged Fuchai to kill me.

“If Yue is not destroyed now,” he warned, “you will regret it later.”

He said it in front of me more than once.

Each time, he looked at me not as a human being, but as a future threat—a danger that had not yet struck.

I often thought:

Wu Zixu, Wu Zixu.

You fled Chu for Wu, using Wu's armies to avenge your father and brother. You even ordered the tomb of King Ping of Chu opened and his corpse flogged three hundred times.

If anyone understands the weight of revenge, it is you.

You want Fuchai to kill me because you fear I will become another you.

But Fuchai refused to listen.

He was obsessed with glory. He wanted to dominate the Central States. He needed Yue alive—as a vassal state that could provide horses, tribute, and soldiers.

I was more useful to him alive than dead.

So I lived.

But I lived as a corpse.

Every morning before sunrise: feed horses, shovel manure, chop wood, carry water.

Wu guards spat in my face. I did not dare wipe it away.

They cursed me behind my back in the Wu dialect:

“Yue dog.”

I understood every word.

I did not turn around.

Once, Fuchai happened to visit the stables.

He saw me sweeping horse manure and asked with amusement:

“Goujian, how do you find your new life as a stableman?”

I knelt in the mud, pressed my forehead to the ground, and answered:

“To serve Your Majesty's horses is the greatest blessing of three lifetimes.”

He laughed.

Then he casually threw his riding crop against the back of my head and walked away.

That night I crouched alone in the corner of the stable.

I picked up the riding crop.

It was still warm from Fuchai's hand.

I held it throughout the night, my fingers digging into the leather until my palms bled.

I did not hate him.

I dared not hate him.

Because if I allowed myself to hate him, I might reveal something.

And a single crack in my mask meant death.

Yet I knew my heart was no longer a heart.

It had become a piece of red-hot iron, plunged repeatedly into water, cooled, reheated, and cooled again.

My fire of revenge could not be seen by anyone.

But it had never gone out.

It burned somewhere beneath my bones.

And it kept me awake night after night.

Three years.

I have pretended for three years.

Winter, 488 BCE — Palace of Yue, Kuaiji

I am home.

To call this place a palace would be a joke.

The Wu army stripped Kuaiji bare.

The royal palace is now nothing more than broken walls. Even the thatch on the roof was torn away and burned by Wu soldiers.

I stood among the ruins.

The foundations of the great hall were covered with weeds.

The wind moved through them with a dry rustling sound.

It sounded as though the grass itself were weeping for me.

When Wenzhong came to receive me, he knelt and refused to rise.

He cried.

I cried too.

But I quickly wiped my face.

Tears were useless.

They would only remind me of the humiliation I had suffered in Wu.

And humiliation is a poison.

It corrodes the mind of a king.

Fan Li accompanied me all the way home.

We barely spoke.

He understood me.

He knew what I wanted to say.

He also knew what I could not say.

The first thing I did after returning was hang a gallbladder from the beam of the ruined hall.

Not for others to see.

For me.

Every morning I look up at it.

And I remember Wu.

Before every meal, I taste a drop of its bitterness.

The taste spreads from the tip of my tongue down into my throat and stomach, like a dull knife cutting through my organs.

But even the bitterness of the gallbladder is nothing compared with the humiliation I endured in Wu.

Still, bitterness has one purpose.

It reminds me:

Goujian, do not forget.

If you forget, you deserve to die.

As for the famous “sleeping on firewood”—

Yes, I did sleep on firewood.

But not because of some grand principle called “sleeping on firewood and tasting gall.”

That phrase was polished by later scholars.

The simple truth is that the palace had been destroyed.

There were no beds.

No blankets.

Only firewood.

Fan Li told me that a king could not sleep on straw.

I told him I could.

I would sleep as the people of Yue slept.

Eat what they ate.

Wear what they wore.

I was their king.

But I could not live better than they did.

If I covered myself in silk and slept upon ivory while my people starved, what would they think?

They would say:

Goujian knew hardship when he was a slave in Wu, but forgot it the moment he returned home.

So I must suffer.

I need to suffer.

Suffering is the last armor I possess.

A few days ago, Wenzhong presented me with nine strategies for destroying Wu.

If all were implemented, he said, Yue could swallow Wu within ten years.

I read them carefully.

My heart burned.

But my voice remained calm.

“I understand.”

Then I dismissed him.

It was not because I distrusted Wenzhong.

It was because I had discovered something frightening.

I no longer knew how to laugh.

During those three years in Wu, I learned to maintain an expressionless face.

No matter how deeply I was humiliated, I had to show no hatred.

Eventually, the mask became my face.

When I wanted to smile, the muscles of my face felt frozen.

When I wanted to cry, my eyes remained dry.

Once Fan Li asked me:

“My King, do you remember the last time you laughed from the heart?”

I thought for a long time.

I could not remember.

Suddenly, I was afraid.

I was afraid that I had become a creature capable of only one thing:

revenge.

A hollow shell whose flesh had been consumed by hatred.

Hatred had once been the force that kept me alive.

Now it was growing beyond my control.

But I could not stop.

If I stopped seeking revenge, then everything I had endured in Wu would become meaningless.

The hunger.

The humiliation.

The blood.

The tears I swallowed.

All of it would have been for nothing.

So I could not stop.

Summer, 484 BCE — Palace Hall, Yue

Another letter from Bo Pi.

That Grand Steward of Wu has received countless gifts from us over the years—gold, jade, silk.

Whenever Fuchai discusses Yue, Bo Pi speaks on our behalf.

Wu Zixu rages.

Fuchai listens to Bo Pi.

It is strange, the human heart.

The more loyal Wu Zixu becomes, the more Fuchai thinks him arrogant.

The more Bo Pi flatters him, the more Fuchai believes him sincere.

Sometimes I wonder:

Which of us is more pitiable?

I was a slave in Wu, my body humiliated.

Fuchai was a king in Wu, his mind corrupted.

Our thrones were both built upon lies.

The difference was simple:

My lies were meant to keep me alive. His lies were meant to keep him comfortable.

Today Wenzhong reported that Wu had been exhausting itself in repeated campaigns against Qi, Lu, and Jin.

The kingdom was weakening.

Its people were restless.

Wu Zixu, having repeatedly warned Fuchai in vain, had finally been forced to take his own life by the king.

When I heard that Wu Zixu was dead, I felt many things at once.

He had been my most dangerous enemy.

He was also the man I had most reason to thank.

Had he lived, there would always have been a pair of eyes beside Fuchai watching me.

Now those eyes were gone.

Fuchai's ambition was no longer restrained.

His campaigns to the north had left Wu vulnerable.

That was my opportunity.

And yet I felt a strange sadness.

Was Wu Zixu's fate not also a warning of my own?

He had helped Fuchai build his power.

In the end, he was given a sword and ordered to die.

If I destroyed Wu, would Wenzhong and Fan Li eventually meet the same fate?

I thought about it once.

Then I refused to think about it again.

Because if I thought too much, I might hesitate.

And revenge is a gamble in which there is only forward motion.

Once you hesitate, you lose everything.

Winter, 473 BCE — Gusu, Wu

Wu is dead.

Twenty years of waiting have finally reached their end.

When the banner of Yue rose above the walls of Gusu, I stood upon the battlements.

The wind tore at my robes.

Far away, the palace of Wu burned.

Nearby, soldiers of Yue shouted in triumph.

I gripped the hilt of my sword.

My palm was wet with sweat.

Not warm sweat.

Cold.

Fuchai had killed himself.

Before his death, he sent someone to ask whether I would meet him one last time.

I refused.

Not because I was afraid.

I simply did not know what I could possibly say.

Should I tell him that for twenty years I had slept on firewood, tasted gall, pretended obedience, and secretly prepared my revenge?

Should I tell him that every time I fed his horses in that stable, I swore an oath?

There was no need.

He knew.

Before dying, he reportedly said:

“I regret not listening to Zixu's counsel. I brought this upon myself.”

Too late.

Wu Zixu's bones had already been rotting somewhere in the wilderness for twenty years.

Perhaps his spirit had been waiting in the underworld to witness this day.

Fuchai was dead.

By all reason, I should have been happy.

I should have laughed.

I should have celebrated.

I should have held a feast.

But I could not smile.

I stood upon the walls of Gusu and looked over the ruined city.

Something inside me felt hollow.

Like an old pillow whose stuffing had been ripped out.

I did not understand.

For twenty years, revenge had been my only reason to live.

It had been the force that made me open my eyes each morning.

The last thread holding me together whenever I wanted to give up.

But now—

The revenge was complete.

The kingdom was restored.

Wu was destroyed.

And then what?

For the first time, I had no answer.

The feeling was terrifying.

Like an archer who has drawn his bow to its fullest strength, released the arrow, watched the target fall—

and suddenly finds himself holding an empty bowstring with nowhere left to aim.

Winter, 465 BCE — Palace of Yue, Langya

I know I do not have much time left.

Today I coughed blood more often than usual.

The court physicians knelt before me, terrified to speak.

I dismissed them all.

Life and death are matters of fate.

I have lived more than eighty years.

I have been king for more than half a century.

I have witnessed almost every storm a human being can endure.

And yet, as death approaches, there is one thing that weighs heavily on me.

I want to say a few words to those who will come after me.

If future generations write about Goujian, they will surely portray me as the man who endured humiliation, slept on firewood, tasted gall, and finally achieved revenge.

They will praise my perseverance.

They will celebrate my determination.

They will turn me into a moral monument and hang the story of my endurance in schools and temples for generations to admire.

But that will not be the real me.

The real me was once a young king, arrogant enough to suffer catastrophic defeat at Fujiao.

The real me was a slave who spent three years feeding horses in Wu and three years being spat upon.

The real me was a man so desperate to survive that I was willing to taste Fuchai's feces in order to prove that he was ill.

I still remember the stench.

Sometimes I wake from nightmares with that taste still in my mouth.

The real me was a conspirator who spent twenty years weaving a web of deception after returning home, swallowing every genuine thought before it could escape my lips.

And the real me was the solitary ruler who, after victory, had Wenzhong put to death and drove Fan Li away.

I was no saint.

I was a man nailed to the cross of hatred.

I wonder what my story will become after I am gone.

The scholars will surely transform me into an example of perseverance:

The determined man will ultimately be rewarded by Heaven.

They will put the words “sleeping on firewood and tasting gall” on the desks of children and tell them:

“Look at Goujian. He succeeded because he endured hardship.”

But will they tell those children what I actually did in Wu?

Will they tell them that I survived through lies, deception, bribery, manipulation, and conspiracy?

Will they tell them that after restoring Yue, I had Wenzhong executed and drove Fan Li away?

No.

They will not.

They will select what sounds noble.

They will preserve what is useful.

Because history is never simply a record of truth.

It is also a story each age tells itself for its own purposes.

I know that when Sima Qian eventually writes The Records of the Grand Historian, centuries from now, my story will already have begun to change.

He will write of the gallbladder hanging in my palace.

He will write of my hardship and determination.

But he will not write about how I knelt before Fuchai.

He will not describe the mud beneath his boots.

He will not describe the nights when I silently cried in the corner of a stable.

Because those things are not dignified. Those who write history prefer dignity.

But real history has never been dignified.

Crowns are purchased with humiliation.

Great ambitions are built upon deception.

Glory is watered with blood.

The day Fan Li left, he came to bid me farewell.

I did not ask him to stay.

Not because I wanted him gone.

I knew he was right.

He understood that a man like me could share hardship with his ministers, but perhaps not prosperity.

He saw through me.

Just as I had once seen through Fuchai.

After he left, I summoned Wenzhong.

I told him, in effect:

“You possess strategies and military wisdom. Nine plans were proposed for the destruction of Wu. Three have already destroyed this great enemy. Six remain with you. Why not use them to plan for Wu in the world below?”

The sword was delivered to him.

Wenzhong did not cry.

He did not beg.

He said only:

“I regret not following Fan Li.”

He regretted it.

I did not.

I could not afford to.

Because regret would mean admitting that I had been wrong.

And a king cannot admit weakness.

Weakness leads to death.

I climbed out of a graveyard of corpses.

I know better than anyone what this road costs.

Night has fallen.

Snow is falling heavily outside.

Winter in Langya is colder than Kuaiji.

The wind shakes the window lattice.

I lie upon my bed and close my eyes.

My life passes before me like a lantern show:

the broken soldiers on Mount Kuaiji;

the piles of manure in the Wu stables;

Fan Li's whispered reports in the middle of the night;

the bitterness exploding across my tongue;

the palace of Gusu burning;

Wenzhong's final sigh.

If Heaven allowed me to live again, would I choose the same road?

I have thought about it for a long time.

Yes.

I would.

Not because I had a choice.

But because I did not.

From the day a king puts on the crown, his fate no longer belongs entirely to him.

He belongs to his kingdom.

To his people.

To the spirits of the soldiers who died at Fujiao.

So I had to restore Yue.

I had to take revenge.

I had to do whatever was necessary.

As for what future generations choose to call me—

Let them.

By then I will already be nothing more than a handful of earth outside Langya.

I only hope that someday, when someone reads the four characters “sleeping on firewood and tasting gall,” they will pause for a moment and ask:

What did the man sleeping on that firewood dream about at night?

What else did he swallow besides the bitterness of gall?

Only I know the answer.

And soon,

I will carry it with me into the earth.

Author's Note

This piece is a literary reconstruction, not a historical transcription or clinical psychological diagnosis.

The first-person diary format deliberately places Goujian's legendary story inside an imagined psychological interior. Historical episodes are combined with fictionalized thoughts, sensory experiences, dialogue, and retrospective reflection.

The central question is therefore not simply:

How did Goujian defeat Wu?

It is:

What did victory cost the man who survived long enough to achieve it?

The traditional narrative celebrates endurance.

This reconstruction asks what endurance might look like from inside the mind of the person enduring it: humiliation becoming discipline, hatred becoming purpose, deception becoming survival, and finally victory becoming a new psychological void.

In that sense, Goujian is not merely the archetype of “sleeping on firewood and tasting gall.”

He is also the darker question hidden beneath that legend:

When revenge becomes the reason to live, what happens when revenge is finally complete?
