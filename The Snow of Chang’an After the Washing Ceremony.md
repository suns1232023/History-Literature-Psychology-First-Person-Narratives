# The Snow of Chang’an After the Washing Ceremony

### Chang’an, Private Residence — Night of the Ninth Day of the Eleventh Month, 755 (Tianbao 14)



The wind had been howling all day. It had not stopped even after nightfall.

The candle on my desk flickered restlessly, throwing the words I had written on silk into alternating patches of light and shadow:

**“An Lushan will rebel.”**

I have written those four words countless times.

In memorials to the Emperor. In secret letters to Chen Xilie. In every sleepless night when I lay staring into the darkness.

But tonight, the brush felt heavier than usual.

Because at noon, an urgent report arrived from Daxi Xun, the Governor of Henan:

An Lushan had requested permission to enter the capital in mid-November under the pretext of **“presenting three thousand horses.”**

Three thousand horses.

Two armored soldiers for every horse.

Three thousand horses meant six thousand elite cavalrymen.

Enter the capital?

I almost laughed.

**Was this an imperial visit—or an invasion?**

I put down my brush and walked to the window.

Chang’an lay beneath the night like an enormous cauldron turned upside down. On the distant walls of the wards, the torches of the night patrol appeared here and there, tiny points of light, like the last breaths of a dying man.

And suddenly I remembered something from three years ago.

The first time I warned the Emperor in the Secretariat about An Lushan's strange behavior, His Majesty had been playing with a parrot sent as tribute from Hebei.

He did not even look up.

> **“Guozhong, you worry too much. Lushan is utterly loyal to me. I know his heart.”**

**Utterly loyal.**

When those words came from the Emperor's mouth, half the blood in my body seemed to turn cold.

I am Yang Guozhong. I have not walked the corridors of power for only a day.

From a minor official in Jiannan to Right Chancellor, overseeing more than forty commissions and offices—I have spent twenty years watching men reveal what lies beneath their faces.

Zhang Jiuling's integrity.

Li Linfu's poisonous cunning.

Wei Jian's ambition.

Wang Hong's greed.

How many of them did not begin with the words **“utterly loyal”**?

And how many eventually ended with their heads separated from their bodies?

Yet the Emperor simply would not believe me.

In His Majesty's eyes, An Lushan was merely a **“simple, honest Hu boy”**—a man who spoke Turkic, danced the Hu xuan, and existed chiefly to amuse his sovereign.

But I know what An Lushan is.

I closed my eyes.

A scene from five years ago rose before me.

It was winter of Tianbao 9, at the Hot Spring Palace.

An Lushan had come to court, and the Emperor had graciously permitted him to bathe at Huaqing Palace.

As I passed the hot springs, I saw him from a distance.

He was standing alone beside the pool, his back toward me. His broad shoulders loomed through the rising steam like a wall.

There was not the slightest trace of his usual foolish smile.

Only a cold severity on his profile.

Even now, remembering it makes my spine go cold.

At that moment, I understood.

**All his flattery, all his clumsiness, all his supposed “honesty”—they were nothing but a skin.**

And whatever lay beneath that skin was more frightening than anything Li Linfu had ever plotted against me.

From that day onward, I began collecting evidence against An Lushan.

He privately forged weapons in Fanyang.

He maintained a personal force of eight thousand **Yeluohe**—elite Khitan and Tongan warriors personally selected by him, men reputed to fight ten opponents apiece.

He brought Han Chinese scholars such as Zhang Tongru and Yan Zhuang into his military headquarters to draft documents and devise strategies.

Under the guise of frontier trade, he accumulated warhorses.

Under the name of disaster relief, he secretly stockpiled grain.

I knew these things.

Zhang Lizhen, the Investigating Commissioner of Hebei, knew them.

Lü Zhihui, An Lushan's deputy in Pinglu, knew them.

But the Emperor did not.

Or perhaps—

**the Emperor did not want to know.**

> **“Guozhong, I know that you and Lushan do not get along.”**

Last month, at Qinzheng Tower, the Emperor summoned me to his side. There was almost a touch of weary kindness in his voice.

> **“One of you is my Chancellor; the other is my frontier commander. You are both pillars of the realm. Surely you should live in harmony.”**

I was kneeling on the floor, my forehead pressed against the cold golden bricks.

The words I wanted to say turned three times in my throat.

In the end, they became the sentence I had repeated countless times:

> **“Your Majesty, An Lushan harbors treasonous intentions. I beg Your Majesty to prepare against him without delay.”**

The Emperor's expression darkened for a moment.

Then he smiled again.

> **“Enough, enough. Let us not speak of this. Come—look at the new *Rainbow Feather Garment* I have acquired.”**

When I left Qinzheng Tower, Gao Lishi came after me.

That old eunuch had spent fifty years beside the Emperor. His judgment of men was sharper than anyone's.

He caught my sleeve and lowered his voice.

> **“My Lord Chancellor, allow this old servant one piece of advice—stop speaking of An Lushan.”**

I stared at his deeply wrinkled face.

“Elder Gao, do you also believe An Lushan has no intention of rebelling?”

Gao Lishi remained silent for a long time.

Then he sighed.

> **“This old servant merely thinks that some things, if said too often, will eventually make people believe that the Chancellor himself has ‘ulterior motives.’”**

**Ulterior motives.**

Me?

Yang Guozhong?

It is true that my reputation in Chang'an is not good.

Everyone says I climbed to the chancellorship through my connection to Yang Guifei. They say I suppress rivals, eliminate political enemies, and appoint my own people.

I admit all of it.

But there is one thing my critics can never take away from me:

**I understand better than anyone how rotten the foundations of this empire have become.**

The imperial treasury is empty.

The frontier commands have grown too powerful to control.

The armies of Hebei, Hedong, and Pinglu are effectively concentrated in the hands of one man:

**An Lushan.**

And what is the Emperor doing?

Teaching musicians to sing at the Pear Garden.

Bathing with the Noble Consort at Mount Li.

Watching An Lushan and Yang Guifei perform that absurd **“Washing Ceremony”** at Xingqing Palace—and laughing until he could barely breathe.

I remember that night too clearly.

An Lushan had come to court.

Yang Guifei, acting as his “godmother,” arranged a **washing ceremony** for him.

A Hu general nearly fifty years old, weighing something close to three hundred *jin*, was placed into a bathing basin like an infant.

The palace women laughed as they splashed water over him.

The Emperor stood nearby, applauding.

> **“Lushan is so simple and lovable! So simple and lovable!”**

I stood among the assembled officials.

Beside me, Censor-in-Chief Lu Yi whispered:

> **“When a ruler is no longer a ruler, and a subject is no longer a subject, the state is doomed.”**

I turned to look at him.

His face was white as paper.

The next day, Lu Yi submitted a memorial apologizing for his “improper words.”

He never mentioned the incident again.

And neither did anyone else.

The entire court fell silent.

Sometimes I wonder—

If I were not Yang Guozhong—

If I were not the man who rose through connections, the “treacherous chancellor” everyone loves to hate—

If I were as upright and incorruptible as Zhang Jiuling—

Would my warnings carry more weight?

But then I think:

What happened to Zhang Jiuling?

Did he not once urge the Emperor not to depose the Crown Prince?

Did he not warn against relying on Li Linfu?

And what became of him?

Exiled to Jingzhou.

Dying in frustration and obscurity.

**In this court, those who speak the truth are never rewarded with a good ending.**

But I am Yang Guozhong.

I am not Zhang Jiuling.

I am not warning the Emperor for the sake of some abstract ideal of “loyal ministerial virtue.”

Half of my warning comes from fear.

**I am afraid.**

Afraid that on the day An Lushan marches into Chang'an, the first head he will demand is mine.

Our feud is no longer a matter of yesterday or today.

In Tianbao 11, when I succeeded Li Linfu as Chancellor, the first thing I did was investigate An Lushan's network in the capital.

I had his ally Ji Wen demoted and sent away.

I pulled his informants out of the Censorate one by one.

He hates me to the bone.

And I fear him just as deeply.

There is no way out of this deadlock.

If he does not rebel, sooner or later I will gather enough evidence before the Emperor to bring him down.

If he does rebel—

**my head will be the first one he cuts off.**

Therefore I must make the Emperor believe one thing:

**An Lushan will rebel.**

But the Emperor does not believe me.

I have studied the Emperor's mind for three years, and even now I do not fully understand it.

Perhaps he really is old.

Sixty-nine years old.

The decisive edge of his youth has faded.

He does not want to believe An Lushan will rebel because believing it would mean admitting that the “golden age” of the past decade was partly an illusion.

It would mean acknowledging that the “loyal subject” he personally elevated had become a sword hanging above his own head.

It would mean admitting that his vaunted ability to judge men was a joke.

**A man who reaches sixty-nine may no longer possess the courage to destroy the world he spent his entire life building.**

Perhaps this is what Mencius meant by **self-deception**.

To deceive oneself into believing that everything is well.

To deceive oneself into believing that everyone nearby is sincere.

To deceive oneself into believing that the fat man who dances the Hu xuan will forever remain nothing more than an oversized baby.

But I cannot deceive myself.

Daxi Xun's urgent report tonight is ironclad evidence.

Enter the capital under the pretext of presenting horses?

This is An Lushan testing us.

Testing the Emperor's limits.

If His Majesty agrees, six thousand elite cavalrymen will enter Chang'an.

If His Majesty refuses, An Lushan will have his excuse:

**“The imperial court suspects me.”**

And then rebellion becomes, in his eyes, justified.

I drafted a secret memorial using the most earnest language I could find.

I urged the Emperor to refuse the horses on the grounds that:

**“The weather is too cold; horses should not be moved under such conditions.”**

And I urged him to order An Lushan to **“remain where he is.”**

I have already worked out the next step.

If I can delay him for just one month, I can mobilize troops from Hedong and Shuofang, reinforce the defenses, and choke off his route south.

But after sending the memorial into the palace, I suddenly became afraid.

Afraid that the Emperor would see my name and set it aside without reading.

Afraid that Gao Lishi, that old fox, would whisper:

> “The Chancellor and An Lushan have always been enemies. His words cannot be entirely impartial.”

Afraid that Yang Guifei—

**my own cousin**—

might speak on An Lushan's behalf.

Yes.

Yang Guifei.

My own cousin.

I cannot even be certain which side she would choose.

Whenever An Lushan comes to court, he brings her the rarest treasures:

Perfumed powder from Fanyang.

Khitan fox fur.

Pearls from Pinglu.

She likes him.

She treats him as an amusing toy who can make her laugh.

How could she possibly know that the fat man she calls **“Luer”** carries beneath his robes a blade capable of cutting through the entire Tang Empire?

The wind outside grew stronger.

The window lattice creaked.

I heard the night servants whispering beneath the corridor.

They said tomorrow might bring a change in the weather.

A change in the weather?

**The world has already changed.**

It is merely that the people of Chang'an do not know it yet.

I returned to my desk.

I picked up the half-finished memorial and considered adding a few more lines.

But as I raised the brush, I suddenly remembered something.

Three years ago, I had investigated An Lushan's household registration.

He did not even originally bear the surname An.

His original surname was Kang.

He was descended from Turkic people, and his mother had been a Turkic shaman.

The surname “An” had been given to him by Zhang Shougui, the military governor of Youzhou.

**A man whose very surname was given to him by someone else—would such a man truly care about “loyalty” and “filial devotion”?**

He has believed in only one thing throughout his life:

**The man with the biggest fist makes the rules.**

Tomorrow morning, at court, I will read Daxi Xun's urgent report aloud before every minister and every military commander.

I will force the Emperor to choose.

**Will he believe me—or will he believe An Lushan?**

**This time, I cannot retreat.**

But what if His Majesty still refuses to believe me?

Suddenly, I felt cold all over.

My face stared back at me from the bronze mirror.

My temples had already turned gray.

The wrinkles around my eyes were deep as knife cuts.

I am fifty-seven years old.

From Jiannan to Chang'an.

From a minor clerk to Chancellor.

Thirty years on this road.

And tonight I finally understand:

There is no safe destination at the end of it.

**There is an abyss ahead and a cliff behind.**

**I stand between them, holding nothing but a memorial that no one is willing to believe.**

I folded the memorial and tucked it inside my sleeve.

From somewhere beyond the walls came the sound of the fourth watch drum.

Dull.

Distant.

Almost as if it were beating from beneath the earth.

Two hours until court.

I extinguished the lamp and sat in the darkness.

Cold winter air slipped through the cracks in the window and touched my face.

It felt like the arrows of An Lushan's Turkic archers, drawn to full tension—

cold and whispering against the skin.

I suddenly thought:

What if tomorrow the Emperor accepts my memorial?

What if he is still willing to listen to me one last time?

Perhaps there is still time.

Perhaps the defenses of Taiyuan can still be strengthened.

Perhaps troops can still be transferred to Tong Pass.

Perhaps the loyal men of Hebei, crushed beneath An Lushan's rule, can still raise their banners.

But a voice inside me whispered:

**Too late.**

**It has been too late for a long time.**

From the moment the Emperor said,

> **“Lushan is utterly loyal to me,”**

the empire's **golden vessel**—the *jin'ou*, the symbol of an intact and secure realm—had already cracked.

Everything I am doing now is merely shouting into the crack.

So that when future historians write my name—

**Yang Guozhong**—

perhaps they will add a single sentence after it:

> **“He repeatedly warned that An Lushan would rebel.”**

Ah.

**“He repeatedly warned that An Lushan would rebel.”**

Sixteen characters in Chinese.

Perhaps those words will be the only measure of my entire life—

my only merit,

my only guilt,

my only defense before history.

Outside, the sky was beginning to pale.

I stood up and straightened my robes.

I tucked the memorial against my chest, as close to my heart as I could place it.

**Time to go to court.**

Chang'an was still asleep.

Only the lonely clappers of the night watch broke the silence.

I climbed into my sedan chair.

As the curtain fell, I caught sight of the Morning Star in the eastern sky, still hanging there before its descent.

Pale.

Cold.

Like an unblinking eye.

I remembered something Li Linfu had said to me ten years ago, just before his death.

He had gripped my hand then.

His fingernails dug into my flesh.

There was one last spark of light in his clouded eyes.

> **“Guozhong, remember this. In your position, the only person you can trust is yourself. You cannot trust the sovereign. You cannot trust your fellow ministers. You cannot even trust your relatives. Only the sword and grain can tell you the truth.”**

I did not understand him then.

**Now I do.**

But it is already too late.

The sedan chair creaked forward toward the Imperial City.

I closed my eyes.

There was only one thought in my mind:

**An Lushan, you had better not rebel.**

**If you do, I will spend my life, my power, and my very blood to keep you from crossing the Yellow River.**

But then I heard another voice inside me—

small,

cold,

merciless:

**If you rebel, perhaps the first thing that will fail to cross Tong Pass will not be your army.**

**It will be the Emperor's heart—still dreaming of a golden age that no longer exists.**

The sedan chair stopped.

The gates of the Imperial City stood before me.

I took a deep breath and stepped down.

The morning dew soaked through my boots.

The cold went straight to the bone.

# Chang'an, Before Hanyuan Hall

### Morning of the Tenth Day of the Eleventh Month, 755 (Tianbao 14)

Fine snow had begun to fall.

I stood on the stone steps outside the hall, waiting for the court drums to sound.

The memorial in my hand was already damp with sweat.
