# **Mi Fang’s Diary: Identity Trapped in the Mists of Jingzhou and the Psychology of Betrayal**

### *A Psychohistorical Reconstruction of Identity Conflict, Authority Trauma, and the Collapse of Group Belonging*

**Time span:** 200–221 CE
**Core perspectives:** The psychological mechanisms of identity disturbance, the intergenerational transmission of authority-related trauma, and the cognitive collapse of group belonging

> **Editorial note:** The “Diary of Mi Fang” presented here is a literary and psychological reconstruction inspired by historical records. It is not a surviving primary-source diary attributed to Mi Fang.

---

## **Preface: The Prison of the Brother-in-Law — Identity as a Physiological Imprint**

**Jian’an 5 (200 CE), Xuzhou.**

When the dust of Xuzhou mixed with the smell of blood and entered my nostrils, I was hiding with A-Dou in a dry well.

My sister’s skirt flickered above the mouth of the well. Her fingers, gentle yet merciless, felt like silver needles dipped in poison.

“Ah-Fang, remember this,” she said. “You are a son of the Mi family. More importantly, you are Liu Bei’s brother-in-law. Never let them look down on you.”

Only then did I realize that **being ‘Liu Bei’s brother-in-law’ was more than a social label. It had become a physiological imprint.**

That identity gave me an intimacy with the Shu-Han political circle that others could never possess. Yet it also trapped me permanently between two worlds: imperial relative and local administrator; family member and subordinate; insider and outsider.

And then there was Guan Yu.

This diary is written for the man I might have become thirty years from now—and for anyone trapped inside an identity maze.

When we say, **“I had no choice but to betray,”** were we truly forced by circumstances? Or had weakness already been written into the neural script of the self?

---

# **Chapter I. The Boy of Pengcheng: Awakening to Identity**

### *200–208 CE — The Formation of an Attachment-Based Personality*

## **Diary Entry I: October, Jian’an 5 — Night of Flight**

My sister is dead.

When Cao Chun’s Tiger and Leopard Cavalry caught up with us, she pushed me onto a horse. A storm of arrows struck her back.

Before she died, she seized my wrist so tightly that her fingernails cut into my flesh.

“Ah-Fang,” she whispered, “protect the young lord. The reputation of the Mi family must not be destroyed by you.”

Her blood soaked through my sleeve. It was warm, almost like the summer sun over Xuzhou.

I remembered my father, Mi Zhu, selling the family’s enormous fortune a decade earlier to support Liu Bei.

“Lord Xuande is destined to restore the Han,” he had said. “We follow him not merely for wealth, but for righteousness.”

At the time, I believed righteousness was a banner beneath which one marched to restore the Han.

Only now did I understand:

**The weight of righteousness falls first upon those who have the least room to escape it.**

---

## **Psychological Mirror: The Neural Encoding of Traumatic Attachment**

From the perspective of attachment theory, the death of a primary attachment figure can profoundly alter an individual's expectations of abandonment and security. Bowlby’s framework suggests that early attachment experiences contribute to internal working models through which later relationships are interpreted.

In this reconstruction, my sister's death becomes the foundational trauma.

Afterward, every expression of displeasure from Liu Bei can be interpreted by my threatened mind as a possible signal of abandonment.

I obey not simply because he is my lord.

I obey because **obedience has become psychologically associated with security.**

At the same time, I idealize Liu Bei.

His reputation for benevolence becomes an archetypal image of the righteous leader. Evidence that contradicts that image is discounted or rationalized.

This is the familiar mechanism of **confirmation bias**:

> Once loyalty becomes part of identity, evidence against the object of loyalty becomes evidence against the self.

---

## **The Jingzhou Arrangement Before Chibi**

In Jian’an 13 (208 CE), Liu Bei appointed me Administrator of Nan Commandery, with Jiangling as my base. Guan Yu was stationed to the north.

On the night before my departure, Liu Bei placed his hand on my shoulder.

“Ah-Fang, Jingzhou is our foothold. Guard the gate for me.”

His hand was rough and powerful.

Yet I thought I saw something in his eyes—hesitation.

Perhaps it was the instinctive caution of a ruler toward a relative entrusted with military power.

Perhaps it was only my imagination.

But from that night onward, I began to understand something I had never wanted to admit:

**Being close to power does not mean possessing power.**

---

# **Chapter II. Gunpowder in the Air of Jingzhou**

### *209–219 CE — The Accumulation of Authority Trauma*

## **Diary Entry II: Jian’an 14 — Jiangling Administrative Office**

Guan Yu has arrived.

He rode a red horse. The crescent blade of his weapon rested beside the saddle, while his robe brushed across my desk.

My account books were spread before me.

He looked down.

“Mi Fang. Why are there three hundred fewer crossbows in Jiangling?”

I bowed.

“The troops at Ruxu urgently required supplies. General Liu Qi temporarily requisitioned—”

“Excuses.”

His scabbard struck the floor.

Ink splashed across my accounts.

He seized me by the collar.

“You are Liu Bei’s brother-in-law. But are you his relative—or my subordinate? Delay supplies again, and I will have your head before the army.”

His guards suppressed their laughter as they withdrew.

I stared at the mark left by his scabbard on the floor.

Suddenly I remembered Xuzhou.

He had spoken to me in much the same way then.

He had not yet become the celebrated Han Shou Tinghou, yet he already carried himself as though the world belonged to him.

I finally understood:

**Arrogance grows like a vine in Jingzhou. Once it wraps around your throat, it becomes difficult to breathe.**

---

# **Three Layers of Psychological Conflict**

## **1. A Hierarchy Built on Fictive Kinship**

Guan Yu was Liu Bei’s sworn brother.

I was his brother-in-law.

In the political culture of the late Han, fictive kinship could function as a powerful form of political legitimacy.

But within Liu Bei’s inner circle, these relationships did not carry equal weight.

Guan Yu's position was reinforced by decades of military loyalty and symbolic brotherhood.

My position depended heavily on marriage.

That distinction mattered.

It created an unstable identity:

**Was I a member of the family, or merely a political appointment protected by the family?**

---

## **2. Reciprocal Attribution Bias**

Guan Yu interpreted failures in military supply as evidence of my incompetence.

I interpreted them as evidence of structural constraints.

He saw:

> “You failed because you are incapable.”

I saw:

> “I failed because the circumstances were impossible.”

Both interpretations contained part of the truth.

Neither contained the whole truth.

This is how interpersonal conflict becomes self-reinforcing.

One party makes a dispositional attribution.

The other responds defensively with a situational attribution.

Each then interprets the other's defense as further evidence of the original accusation.

---

## **3. The Physiological Cost of Threatened Self-Esteem**

Every public humiliation activated the threat system.

I learned to suppress anger rather than express it.

Outwardly, I became increasingly obedient.

Internally, resentment accumulated.

The result was not immediate rebellion.

It was something more dangerous:

**passive resistance.**

Small alterations appeared in the accounts.

Minor delays became deliberate.

I began telling myself that I was merely protecting myself.

But resentment has a strange property:

> **What begins as self-protection can gradually become preparation for betrayal.**

---

## **The Altered Supply Ledger**

Among my private papers was a hidden ledger.

One entry read:

> “Third month of Jian’an 16: Guan Yu requested fifty warhorses. Thirty were actually delivered. Twenty-five were recorded as lost in an accident. Five were genuinely lost; the remainder were transferred elsewhere.”

When Lü Meng eventually found the altered accounts, he circled the discrepancies in red.

“You say Guan Yu wronged you,” he said.

“Then why did you begin falsifying the records long before today?”

I had no answer.

Because the answer was uncomfortable.

**I had already begun withdrawing from the relationship long before I physically opened the gates.**

---

# **Chapter III. The Blade at My Throat**

### *219 CE — The Neural Shock of a Death Threat*

## **Diary Entry III: Jian’an 24 — The Walls of Gong’an**

Guan Yu's messenger arrived.

The letter carried his seal.

Its message was simple:

> *Delay supplies again, and you will answer with your head.*

Then came another accusation.

He reminded me of Changban.

He claimed that without his intervention, my sister would never have survived.

But the story was false.

It was Zhao Yun who rescued A-Dou.

Why would Guan Yu invent such a debt?

To intimidate me?

To remind me that my life depended on him?

Or perhaps because power itself encourages people to rewrite history around themselves.

I could not know.

But my body reacted before my mind did.

My hands trembled.

I could not sleep.

Every sound of horse bells startled me.

Even the sight of a red horse made me nauseated.

I had begun to understand something terrifying:

**My body had already chosen submission before my conscious mind had made a decision.**

---

## **“I Will Deal With Them Later”**

The *Records of the Three Kingdoms* records Guan Yu as saying, regarding Mi Fang and Fu Shiren:

> *“I will deal with them later.”*

For a subordinate living under military discipline, those words could reasonably be interpreted as a threat of punishment.

Fear does not always produce rebellion.

Sometimes it produces paralysis.

When escape appears impossible and resistance appears fatal, the mind may gradually learn helplessness.

The question is no longer:

> *How do I win?*

It becomes:

> *How do I survive?*

That distinction would prove decisive.

---

# **Chapter IV. The Attack on the Mind**

### *219 CE — Lü Meng and the Manufacture of Cognitive Dissonance*

## **Diary Entry IV: The Docks at Gong’an**

Lü Meng came.

He wore plain clothes and arrived by a small boat.

No army stood behind him.

When he stepped ashore, he pointed toward the watchtowers across the river.

“Administrator Mi, Guan Yu knows Jiangling is vulnerable. He will soon return.”

I laughed.

“Are you here to persuade me to surrender?”

“No.”

He poured tea.

“I am here to tell you something you already know. Open the gates, and you live. Resist, and you die.”

Then he said the words that broke something inside me:

“Will Liu Bei risk his army for you?”

I said nothing.

Lü Meng continued.

“You are his brother-in-law. Has that identity ever protected you?”

The question entered my mind like a key entering a lock.

For years I had believed:

**Liu Bei's family relationship would protect me.**

But perhaps I had confused emotional proximity with political protection.

---

## **Lü Meng’s Three-Step Psychological Strategy**

### **1. Manufacturing Cognitive Dissonance**

He placed three contradictions before me.

**Survival instinct**
versus
**moral loyalty**

> “Guan Yu will kill you.”

**Familial expectation**
versus
**observable reality**

> “Will Liu Bei come to save you?”

**Group belonging**
versus
**personal survival**

> “You are a Shu-Han official—but what does that identity now require of you?”

Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance predicts that people tend to reduce psychological conflict by modifying beliefs, attitudes, or behavior.

I chose behavior.

I surrendered.

Not because I had suddenly stopped believing in Liu Bei.

But because **I could no longer tolerate the psychological contradiction between loyalty and survival.**

---

## **2. Reversing Social Identity**

Lü Meng presented the soldiers of Jiangdong not as conquerors, but as ordinary men who wanted to survive.

He implied:

> “They are like you.”

This was a subtle psychological maneuver.

Instead of asking me to abandon one group and join another, he attempted to redefine the relevant group altogether.

I was no longer:

> Shu official versus Wu invader.

I became:

> frightened subordinate versus powerful commander.

The political identity was displaced by a psychological identity.

---

## **3. Rationalization**

That night I wrote:

> “Guan Yu drove me toward this. Liu Bei abandoned me. Surrender is merely damage control.”

It sounded reasonable.

Perhaps that was why it worked.

Rationalization does not require a lie.

It only requires **selecting the explanation that makes an uncomfortable decision emotionally tolerable.**

I could tell myself that I had surrendered to protect civilians.

But another possibility remained:

**I had surrendered because I was afraid.**

And I did not want to look directly at that fact.

---

# **Chapter V. The Morning the Gates Opened**

### *219 CE — The Completion of Moral Disengagement*

## **Diary Entry V: Jiangling**

The gates opened.

Lü Meng's troops entered.

They did not massacre the population.

They distributed grain.

I stood upon the walls and watched Guan Yu's banners disappear into the distance.

I remembered Xuzhou twenty years earlier.

The sunlight had been painfully bright then.

I had believed that following Liu Bei meant that eventually the sky would clear.

Fu Shiren seized my sleeve.

“General… we really surrendered?”

I looked at the city below.

Then I said:

> **“It is the Administrator of Nan Commandery who has surrendered.”**

Not Mi Fang.

Not Liu Bei's brother-in-law.

Not a traitor.

The Administrator.

A title.

A role.

A bureaucratic identity.

---

## **The Psychology of Moral Disengagement**

By separating my official role from my personal identity, I created psychological distance between myself and the act.

I was no longer saying:

> “I betrayed Liu Bei.”

I was saying:

> “The administrator made a decision under extraordinary circumstances.”

That linguistic shift mattered.

Moral disengagement often begins not with an act, but with a change in how the act is described.

---

## **The Pressure of the Group**

Fu Shiren had already surrendered Gong'an.

His argument was simple:

> “The situation is hopeless. Resistance will only kill civilians.”

Lü Meng's restraint reinforced the impression that surrender was reasonable.

Once others crossed the line, my own crossing became easier.

The psychological mechanism was familiar:

**If everyone else is doing it, perhaps the decision cannot be morally wrong.**

---

## **The Key to Jiangling**

Lü Meng handed me a key engraved with the name of the city.

“From now on, this is yours.”

The metal was warm in my palm.

Yet it burned.

Every time I touched it, I remembered my sister's final words:

> “Never let them look down on you.”

I had spent my entire life trying not to be looked down upon.

And now I had become exactly the man I feared becoming.

---

# **Chapter VI. The Fire of Yiling**

### *221 CE — The Neuroscience of Survivor’s Guilt*

## **Diary Entry VI: Jianye Prison**

Liu Bei came.

He wore mourning clothes.

For a moment I thought he had come to condemn me.

Instead, he remained silent.

Then he looked at me.

“Ah-Fang,” he said, “your sister once told you not to let people look down on you.”

He paused.

“You succeeded.

Now everyone looks down on you.”

I lowered my head.

His hair had turned gray.

And suddenly I understood:

**We had all grown old.**

We had forgotten the vows.

We had forgotten the reasons.

And somewhere along the way, loyalty had become indistinguishable from pride.

---

## **The Three Burdens of Survivor’s Guilt**

### **1. Intrusive Memory**

I dreamed repeatedly of Guan Yu's final moments.

In the dreams he would turn toward me and ask:

> “Ah-Fang, why did you betray me?”

I always woke before answering.

---

### **2. Self-Disgust**

Liu Bei did not kill me.

That was almost worse.

His silence felt heavier than punishment.

Forgiveness requires recognition.

His indifference offered neither.

---

### **3. Moral Rumination**

After Jiangling fell, the political and military catastrophe continued.

I kept asking:

> *If I had resisted, would the outcome have been different?*

There was no experiment that could answer the question.

That is the cruelest feature of moral counterfactuals:

**They can never be falsified.**

The mind can therefore punish itself indefinitely.

---

## **The Failed Attempt at Redemption**

I asked Liu Bei to let me fight.

“I will lead troops to avenge General Guan.”

He looked at me.

“You could not hold Jiangling.”

Then he walked away.

I later asked for another opportunity.

None came.

I finally understood that redemption is not merely an internal decision.

It requires **a social environment willing to recognize the possibility of transformation.**

By then, my identity had already been frozen by history.

---

# **Epilogue: The Brother-in-Law’s Epitaph**

In this reconstruction, I imagine my final years far from the center of power.

The key to Jiangling remained with me.

Before death, I asked my son to throw it into the sea.

That night, I dreamed of my sister.

“Ah-Fang,” she asked, “have you finally found the answer?”

I looked toward the stars.

“Yes.”

“Identity is a label other people give us.

Loyalty can become a story we tell ourselves.

And I lost to Guan Yu not because I lacked ability, but because I was desperately trying to prove that I belonged.”

I paused.

Then I said:

> **When self-worth depends entirely upon another person's recognition, the loss has already begun.**

---

# **Psychological Case Closure**

The tragedy of Mi Fang can be interpreted, within this fictionalized psychohistorical framework, through several interacting mechanisms.

### **1. Identity Diffusion**

Mi Fang oscillates among three identities:

* **Mi Fang, the individual**
* **Liu Bei's brother-in-law**
* **Administrator of Nan Commandery**

None becomes a stable core self.

The result is identity diffusion: when external roles collapse, the individual no longer knows which self is supposed to remain.

---

### **2. Chronic Threat and Cognitive Rigidity**

Repeated humiliation and perceived threats may narrow attention toward immediate survival.

The cognitive system becomes increasingly focused on:

> *Who threatens me?*

rather than:

> *What strategy serves the larger system?*

This produces a dangerous transition:

**long-term political reasoning → short-term threat management.**

---

### **3. Moral Disengagement**

Once surrender becomes psychologically necessary, the mind begins constructing justifications:

> “I did it for the people.”

> “Guan Yu forced me.”

> “Liu Bei would never save me.”

> “Everyone else had already surrendered.”

Each statement may contain some truth.

Together, however, they create a narrative in which personal responsibility becomes increasingly difficult to see.

---

### **4. The Final Paradox of Betrayal**

Mi Fang's betrayal was not necessarily born at the moment the gates opened.

The psychological process may have begun much earlier:

**humiliation → resentment → withdrawal → rationalization → identity fracture → threat perception → surrender → post hoc justification.**

The gate was merely the final physical manifestation of a psychological door that had already been opened.

---

# **Modern Implications: The “Mi Fang Problem”**

The story is not simply about an ancient Chinese administrator.

It describes a recurring organizational phenomenon.

People rarely betray institutions in a single moment.

More often, they gradually disengage.

They begin by feeling undervalued.

Then they perceive humiliation.

Then they stop identifying with the organization.

Then they construct an alternative identity.

Finally, when an external opportunity appears, departure is psychologically reframed as **inevitable**.

The important lesson is therefore not:

> “Never betray.”

It is more fundamental:

> **When identity, dignity, belonging, and authority become chronically misaligned, loyalty becomes psychologically unstable.**

Organizations that rely exclusively on hierarchy and punishment may therefore create the very disloyalty they later condemn.

---

# **Final Reflection: The Key, the Gate, and the Self**

Mi Fang's story can be reduced to three objects:

**the ledger,
the key,
and the gate.**

The ledger represents resentment.

The key represents rationalization.

The gate represents the final decision.

But none of them explains the entire tragedy.

The deeper mechanism is identity.

Mi Fang spent much of his life trying to answer one question:

> **“Who am I in relation to powerful people?”**

He never fully learned to ask the more important question:

> **“Who am I when nobody is validating me?”**

That is the psychological core of his tragedy.

Because once identity is entirely borrowed from others, loyalty becomes conditional upon recognition.

And when recognition disappears, the borrowed identity collapses.

The gate then opens.

Not necessarily because the enemy has become stronger.

But because **the defender has stopped believing that the city still belongs to him.**

## **Author’s Note**

This essay is best understood as **psychohistorical fiction informed by historical sources**, rather than as a clinical diagnosis of Mi Fang.

Historical evidence cannot establish that Mi Fang possessed a particular personality disorder, neural abnormality, attachment pattern, or subjective emotional state. Nor can modern neuroimaging findings be retrospectively assigned to a historical individual.

The psychological mechanisms discussed here are therefore **interpretive models**, not historical facts.

Their purpose is not to diagnose Mi Fang.

It is to use his historical trajectory as a controlled narrative experiment:

> **What happens when identity depends on status, loyalty depends on recognition, authority depends on fear, and survival eventually conflicts with belonging?**

The answer may be as relevant to modern organizations as it was to the political world of Jingzhou.

**The city may change.

The uniforms may change.

The keys may change.

But the psychology of the gate remains remarkably familiar.**
