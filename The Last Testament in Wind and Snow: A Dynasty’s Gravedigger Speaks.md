# The Last Testament in Wind and Snow: A Dynasty’s Gravedigger Speaks

**Guangxu Year 16, Tenth Month, Twenty-Ninth Day — Snow Buries the City**

*By Pan Zuyin*

*Keywords: Cultural Media*

### Prologue: Twilight Coughed from the Lungs

I coughed through the night.

It was not coughing, really. It was as if I were dragging the last trace of warmth from my lungs and forcing it into the cold air.

When Feng Shi took my pulse this afternoon, his fingers remained on my wrist for a long time—so long that it seemed he was counting the water clocks of my entire life. He said nothing. He prescribed a restorative formula.

I recognized the characters on the prescription.

It was medicine meant to comfort a dying man.

I know my time has come.

The snow outside is fierce, as though it means to bury Beijing whole. I ordered my attendants to bring a rubbing of the *Pan of Ji Zibai* to my bedside. My hands trembled so badly that I could barely hold it. The bronze inscriptions blurred before my eyes.

Those characters, carved three thousand years ago, can grow old too.

In the second year of Xianfeng, the year I placed third in the metropolitan examination, it also snowed.

The white marble steps before the Hall of Supreme Harmony were covered in snow, pure and immaculate. I believed that if I stepped onto those steps, I would be walking into the very heart of a prosperous age.

Now I know better.

**Beneath the snow, there were cracks everywhere.**

---

## I. The Instrument of Punishment at Dawn

I have feared the hour before dawn all my life.

Not because of the darkness.

Because of the sound of the bolt sliding shut on Donghua Gate.

That sound was like the prelude to the fall of a executioner’s blade, striking me once every day for sixty years.

What would psychology call it?

**Chronic trauma.**

The daily repetition of small, inescapable pressures from which there is no escape.

I would rise around midnight, climb into a carriage, and endure an hour of jolting through snow and mud. By the time I reached Donghua Gate, my bones felt scattered across the road while my soul was still lying in bed.

Last year, my carriage overturned. I crawled into the Grand Council covered in mud.

My colleagues laughed.

I laughed too.

Laughter was the rouge of officialdom. You painted it on so that no one could see the color of a corpse beneath.

But the most exhausting thing was not the journey.

It was the feeling of **being suspended in midair**.

I had risen to the highest ranks of government, yet I knew that in the eyes of those princes of the imperial clan, I was still merely a Han Chinese “slave official” who happened to be useful.

Favor was a whip.

It drove you toward endless diligence until you were worked to death.

In the eighth year of Guangxu, I entered the Grand Council. During the Empress Dowager’s birthday celebrations, seventy plaques were sent to me in a single day for calligraphy.

My wrist was nearly dislocated from writing.

Before the ink had dried, another batch arrived.

This was not governing a country.

**It was consuming lives.**

The energy of an entire generation was being burned away on endless calligraphy, ceremonial documents, bureaucratic formulas, kneeling, greetings, and meaningless formalities.

I once wrote to my old friend Zuqian:

> “My brother, life has lately become so unbearably busy…”

Then my brush stopped.

What use was complaining?

The imperial court itself was a giant mill.

We were its donkeys, blindfolded, convinced that we were moving forward.

In truth, we were merely grinding the years of our lives into dust.

That is why I became obsessed with bronze inscriptions and ancient artifacts.

Those bronzes never spoke.

They never called me “Lord Pan.”

They never handed me another memorial that had to be answered.

They simply sat there, silently turning green with patina, aging with complete and righteous indifference.

When I touched that green patina, I could briefly forget who I was.

I could forget that the blade of dawn was still hanging above my head.

**Pangxi Studio was not a study. It was an ICU.**

Inside it, I performed emergency resuscitation on myself.

---

## II. The Great Gamble

If there was one thing I did right in this life, it was what I did in the tenth year of Xianfeng.

I placed my entire fortune on the survival of **Zuo Zongtang**.

At the time, he was merely a controversial adviser in the Hunan governor’s office, his life hanging by the thread of two words:

**“Overbearing” and “insubordinate.”**

I barely knew him personally.

But I had read his policy essays.

There was something like the flash of a blade between his lines.

And that was precisely what a collapsing empire needed most.

I submitted three memorials in succession.

In one of them, I wrote the words that later became famous throughout the realm:

> **“The state cannot be without Hunan for a single day; Hunan cannot be without Zongtang for a single day.”**

Looking back now, that was not rational calculation.

It was **intuition**.

When a dynasty is collapsing, a true gambler does not look at the cards.

He looks at the momentum of the game.

I sensed in Zuo Jiguo that peculiar force of someone capable of “holding up a falling sky.”

So I placed everything on him:

My career.

My reputation.

My fortune.

My life.

From the perspective of psychology, perhaps it was **extreme compensation under cognitive dissonance**.

I watched half of the country fall into chaos while the court continued arguing about Manchu-Han distinctions and ritual propriety.

The internal structure of my world was breaking apart.

Saving Zuo Zongtang was my attempt to repair that rupture.

I used one radical intervention to prove that the system might still possess the capacity for self-rescue.

I won the gamble.

Zuo Zongtang later reconquered Xinjiang and entered the history books as a hero.

But the moment I won, I also lost.

Because I finally understood something.

The so-called **Tongzhi Restoration** was partly an illusion of power transfer.

The Eight Banners and Green Standard Army had become rotten to the core. The court had no choice but to allow men such as Zeng Guofan, Zuo Zongtang, and Li Hongzhang to recruit local forces and raise their own funds.

On the surface, this was central authorization.

In reality, it was the central government losing control.

From then on, the lifeblood of the Qing dynasty no longer flowed entirely from imperial vermilion decrees in the Forbidden City.

It flowed through the private offices of provincial governors.

Men like us in Beijing became accountants.

Every day we checked the figures submitted by the provinces without knowing how many private armies, diverted taxes, and semi-independent commanders were hiding behind those numbers.

They obeyed orders when convenient.

They ignored proclamations when necessary.

**Restoration?**

No.

**It was slow death.**

The authority of the central government was like a leaking skin bag, slowly collapsing.

I promoted Zhang Zhidong.

I supported Wu Dacheng.

I did everything I could.

But I knew that I was merely rescuing a few names.

I could not save the examination system, already rusting from within.

I could not save an empire held together by provincial military power.

Zuo Zongtang's victory was the Qing dynasty’s final flash of returning light.

The light was so bright that we forgot one simple fact:

**The sun had already begun to set.**

---

## III. Diseased Eyes, Blind Heart

My eyes were ruined because I spent too many years looking at things that should never have been seen.

The doctors called it an eye disorder.

They told me to stop reading and writing.

How absurd.

Had I not survived my entire life by looking and writing?

I read memorials.

I read people's faces.

I read the direction of political winds.

I wrote reports.

I wrote congratulatory inscriptions.

I wrote epitaphs.

But when I looked at ancient bronzes and stone rubbings, my eyes did not hurt.

The characters carved onto oracle bones, bells, bronze vessels, steles, and bricks were straight and honest.

They did not lie.

They told me that three thousand years ago, a ritual was a ritual.

A campaign was a campaign.

Merit and failure were carved into bronze.

And once carved, they did not change with every political season.

Our own age was different.

In the ninth year of Guangxu, while observing mourning for my deceased parent in Suzhou, I met Wu Changshuo at my old residence on Nanshizi Street.

He was poor then.

But he possessed backbone.

He carved seals for me, and every stroke of the knife seemed to draw blood.

One seal read:

**“Better to close the gate.”**

I laughed bitterly.

Could I close the door of my study?

Could I close the door against the collapse of the entire country?

Bronze and stone were my **opium**.

In reality, I administered the Ministry of Revenue and faced a treasury that constantly spent tomorrow's money today.

I administered the Ministry of Justice and helped overturn the notorious Yang Naiwu and Xiaobaicai case.

Yet I could not overturn the upside-down world itself.

Only before those mottled rubbings could I pretend that there was still such a thing as **eternity**.

But opium cannot save a life.

During the so-called Tongzhi Restoration, customs revenues increased.

The *likin* taxes came in.

The Jiangnan region appeared to be recovering.

It was a **collective illusion**.

We diverted naval funds to renovate the Summer Palace.

We dressed several ironclads of the Beiyang Fleet in the costume of “self-strengthening.”

Li Hongzhang established the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company and the Kaiping Mining Company.

We applauded.

We declared that the Self-Strengthening Movement was succeeding.

But in our hearts, we knew the truth.

It was **papering over decay**.

The old beams were rotten.

Instead of replacing the beams, we desperately pasted new wallpaper over the walls.

The more magnificent the wallpaper became,

**the more fragile the structure underneath.**

The storm of the First Sino-Japanese War had not yet arrived.

But I could already smell the salt on the wind.

Japan was building warships at astonishing speed.

And what were we doing?

Calculating the expenses of the Empress Dowager's birthday celebrations.

Arguing over whether the Tongwen College should teach astronomy and mathematics.

Impeaching one official for being “too radical” and another for being “too sympathetic to foreigners.”

My eyes failed because I had spent too many years looking at things that everyone should have seen—

**yet pretended not to see.**

The rust on those ancient bronze rubbings had already spread across the bones of the dynasty itself.

---

# IV. The Rearview Mirror: Five Cracks

When a man is about to die, he sometimes sees things with unusual clarity.

It is like standing on a mountain peak and suddenly seeing the entire road behind him.

With my dying eyes, I look back now.

The fall of the Qing dynasty was not caused by a single flaw.

It was the result of **five cracks acting together**.

I lived within this system for forty years.

I touched every one of those fractures with my own hands.

### Crack One: The Cancer of Power

### From “Manchu and Han as One” to “Manchus Above All”

At the founding of the dynasty, “Manchu and Han as one” was both a political slogan and a form of political wisdom.

By the Tongzhi and Guangxu eras, however, it had become little more than a hypocritical curtain hiding reality.

Central offices remained dominated by Manchu officials.

Real power in the provinces increasingly fell into Han Chinese hands, yet those same men were viewed with suspicion.

It was a form of **political schizophrenia**.

The brain—the Manchu center—did not trust the limbs—the Han provinces.

The limbs, meanwhile, increasingly acted on their own because the brain was incapable of governing them effectively.

The Taiping Rebellion shattered the fragile equilibrium.

The court had no choice but to rely on the Hunan and Huai armies.

It was a desperate remedy that carried its own poison.

From that point onward, the struggle between center and province became the **central theme of Qing politics**.

Inside the Grand Council, I spent every day navigating this distrust:

Allow Li Hongzhang and men like him enough freedom to act,

while simultaneously preventing them from becoming too powerful.

The empire spent enormous amounts of energy on this balancing act.

---

### Crack Two: The Fiscal Lie

### From “Never Increase the Land Tax” to “Tax Everything That Moves”

Under the Qianlong Emperor, the treasury reportedly held eighty million taels of silver.

By the Daoguang era, it had fallen below ten million.

Where had the money gone?

The White Lotus Rebellion.

Opium.

Indemnities.

But the most dangerous problem was **systemic corruption**.

When I served as Minister of Revenue, I knew exactly what the tax system looked like.

Like a sieve.

Money was extracted at every level, and by the time it reached the central treasury, barely a fraction remained.

But we dared not investigate too deeply.

Because if we did, the entire bureaucratic system might collapse.

And so the vicious cycle continued:

The poorer the state became, the more taxes it demanded.

The more taxes it demanded, the more people rebelled.

The more rebellions it suppressed, the more money it needed.

**Fiscal collapse was the financial warning signal of dynastic collapse.**

We pretended not to understand the numbers.

Then we returned to the lakes and gardens of the Summer Palace and dreamed of being the civilized center of the world.

---

### Crack Three: The Prison of Perception

### From “The Middle Kingdom” to “The World”

During the Opium Wars, we still said that the British barbarians were physically handicapped because of the shape of their legs.

What a tragic form of arrogance.

The burning of the Old Summer Palace should have awakened everyone.

Instead, it awakened only a few.

Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang pursued the Self-Strengthening Movement.

That was probably as far as the old system could go without transforming itself.

But the principle of “Chinese learning as substance, Western learning for practical use” contained a contradiction.

**Substance and application cannot truly be separated.**

Trying to place modern technology inside an unchanged institutional structure meant walking with one leg shorter than the other.

Grand Secretary Woren declared that the foundation of a nation lay in the hearts of its people, not in technology.

It sounded beautiful.

But bullets do not listen to beautiful words.

The greatest tragedy was **cognitive time lag**.

When the world had already entered the age of ironclad warships, our elites were still thinking through the formats of memorials, the model essays of the civil service examinations, and the ethics of ruler and subject.

That civilizational gap could not be closed by purchasing a few warships.

---

### Crack Four: Society Turned to Quicksand

### From “Farming and Learning Across Generations” to Refugees Everywhere

During the Qianlong era, the population had reached roughly 140 million.

By the Daoguang era, it had approached 400 million.

More people.

Less land.

Increasing land concentration.

I reviewed countless cases involving “bandits.”

But the case files were full of starving peasants.

You could never execute them all.

Because the soil that produced starving people was expanding every day.

The community security system had deteriorated.

County officials answered upward rather than downward.

The foundations of local administration were crumbling.

The White Lotus movement.

The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom.

The Nian Rebellion.

The problem was not that these people were extraordinarily powerful.

It was that **dry tinder was everywhere**.

We sat in Beijing reading reports declaring that everything was “peaceful.”

We did not realize that beneath the calm surface,

**the water had already reached boiling point.**

---

### Crack Five: The Paradox of Reform

### The Naivety of Reformers and the Intelligence of Conservatives

This is the wound that hurts most in my old age.

The Emperor wanted reform.

Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao were young and passionate.

I had read their writings.

They possessed real knowledge.

But why did the reform fail?

Because the reformers were trying to take away the **cheese of the entire old system**.

Abolish the civil service examinations?

Millions of scholars would lose their future.

Cut redundant officials?

Thousands would lose their livelihoods.

Establish a parliament?

What would remain of imperial authority?

Behind the Empress Dowager stood a coalition of Manchu aristocrats, conservative officials, and examination-trained scholars.

They were an **entrenched interest group** whose survival was intertwined with the old system.

Reform meant threatening their very existence.

What did the Guangxu Emperor and the reformers possess?

A few imperial edicts.

Passion.

Idealism.

What they did not possess was military power.

Or financial power.

Or real institutional power.

**This game was lost from the opening move.**

Because both the board and the pieces were controlled by the opponent.

I did not remain silent because I was weak.

I remained silent because I could see the **structural deadlock**.

Just like the ancient jade I collected.

Inside, it was already filled with hidden fractures.

Any attempt to carve it further might cause the entire piece to shatter.

---

# Epilogue: Buried in Snow

The medicine has gone cold.

The attendants are afraid to enter.

I know what they are waiting for.

That final breath.

In this life, I was the Metropolitan Graduate of Xianfeng.

A Minister under Tongzhi.

A member of the Grand Council under Guangxu.

I supported Zuo Zongtang.

I nurtured Wu Changshuo.

I compiled the *Pangxi Studio Collection of Books*.

I collected the *Yu* and *Ke* bronze vessels.

People said I had achieved everything.

That my reputation was secure.

That my collection was without equal.

But I know the truth.

I failed to preserve anything.

I could not preserve the nation's declining fortunes.

I could not preserve the rotten foundations of government.

I could not even preserve the bronzes and stones I loved.

After I die, they will scatter.

Some will be lost.

Some will be damaged.

Just like this dynasty.

It too will eventually dissolve into the dust of history.

“Minister Pang, too, merely embroidered wedding clothes for another.”

Yes.

All my life I walked on ice, trembling, repairing one crack after another.

And in the end, all I did was sew a few useless flowers onto a sinking ship.

The snow falls harder.

As if it means to bury everything.

Very well.

Let it.

Bury the ambitions of the Xianfeng era.

Bury the illusions of the Tongzhi Restoration.

Bury the struggles of the Guangxu era.

Bury this old man who could never quite adapt to the times.

Bury us all beneath the snow of the sixteenth year of Guangxu.

But you who come later—

**When you uncover these rusted bronzes,**

**when you read these yellowing diaries,**

remember this:

**It was not that we did not know the ship was sinking.**

**We knew.**

**We simply had no choice but to watch it happen.**

**And then, one by one,**

**we pulled the bones from our own bodies,**

**one after another,**

**and used them**

**as the final rivets**

**holding the broken ship together.**

On a night of wind and snow, a man prepares for sleep.

The lamp burns low.

This sorrow—

**will have no end.**

**—End—**

### Author's Note

**This work is a historical-fiction piece.**

It is based on historical materials and reasonable literary imagination, using a fictional first-person voice to explore the inner world of Pan Zuyin, a high-ranking Qing official, and his observations of an empire approaching historical rupture.

The psychological states, internal monologues, and certain interpretations presented in the text are **literary constructions rather than documented statements by Pan Zuyin**.

The purpose is not to claim that Pan Zuyin actually thought or spoke in these terms, but to explore, through historical fiction, the complex relationship between individuals and historical structures at a moment of civilizational transition.
