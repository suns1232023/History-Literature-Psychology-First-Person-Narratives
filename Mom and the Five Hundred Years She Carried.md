# **Mom and the Five Hundred Years She Carried**

### *A Diary by an Alpha-Star Editor*

**Keywords:** Cultural Media · Identity · Migration · Memory · Discrimination · Intergenerational Trauma

> My mother once told me that, in a previous life, her name was Su San.
>
> I laughed. I thought she had simply read too many Ming-dynasty stories.
>
> Later, I searched the database. There it was, in black and white, in *Stories to Caution the World*: the story of Yutangchun.
>
> She lost her parents at the age of five and was sold into a pleasure house in Beijing. There she met Wang Jinlong. When his money ran out, the madam threw him out. Su San secretly went to a ruined temple and gave him the silver hairpin she had saved, so that he could travel to the capital and sit for the examinations.
>
> In the end, Wang became an investigating censor and cleared her name. The lovers were reunited.
>
> The story ends there.
>
> But my mother says the book only recorded the day she was acquitted.
>
> No one wrote about the years that followed—how she climbed out of the mud, one trembling step at a time.
>
> Years later, on Alpha Star, I saw people calling my mother a **“Terran spouse.”**
>
> They rolled their eyes at her. Watched her as though she were a thief. Questioned her in public.
>
> And suddenly, I understood.
>
> **Five hundred years had passed. The world had changed its shell. But suffering had changed almost nothing.**
>
> Her enemy in the old life had been a minor official in Hongdong County.
>
> In this life, her enemy had become an interstellar statute.
>
> I could not change it.
>
> All I could do was remember.

## **Alpha Star Year 49, Frostmoon 26**

### *Yongkang Town, Southern Frontier — Cloudy*

My name is Su Xiao.

“Xiao” means dawn.

My mother is called Su San.

As for my father—

I don't want to talk about him today.

I took my mother's surname. It was the only thing she won when they divorced.

She fought for it with clenched teeth and red-rimmed eyes. After it was settled, she wiped away her tears and said only one thing to the lawyer:

“Thank you.”

I work as a junior editor for an online media outlet called **The Dialogue Record** in Yongkang Town.

The polite version is *editorial producer*.

The honest version is that every day I find topics, dig for material, write scripts, edit footage, and then sit in the comment section arguing with strangers.

Our motto is:

**“Wild. Civil. Human.”**

We specialize in people on the margins and injustices that everyone else would rather ignore.

We don't make much money.

But we earn a little something that money can't buy:

a conscience.

I've been saving that little bit ever since I started working here.

My supervisor is Lin Heng, a native of Alpha Star. She's a woman who works with the efficiency of a pair of scissors. Her tongue is sharp, but her heart isn't.

She has a five-year-old daughter studying at a kindergarten in Thailand, on Earth.

Yes.

You heard that correctly.

**A native Alpha-Starrer sends her daughter to Earth for school.**

I once asked her why.

She was making coffee and didn't even look up.

“Because the kindergartens in Phuket teach children to walk barefoot in the sand, catch crabs, and make whistles out of leaves. Here on Alpha Star, children start interstellar-standard testing at three, study interstellar history at four, and memorize the periodic table at five.

“You want me to raise my daughter as a weapon?”

I said nothing.

Her words reminded me of something my mother once told me:

> **“I've lived two lives, and there's still one thing I haven't learned—how to keep my child from walking the road I walked.”**

That word stayed with me.

**Road.**

It is a long one.

So long that it begins five hundred years ago, in the Ming dynasty.

## **My Mother Was Su San**

In her previous life, my mother was Yutangchun—Su San.

She lost her parents when she was five.

She was taken to a pleasure house like a bundle of merchandise.

By fifteen or sixteen, she had become something to be appraised, purchased, displayed.

Then she met Wang Jinlong.

He was the son of a high-ranking official in the Ministry of Personnel, born into wealth and privilege.

When his money ran out, the madam threw him into the street.

He curled up in a ruined temple, wrapped in a piece of filthy cotton cloth he had found somewhere.

It was Su San who slipped out in the middle of the night, took the silver hairpin and the private savings she had hidden away, stitched them into his coat, and pushed him toward the capital.

Later she was deceived, sold, and became the concubine of a merchant named Shen Yanling.

Shen was poisoned by his principal wife.

The murder was blamed on Su San.

**Hongdong County Prison.**

The beating.

The restraints.

The branding irons.

She bit down so hard that the skin around her mouth split. One side of her ear was torn by the clamps.

Had Wang Jinlong not eventually passed the imperial examinations and become an investigating censor in Shanxi, uncovered the old case, and overturned the verdict, she would have died at the execution ground.

No grave.

No mourner.

No name worth remembering.

Feng Menglong later turned the story into *Su San Finds Her Lover in Adversity* in *Stories to Caution the World*.

The moral was simple:

Love survives.

Justice prevails.

The lovers live happily ever after.

And then the curtain falls.

My mother says that on the day her name was cleared, she knelt in the courtroom with cold stone beneath her knees.

There was still her own blood on the floor.

After she stood up, **it took her decades to learn how not to wake screaming in the night.**

---

## **The Voice on the Wall**

Our office has an “audio wall.”

It can reconstruct voices so realistically that, when someone speaks, it feels as though they're standing beside you.

One day I secretly played an old opera recording of *Su San Leaves Hongdong County*.

The famous line came through the wall:

> **“Su San leaves Hongdong County…”**

The voice seemed to seep upward through a crack in the floor.

Lin Heng happened to walk past with a cup of coffee.

She stopped.

“Who's that?” she asked.

“Sounds tragic.”

I said:

“My mother.”

Her hand froze.

The coffee nearly spilled.

“Your mother?”

“She had a terrible life.”

I didn't answer.

Because Lin Heng knew only the tragic Su San.

She didn't know what happened afterward.

**The Su San who came later.**

The Su San who married an Alpha-Starrer.

The Su San who became a **Terran spouse**.

The Su San who was stared at in the market.

Questioned at government counters in that particular tone reserved for outsiders.

The Su San who spent sleepless nights turning an identity-card application over and over in her hands.

The Su San who eventually divorced her husband, fought for her son's surname, and raised him alone.

None of that was in the opera.

Feng Menglong didn't know.

Lin Heng didn't know.

---

## **The Weekend I Learned What “Tragedy” Really Means**

Last weekend, I was sorting footage in the living room while my mother sat curled up on the sofa watching a holographic projection.

I assumed she was watching another ancient drama from the Ming dynasty.

She wasn't.

It was a production from the Alpha Star Theatre.

A remake of an old Earth opera:

**The Fourth Son Visits His Mother.**

I had studied it in my Earth culture class.

The story goes back to the Song dynasty.

Yang Silang, the fourth son of the Yang family, is captured by the Liao after a battle and forced to live under another name. He eventually marries a Liao princess.

Fifteen years later, the two sides go to war again.

He hears that his mother, Lady She, has arrived near the battlefield with military provisions.

That night, he secretly crosses the border and finds his way into the Song camp.

The scene where mother and son finally meet is famous.

Yang Silang kneels before his mother and sings:

> **“A thousand bows, ten thousand prostrations—
> still they cannot repay a son's guilt…”**

My mother began to cry.

Not quietly.

Not the kind of crying you can hide by wiping your eyes once.

She folded in on herself, shoulders shaking.

I panicked.

“Mom?”

She waved me away and pointed at the screen.

When the scene ended, she finally whispered:

“He misses his mother.”

Then she paused.

“So do I.”

I didn't understand at first.

Then I realized.

She meant **my grandmother**.

Her mother.

The woman who had given birth to her in the previous life.

My mother had lost her parents at the age of five.

In this life, she was born on Alpha Star and given new parents—my grandparents.

But somewhere inside her memory, there had always been two mothers.

One was the woman on Alpha Star who knitted her sweaters, helped raise her child, and waited for her to come home.

The other was a woman from five hundred years ago whom she had never seen even in a portrait.

The woman who had given birth to her and, before she had time to memorize her daughter's face, had been forced to send her away to survive.

**Five hundred years had passed, and my mother had never once called that woman “Mother.”**

Yang Silang said it for her.

> **“A thousand bows, ten thousand prostrations—
> still they cannot repay a son's guilt.”**

I remembered an essay I had once read.

Someone had taken his father to see *The Fourth Son Visits His Mother*.

The father cried uncontrollably.

Not because the opera was particularly brilliant.

But because someone on the stage had finally spoken the words he had kept buried for decades.

Those who left their hometowns and never returned.

Those who left their mothers behind and spent an entire lifetime carrying the weight of it.

They sat in the audience while Yang Silang cried for them.

My mother was not one of them.

She was Su San.

But on Alpha Star, she shared the same wound:

**It wasn't that she couldn't meet her mother.**

**It was that she could never go home.**

Those people could not return to their hometowns.

My mother could not return to Earth.

They all had mothers on the other side of the stage.

Yang Silang could at least kneel before Lady She.

**My mother did not even have a grave before which she could kneel.**

No one knows where her mother from the previous life was buried.

Her mother in this life lives on Earth.

And my mother is afraid to go back.

Because once she went, she might never be allowed to return.

## **The Two Years That Never Appeared on the Form**

That night, my mother went to bed early.

I tucked the blanket around her and returned to the living room.

I played *The Fourth Son Visits His Mother* again, this time with the volume barely audible.

When the singer reached the line about a thousand bows and ten thousand prostrations, I cried.

I had never met my grandmother from the Ming dynasty.

I had never met my grandmother on Earth either.

And yet, suddenly, I felt as if they were both sitting across from me.

Separated by five hundred years of theatre and fifty light-years of space.

Listening to the same voice call for a mother.

---

My mother married an Alpha-Starrer in this life.

His surname was Chen.

He worked in the synthetic-fruit wholesale business.

They met at a wholesale market.

My mother once described him as:

“Kind of wooden. Doesn't know how to talk much. But he knows how to stand between me and the wind.”

They married.

They had me.

In those years, my mother rarely mentioned the name Su San.

She lived as an ordinary Terran spouse on Alpha Star.

Under Alpha Star law, an off-world spouse could apply for citizenship after four years of marriage.

But a spouse from Earth had to wait **six years**.

Those additional two years meant:

no full medical coverage,

no formal employment rights,

and no choice but to take under-the-table work.

When children entered school, their mother's origin was listed in the registry as:

**Earth.**

Parents noticed.

The atmosphere changed.

My mother was one of hundreds of thousands of Terran spouses carrying that two-year gap through their lives.

She carried it for twenty years.

She never cried in front of me.

She only said:

> “Crying won't make the card arrive sooner.”

But once, I overheard her speaking to my grandmother—the woman on Alpha Star who was her mother in this life.

She said:

> **“Mom, I can live without the card. What I can't live with is the way people look at me.”**

I remembered those words for five years.

They still make my chest tighten.

---

## **The Years Without a Name**

In the early years, my mother worked illegally.

Without legal status, she couldn't apply to legitimate employers.

So she went wherever people asked the fewest questions.

Waiting tables.

Washing dishes.

Moving boxes.

The lowest wages.

The hardest work.

And absolute silence.

One report could destroy everything.

Once, she developed a fever of forty degrees Celsius.

She curled up on the sofa beneath a thin blanket.

Her lips were cracked from the heat.

My father was away on business.

I was alone with her and terrified.

She touched my head.

“Xiao Su, why are you crying?”

Then she smiled.

“Your mother survived a fever in a Hongdong prison cell with a bowl of spoiled rice water.

“This is nothing.”

**She carried her previous life with her to Alpha Star.**

Every New Year, she made dumplings.

Sometimes she hummed an old melody.

If I leaned closer, I could hear:

> “Su San leaves Hongdong County…”

She always stretched the final word.

Long.

Long enough to sound like a rope.

A rope tied around the mouth of a bottomless well.

My grandmother told me that my mother had once lived in Datong Prefecture, Shanxi.

But she could not go back.

She had no secure status.

She was even afraid to apply for a visit to Earth.

What if she went and couldn't return?

## **The Divorce**

Five years ago, they divorced.

My mother never told me exactly why.

I think I know.

My father's relatives.

His friends.

His business partners.

They had said too much.

> “What happens when your wife still can't get citizenship?”

> “What will your child be—Terran or Alpha-Starrer?”

> “People say Terran women marry here just to take money. You'd better be careful.”

I heard versions of those sentences more times than I can count.

My father never contradicted them.

At most, he would say:

“She'll be fine.”

**She'll be fine.**

Two tiny words.

Light as a falling leaf.

Not nearly heavy enough to cover an entire winter.

When they divorced, what did my father fight over?

The house?

The savings?

Neither.

He wanted the money my mother had saved.

She had washed dishes, carried boxes, worked in factories, skipped meals, and saved every credit she could.

It wasn't much.

But every unit of that money had been bought with the dirt beneath her fingernails.

She wanted to use it to send me to school.

My father said it was marital property and demanded half.

My mother didn't argue.

She didn't scream.

She didn't cry.

She transferred the money.

Then she said:

> **“Once he has the money, he has no excuse left. No more telling people that I was the one who wronged him.”**

There was only one thing she refused to give up.

**Me.**

“Everything else can go.

“But my child keeps my surname.”

She wanted her child to remember one thing.

My surname is Su.

**The same Su as Su San.**

## **The Terran Spouses**

At *The Dialogue Record*, we recently produced an episode about **Terran spouses**.

Our guest was Lin Xiuzhi.

She had been married to an Alpha-Starrer for more than twenty years.

After years of waiting, she had finally been placed on the candidate list for a district representative seat.

On the day she took the oath, she reached the registration desk and suddenly began to cry.

I watched her through the camera.

My nose began to sting.

There were nearly **four hundred thousand Terran spouses across Alpha Star**.

Lin Xiuzhi was only one of them.

Some had been given insulting nicknames.

Some were repeatedly investigated.

Some were reported for “saying things they should not have said.”

Some were ordered to leave the planet.

We devoted an entire episode to the story of Aya.

On her personal channel, she once said:

> **“I am a person from Earth.”**

Security authorities ordered her to leave.

Her children had different citizenship statuses.

One Terran.

One Alpha-Starrer.

**A family divided by law.**

I brought some of the Earth footage back to Lin Heng.

The comments were brutal.

> “After twenty years of marriage, she should have become one of us.”

> “If she doesn't want to leave, then who should?”

I closed the page.

It felt as if someone had punched me in the chest.

---

## **The Empty Bowl**

My mother was watching the episode too.

She sat in front of the television peeling green beans.

When the program ended, the bowl was already empty.

But she kept peeling.

Pick up a bean.

Snap both ends.

Break it in two.

Put it down.

Pick up another.

The bowl was empty.

She continued sitting there for a long time.

I said:

“Mom, if you're upset, go lie down.”

She didn't look up.

“I’m not upset.”

Her voice was very quiet.

“I just think they're so stupid.”

She paused.

“Five hundred years ago, people said there were no good people in Hongdong County.

“Five hundred years later, they've come all this way and made the same mistake again.”

She smiled bitterly.

> **“Five hundred years, and people still haven't learned.”**

Her eyes reddened.

But she didn't cry.

I remembered the old line from the opera:

> **“There are no good people in Hongdong County.”**

Today, Lin Xiuzhi was not even looked at.

Not hated.

Not opposed.

Simply deemed unworthy of being heard.

You were a **Terran spouse**.

Therefore, your voice counted for less.

Yang Silang could speak for those who could not return home.

Lin Xiuzhi could speak for the Terran spouses.

But who would speak for my mother?

She could only hum *Su San Leaves Hongdong County* in the kitchen.

That song was both the verdict of her previous life

and the prophecy of this one.

My mother once told me:

“They're separated by five hundred years.”

I said:

> **“No. They're separated by the human heart.”**

## **The Women Who Left**

My mother doesn't have many friends here.

Most of her closest friends are Terran spouses she met while working in factories.

There was an auntie named Zhang.

One day she didn't come to work.

Later we learned what had happened.

Her husband's family had reported her.

They claimed she had said things before marriage that had offended them.

The security bureau summoned her.

Then came the order:

**Leave the planet.**

I wanted to go to the airport when she left.

My mother wouldn't let me.

“You're a child.

“What are you going to do there?”

Only later did I understand.

She wasn't protecting me from the airport.

She was protecting me from watching Zhang Auntie cry.

After Zhang left, my mother barely went outside for a month.

It wasn't that she didn't want to.

She was afraid.

People on the street knew she had been Zhang's friend.

What if someone reported her too?

One night she said:

“Xiao Su, don't be afraid.

“Your mother isn't going anywhere.

“I still have you.”

But she couldn't sleep.

I woke up one night to use the bathroom and saw her sitting alone on the balcony.

The night wind on Alpha Star blew through her hair.

It was turning white.

Not metaphorically.

**Actually white.**

Before Zhang left, my mother had barely had a single gray hair.

Within a month, she looked as if frost had settled over her.

## **What I Can Still Do**

Sometimes I want to make an episode about my mother.

Her previous life.

Her life here.

The lives of all the Terran spouses.

Everything.

Put it together.

Broadcast it.

Lin Heng told me:

“You can.”

Then she paused.

“But don't expect it to change anything.”

I know she's right.

I can't change the politicians.

I can't change the law.

I can't change the way people look at my mother.

**She has lived two lives.**

From Hongdong County to Alpha Star.

From “courtesan” to “Terran spouse.”

The name changed.

The pain in her bones did not.

I can write as much as I want.

Broadcast as much as I want.

And perhaps none of it will change anything.

**But there is one thing I can choose not to do.**

Five hundred years ago, people stepped over the embroidered shoe that fell from Su San's prison cart.

They kept walking.

Their faces unchanged.

Their lives undisturbed.

**I will not step over it.**

I will not do what they did.

---

## **The Last Glass of Wine**

A few days ago, I came home after interviewing Lin Xiuzhi.

My mother and I had dinner together.

I poured her half a glass of red wine.

“Mom,” I said.

“I want to drink to you.”

She glared at me.

“You're just a little editor. What are you drinking for?”

I smiled.

> **“Not because I'm an editor.
>
> Because you are you.
>
> Because you are Su San.”**

She held the glass.

She stared at the white wall across the room for a long time.

As if she were looking at someone.

Or a road that had taken five hundred years and still had not reached its end.

Finally, she said:

> **“Any surname is fine.
>
> Just don't let it be ‘Wronged.’”**

She finished the glass.

So did I.

After dinner, she said she was tired and went to bed.

I waited until she was asleep.

Then I turned on the desk lamp and opened my diary.

I wrote.

Deleted.

Wrote again.

Deleted again.

Eventually my fingers became stiff.

Finally, I took every piece of footage I had collected over the past few years:

Aya.

Lin Xiuzhi.

Zhang Auntie.

My mother peeling beans.

My mother making dumplings.

My mother's silhouette sitting before an empty bowl.

I saved everything onto a single drive.

Not for a story.

Not for ratings.

Not for traffic.

**Because I am afraid that one day, people on Alpha Star will erase this history.**

So I want to leave something behind.

For my mother.

For Lin Xiuzhi.

For every woman who has ever been called a **Terran spouse**.

Something for the people who come after us.

Maybe five hundred years from now, another Su San will be born here.

Maybe she will open this old drive.

And perhaps she will see that, in Alpha Star Year 49, there was a place called *The Dialogue Record*.

There was an editor named Su.

And that editor tried to remember them.

Even if nothing changed.

**At least I will not step over the embroidered shoe.**

---

From *The Three-Hall Inquest* to **discrimination against Terran spouses**.

From Su San to Su Xiao.

From Hongdong County to Alpha Star.

**Five hundred years have passed.**

Much has changed.

And much has not changed at all.

The only difference is this:

In her previous life, Su San endured alone.

She endured in a prison cell in Hongdong County.

She walked alone through the wind.

She waited alone for death at the execution ground.

In this life, Su San lives on Alpha Star.

And there is someone who writes down what happened to her.

Even if it is only a diary.

Dawn is coming.

I go into the kitchen.

I steal one of the osmanthus cakes my mother hid in the refrigerator last night.

It is sweet.
