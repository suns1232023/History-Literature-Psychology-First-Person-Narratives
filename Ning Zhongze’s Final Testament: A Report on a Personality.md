# Ning Zhongze’s Final Testament: A Report on a Personality

### A Snowy Night on Mount Hua


> *Seventh day of the twelfth month, Year of Wuxu. Beneath the Cliff of Reflection on Mount Hua. The snow is three feet deep.*

I chose this place.

From up here, the wind along the cliff will carry the smell of blood away quickly enough. It won't disturb the disciples who are still performing their little drama of “righteousness on Mount Hua.”

If Yue were here, he would frown and call me **“willful.”**

He has used that word to silence me for our entire marriage.

Tonight he isn't here.

He has gone to Fuzhou, following the trail of the *Bixie Sword Manual*.

He never even noticed that I had been fading away, day by day.

Perhaps that is for the best.

I am forty-one this year.

For twenty years, the jianghu has known me as **Lady Ning of Mount Hua**.

But tonight, sitting before the mirror and removing my armor, I still see the fourteen-year-old girl who followed her master up the mountain, her hair tied in a ponytail, believing that the word *heroism* could protect a person for an entire lifetime.

The woman in the mirror has loosened skin around her eyes.

There is a scar at the corner of my mouth, left by the Three Wolves of the Northern Frontier when I gave birth to Linghu Chong.

There is another across my chest, from the day I stepped between Yue and danger when his practice of the Purple霞 Divine Skill went awry.

Twenty-seven years of marriage.

Every scar on my body can tell a story about what it means to be a **virtuous wife**.

But tonight I ask myself:

How many of those stories are true?

And how many did I force myself to believe?

I turn the lamp higher.

If a person is going to perform an autopsy on herself, she needs enough light to see.

---

## I. How I Married Yue Buqun—and Lied to Myself for Twenty-Seven Years

I came to Mount Hua when I was fourteen.

My master, Cangsong, had taken Yue Buqun as his senior disciple.

I was his only female disciple, three years younger than Yue.

The entire jianghu called us **the Twin Jewels of Mount Hua**.

He was refined.

I was fierce.

He knew how to hide a needle inside a sleeve.

I could draw my sword straight to someone's face.

Before my master died, he passed the position of Sect Leader to Yue and entrusted me to him.

He said:

> “Your junior sister is wild. Only you can keep her in check.”

At the time, I thought it was a blessing.

Now I understand.

It was the first trap.

It took me twenty-seven years to climb out of it.

What did I think back then?

I thought my senior brother was a gentleman.

The entire jianghu praised him as **the Gentleman Sword**.

If he was willing to marry a junior sister who knew little beyond swords and fighting, I thought I was fortunate.

I mistook **his superiority to me for love**.

I mistook **my supposed unworthiness of him for humility**.

But think about it.

When does a fourteen-year-old girl need to be “kept in check”?

Who decided that being “wild” was a defect?

Yue began teaching me the answer from the very first day.

When I cursed someone, he would say:

> “Even a lady hero should carry herself with dignity.”

When I wanted to go down the mountain to chase bandits, he would say:

> “I will take care of it. You stay here and look after the house.”

When I said:

> “I insist.”

He would smile.

The smile was warm, like a spring breeze.

Then came the final sentence:

> “There you go again, Zhongze. Being willful.”

He never shouted at me.

He never insulted me.

He simply smiled and **filed down my edges** until I began to believe I had been born round.

It wasn't that I never doubted him.

In our third year of marriage, he rose at night to practice his sword.

Through a crack in the window, I saw a technique that was not Mount Hua swordsmanship.

It was fast.

Devious.

Dark.

It looked...

It looked like what would later become the *Bixie Swordsmanship*.

I asked him about it.

He said:

> “Zhongze, you must have seen it wrong. It was only a variation of the Purple霞 technique.”

The next day I searched the library for the entire day.

I found nothing.

Looking back now, I realize he had already begun hiding things from me.

And I chose to believe the explanation that made me comfortable.

Because the belief that **“my husband is a gentleman”** mattered more to me than what my own eyes had seen.

I changed my eyes.

**I changed them myself.**

---

## II. What I Owe Linghu Chong Is Not Maternal Love, but an Apology

The most painful part of tonight is Chong'er.

The first time he called me “Shimu,” his face turned red like a wild jujube.

He was clutching half a piece of wild honey he had gathered for me.

That child had loved me from the beginning.

Whenever Yue beat him, he would hide behind my apron in the kitchen.

I would secretly slip him a piece of osmanthus cake.

Yue never said anything to my face.

But behind my back, he spent more and more time practicing his sword.

When Chong'er was fifteen, Yue said in front of me for the first time:

> “That wretched disciple is too wild. He will become the ruin of Mount Hua.”

I was embroidering a handkerchief.

The needle pierced my finger.

I said:

> “He's only a child.”

Yue put down his teacup.

His voice was soft as falling snow.

> “Zhongze, the ‘child’ of a sect leader and *your* child are not the same thing.”

I remembered that sentence for twenty years.

Yue never allowed there to be such things as **“mine” and “his”** within the Mount Hua Sect.

The disciples were extensions of him.

So was his wife.

Chong'er's closeness to me was not, in his eyes, a disciple receiving a mother's affection.

It was **faction-building**.

Later, when Chong'er was expelled from the sect, I knelt in the hall for an entire night, begging Yue to revoke the order.

That night, for the first time, Yue truly became angry with me.

Not by shouting.

Something colder.

> “Ning Zhongze, are you the wife of the Sect Leader of Mount Hua—or are you Linghu Chong's mother?”

Look at that.

Even the word **“mother”** came out of his mouth like an accusation.

I cried and said:

> “I... I only think it's unjust.”

But I knew the truth.

I wasn't only grieving Chong's injustice.

**I was grieving my own.**

I simply couldn't say it.

How could a female hero of Mount Hua, a sect leader's wife, a *shimu*, admit that she herself had been wronged?

Chastity.

Virtue.

The greater good.

Three crowns placed on my head.

And beneath their weight, I couldn't even say:

> “I want Chong to stay.”

I had to wrap those words in three layers of “for the sake of Mount Hua” before I dared let them leave my mouth.

The day Chong left, he knelt three times beneath the cliff.

He did not look at Yue.

He looked at me.

He said:

> “Shimu, take care.”

I nodded.

I wouldn't let the tears fall.

I was afraid Yue was watching from the corridor.

I did not become this person in a single day.

It began the first time you wanted to resist and discovered that every road was labeled **“the greater good.”**

It began the first time you said no, and everyone answered with three words:

**“You've changed.”**

And those words pushed you back into place.

It began when you discovered that **chastity and virtue were not shields built to protect you, but ropes used to bind you.**

Later, Chong became famous throughout the jianghu as a great hero.

At the dinner table, Yue sneered:

> “What a fine son-in-law for the Demon Sect.”

I lowered my head and pushed rice around my bowl.

I could not swallow a single mouthful.

How I wanted to tell him:

The instinct in Chong to protect someone without counting the cost was **the only living flame left inside the Hall of Righteousness at Mount Hua.**

But I said nothing.

Because if I did,

I would prove that I was **disloyal**.

---

## III. About That Book—and What I Saw That Night

The words **“Bixie Sword Manual”** first entered Mount Hua through Yue.

He mentioned them as if by accident.

That evening he was turning the pages of an old manuscript beneath the lamp.

After a while he said:

> “Zhongze, how do you suppose Lin Zhen'nan of the Fuwei Escort Agency came to such a miserable end?”

I was sewing the collar of his winter robe.

Without looking up, I said:

> “Fate.”

He was silent for a moment.

“Fate?”

He laughed.

The sound was different from his usual laughter.

Like the edge of a blade scraping against the rim of a bowl.

> “Zhongze, you're still so... unwilling to compete.”

I stopped sewing and looked at him.

Half his face was in shadow.

For a moment, I did not recognize that half of him.

I said:

> “Senior Brother, I don't understand.”

He gave no answer.

He closed the book, blew out the lamp, and took my hand in the darkness.

His hand was warm.

But something inside me went cold.

Then the book arrived.

Then Chong left.

And then—

Lin Pingzhi.

That child changed completely.

His eyes went from those of a frightened young deer to those of a snake:

cold,

coiled,

always ready to strike.

I saw him practicing in the rear mountains.

His movements drifted like a ghost.

And in that moment I suddenly understood:

**That book devoured people.**

It had devoured Lin Pingzhi.

And it was devouring the man sleeping beside me.

During the period when Yue practiced the Bixie Swordsmanship, there was a smell about him.

Not sweat.

Not blood.

Something else.

The sickly sweetness of a flower that had dried and rotted from the root.

At night, when he turned in bed, I pretended to sleep.

My eyes remained open, staring at the canopy.

I wondered:

Is this still the young man who once stood on Chaoyang Peak, his robes floating in the wind, saying:

> “A gentleman should be like the wind”?

Or—

**did that young man never exist at all?**

Was he only an image I had created?

But that wasn't the most painful part.

The most painful part was that **some part of me envied that book.**

Do you believe me?

I had practiced the Ning family sword for half my life.

Straightforward.

Open.

Honorable.

And yet whenever the jianghu spoke of Mount Hua swordsmanship, they spoke of:

**“How the Sect Leader Yue does this.”**

No matter how beautiful my swordplay was, it was always merely:

**“the swordsmanship of the Sect Leader's wife.”**

That book was ugly.

Poisonous.

Ruthless.

But it belonged to the person who practiced it.

At least those who wielded it knew **why they were raising their swords.**

I envied them.

Even if they chose corruption,

**at least they chose it themselves.**

As for me—

I had not even been free to fall.

---

## IV. After “That Incident,” I Finally Saw Myself Clearly

One month ago, Yue made Chong's guilt official.

At the gathering of the Five Mountains Alliance, in front of everyone, he turned Chong's love for a woman of the Demon Sect into:

**collusion with evil, betrayal of his master, and treason against Mount Hua.**

I saw Chong standing below the hall.

He did not argue.

He simply looked up at the ceiling.

His expression reminded me of when he was seven years old, sitting in a ruined temple and using a twig to mend a grasshopper's broken leg.

He knew something in the world had broken.

But he was still trying to put it back together.

When Yue finished speaking, he turned and looked at me.

That look was asking one thing:

**Would I challenge him?**

I stood.

Every eye in the hall turned toward me.

I opened my mouth.

To this day, I don't know what I intended to say.

But Yue smiled first.

That smile opened like a paper fan.

He said:

> “My wife has not slept for several nights because of this wayward disciple. She is soft-hearted. She cannot bear to see a child suffer.”

He said the words **“soft-hearted”** with such tenderness and such weight that they descended upon me like a mountain.

Every word I wanted to speak froze in my throat.

Then I nodded.

I nodded.

I sat back down in my place as **Mrs. Yue**.

Inside my sleeve, I dug my fingernails into my own flesh until they pierced the skin.

Blood seeped into the fabric.

That was the moment I hated myself.

**I hated myself to the bone.**

All my life, I had been executing the more truthful version of myself.

Every time Chong came to me for help, I protected him with half a sentence and swallowed the other half.

Every time Yue lied, I saw the lie and helped him cover it.

Every time I practiced my sword late at night and felt that old force rising inside me—

**I insist.**

I pushed it back down.

I had executed her dozens of times.

But she never truly died.

Tonight,

**she finally turned over and put her hands around my throat.**

---

## V. About the Three Characters “Ning Zhongze”

There are three questions I answered in the wrong direction all my life.

### First: Who am I?

I answered:

**“Yue Buqun's wife.”**

I forgot to answer:

**“Ning Zhongze.”**

### Second: What should I do?

I chose:

**“Support my husband. Raise the children. Serve the sect.”**

And by choosing that road, I made it narrower and narrower until every step took me farther away from myself.

### Third: Where is my boundary?

I protected the bond between husband and wife.

The bond between mother and child.

The reputation of Mount Hua.

But when the husband became a demon,

when the disciple was expelled,

when the sect became a laughingstock—

I finally understood.

Everything I had spent my life protecting was **a tower built on sand.**

Do you know what is hardest?

The hardest thing is that after you finally see everything clearly,

**you no longer have the strength to hate.**

Yue deceived me for half my life.

But he also deceived himself for half his life.

His copy of the Bixie Sword Manual had become worn at the edges from being opened so many times.

He truly believed that the absurd promise of sacrificing himself could somehow turn him into a great man.

How desperate must a person become before he believes that **self-destruction can be exchanged for power?**

He is pitiful.

Lin Pingzhi is pitiful too.

Beneath all the righteousness proclaimed across this mountain,

**everyone is pitiful.**

But I will not pity myself.

I was—

**stupid.**

Stupid because I saw the truth and still helped conceal it.

Stupid because I could have walked away and still chose to smooth things over for someone else.

Stupid because I practiced a sword for thirty years,

and in the end,

**the only person it wounded was me.**

What finally broke me was not seeing him clearly.

It was seeing **myself** clearly.

Seeing how much of what I called “gentleness” was actually cowardice.

How much of what I called “understanding” was actually surrender.

How much of what I called “the greater good” was simply my fear of overturning the table.

I spent half my life polishing the character for **“virtuous.”**

I polished it until it became transparent.

Only then did I discover what was written on the other side:

**Endurance.**

---

## VI. The Last Page

Tonight, sitting inside the stone chamber beneath the Cliff of Reflection, I took out the old things and looked at them one last time.

The copy of the *Zhuangzi* he once made for me.

The crooked characters Chong wrote when he was seven:

> “Happy New Year, Shimu.”

And the wooden hairpin.

I placed the hairpin on the stone table.

Then I drew my sword.

The Ning family sword.

Straight.

Honorable.

Uncompromising.

I have practiced this sword for thirty years.

With it, I killed bandits.

Protected disciples.

And once, stood between Yue and an attack meant for him.

Tonight I will use it for one final act of righteousness.

**I will cut myself free from the shell called “Mrs. Yue.”**

It does not hurt.

Truly.

What hurts are the moments when I was alive.

Snow is still falling beneath the cliff.

Tomorrow morning, the disciples will find the sword standing upright in the snow.

Their shimu will already be cold.

Yue will probably hurry back from Fuzhou.

He will shed a few tears before my spirit tablet and say:

> “Zhongze, how could you have been so foolish?”

And Chong—

Chong will kneel in the snow.

He will not cry.

He will not shout.

He will simply press his forehead into the ground until blood stains the snow.

How I wish I could tell him:

**Child, you did nothing wrong.**

The one who was wrong was me.

Wrong because I saw that you were right and still failed to stand beside you.

Wrong because I traded thirty years of peace for two words:

**“Your shimu.”**

Wrong because I would rather use death to prove that I regretted it than, while still alive, say one simple thing:

**“I believe you.”**

But he will never hear those words from the living me.

So I write them here.

Snow will cover this page.

The wind will tear it apart.

But at least, on this one night,

someone named **Ning Zhongze**

finally told herself the truth.

---

## VII. The Very Last Thing

Before extinguishing the lamp, I looked once more at the wooden hairpin.

When he carved it, I was nineteen.

His hand was steady as he held the knife.

A blister on his thumb had burst, and a bead of blood seeped into the grain of the wood.

I had been terribly worried.

I held his hand and blew gently on it.

He smiled and pulled it away.

> “It doesn't hurt. I carved it for you. How could it hurt?”

I have kept that image all these years.

Kept it until tonight.

I buried the hairpin in the snow.

Along with the nineteen-year-old girl who believed that **clumsy craftsmanship was proof of a sincere heart.**

She should not die with me.

She should remain in that spring.

Forever.

Never knowing what came afterward.

And I—

Ning Zhongze.

Wife of the Sect Leader of Mount Hua.

The woman the jianghu called **Lady Ning**.

Tonight, at last, I will no longer be anyone's wife.

Anyone's hero.

Anyone's *shimu*.

**I am simply myself.**

A sword.

A person.

A field of snow.

**Enough.**

*Seventh day of the twelfth month, Year of Wuxu.
Mount Hua.
The snow has stopped.*

**Ning Zhongze — final testament.**
