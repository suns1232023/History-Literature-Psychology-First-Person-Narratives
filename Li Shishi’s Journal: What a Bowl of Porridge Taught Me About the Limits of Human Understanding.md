# **Li Shishi’s Journal: What a Bowl of Porridge Taught Me About the Limits of Human Understanding**

*Kaifeng, Northern Song Dynasty*

**Keywords:** Cultural & Creative Writing · Historical Fiction · Cognition · Human Behavior

---

The wind in Bianliang always seemed to carry dust.

Sometimes I wonder: if the city had not been falling apart, if the news of war had not crept closer day by day, perhaps I would have grown old in the pleasure quarters, surrounded by music, poetry, wine, and the endless performance of beauty.

But history turns without asking permission.

And when it turns, no one remains untouched.

Tonight, I am writing about the porridge.

Not to defend myself.

Not to make myself appear virtuous.

Only to remember what I learned from those hundreds of pots of rice porridge—and what they taught me about people.

---

## **I. The Mood of the City**

Kaifeng had once been one of the richest cities in the world.

Wine banners fluttered above the streets. Lanterns reflected in the river. Flower sellers called softly through the alleys. Scholars composed poems over cups of wine, while merchants calculated fortunes behind carved wooden doors.

But when the Jin armies began moving south, something changed.

The court debated peace and war.

Officials argued over strategy.

Memorials traveled back and forth.

And ordinary people waited.

What I heard most often was:

*"Nothing will happen."*

*"The government has everything under control."*

*"The Song dynasty has ruled for generations. How could it simply collapse?"*

The words sounded comforting.

But comfort can sometimes be more dangerous than fear.

People tend to believe what allows them to remain inside the world they already understand.

Rice prices rose.

Then rose again.

Then again.

In the poorer districts, families began selling their possessions. Then their livestock. Then, in some cases, their children.

I began to understand something I had never understood during the peaceful years:

**In times of stability, people can hide from reality. In times of crisis, reality forces people to reveal themselves.**

---

## **II. Why I Began Cooking Porridge**

People later asked why a woman like me would suddenly appear in the streets distributing food.

I wish I could say it was pure compassion.

It was not.

One night, I heard a woman crying somewhere beyond the western market.

Her voice was thin and broken, like dry grass moving in the wind.

I sent someone to ask what had happened.

A family of three had not eaten for two days.

That night I could not sleep.

Not because I suddenly became a saint.

Because I became afraid.

If I remained behind embroidered curtains while people outside starved, what did this city have to do with me?

And if the city fell, where exactly would I hide?

For the first time, I began to understand that the boundary between **"my life"** and **"our society"** is often an illusion.

In peaceful times, we can pretend that they are separate.

In a crisis, they become one.

I took part of my savings and bought rice.

Then I asked several old acquaintances for help.

Some agreed immediately.

Others refused.

Those who agreed were mostly merchants. They understood what rising food prices meant.

Those who refused were often educated men who spoke proudly of propriety.

*"A woman should not appear in the streets,"* one of them told me.

*"Charity is the government's responsibility."*

I listened quietly.

And I realized something:

**People rarely act outside the boundaries of the world they believe they inhabit.**

For them, feeding the hungry belonged to the government.

Public action belonged to men.

A woman like me belonged in the private world.

To step outside that role was not merely inconvenient.

It was, in their minds, improper.

But I had begun to see the problem differently.

If everyone waited for someone else to act, then no one would act at all.

---

## **III. The First Pot**

We built a simple shelter on an empty piece of land south of the city.

The early spring wind was still bitter.

We built a stove.

Then another.

Smoke rose into the gray sky.

Three women came to help me.

The first was a widow named Zhao. She had lived alone for years and could work faster than anyone I knew.

The second, Wu, had once worked in a restaurant kitchen.

The third, Chen, was young. Her husband was serving somewhere with the army.

Each carried her own private grief.

When the first pot of porridge was ready, more than a hundred people had gathered.

Some thanked us.

Some pushed forward.

Some fought for their share.

Zhao became furious.

*"Where is the law?"* she shouted.

I stopped her.

There is a kind of hunger before which ordinary rules become very fragile.

That evening, I thought about what had happened.

Good intentions would not be enough.

A single act of generosity could feed a hundred people for one day.

But tomorrow there would still be another hundred.

Perhaps more.

I understood then:

**Compassion without structure eventually runs out of fuel.**

Charity is not merely an emotion.

It is a system.

It needs resources.

It needs organization.

It needs people who will return tomorrow.

---

## **IV. Cooperation and Resistance**

As the days passed, more people came.

Our rice diminished.

Our money diminished faster.

I visited several merchants and asked whether they would sell rice to us at a reduced price.

Some agreed.

Some laughed.

One merchant named Guo looked at me and said:

*"Miss Li, you are famous. If you perform a charitable act, people will praise you. But if I lower my price, what will people think?"*

*"Will they think my rice is poor quality?"*

*"Will they think I have lost my judgment?"*

*"Will they think I cannot run a business?"*

I understood him.

He was not necessarily cruel.

He was trapped inside a model of the world that had served him well.

For years he had survived by protecting his margins.

Profit meant security.

Price meant status.

A concession looked like weakness.

His previous experience had taught him what worked.

And that was precisely the problem.

**The strategies that make us successful in one environment can become liabilities when the environment changes.**

The merchants who eventually helped us were mostly smaller traders.

Perhaps because they had less to protect.

Perhaps because people accustomed to surviving in narrow spaces learn to adapt more quickly.

That day I learned another lesson:

**Experience gives people knowledge—but it can also imprison them inside yesterday's logic.**

---

## **V. Words Are Cheap**

Soon the city began talking.

Some people called me a heroine.

Others called me a performer seeking attention.

Some scholars even wrote poems praising my generosity.

They did not, however, contribute any money.

I did not resent them.

I simply began to notice something.

People often confuse **describing an action with taking an action**.

In their minds, speaking for justice can feel like participating in justice.

Writing about suffering can feel like helping the suffering.

Praise can become a substitute for responsibility.

I remembered the literary gatherings of my younger years.

We had spoken beautifully then.

We had discussed poetry, beauty, morality, loyalty, and the fate of the empire.

But when the sound of cavalry approached the city walls, beautiful words suddenly became very light.

They could not feed anyone.

They could not repair a wall.

They could not stop an army.

**Knowledge that never becomes action remains decoration.**

---

## **VI. When the Enemy Drew Near**

The news became worse.

The court could not agree.

The soldiers were exhausted.

The morale of the city declined.

More and more of those who came for porridge were relatives of wounded soldiers.

One afternoon, an old man holding his bowl began to cry.

*"If the city falls,"* he said, *"you must leave too."*

I did not answer.

Where would I go?

The south was not necessarily safe.

And by then, I had become tied to this city in a way I had never expected.

That was the first time I truly understood fear.

Not fear of death.

Fear of helplessness.

The porridge could postpone hunger.

It could not stop an army.

It could not repair a collapsing state.

It could not restore a political system that had lost its ability to respond.

I realized that I had been measuring the problem at the wrong scale.

**A person can solve a local problem while remaining powerless against a systemic failure.**

And failing to recognize that difference is itself a cognitive limitation.

---

## **VII. Three Kinds of People**

During those months, I began to notice three kinds of people.

The first were those crushed by immediate reality.

They wanted one bowl today.

They did not ask what would happen tomorrow.

I could not blame them.

When hunger consumes your attention, the future becomes a luxury.

The second were those who spoke endlessly about ideals.

They discussed the empire, morality, strategy, loyalty, and the destiny of the dynasty.

But they rarely touched the mud beneath their feet.

Their understanding remained at the level of concepts.

They could describe the problem.

They could not organize a response.

The third group was much smaller.

They were the people who could change their minds.

They admitted when yesterday's assumptions no longer worked.

They listened.

They experimented.

They changed their behavior when reality contradicted their expectations.

Those people seemed to survive longer.

I began to understand:

**Human beings do not act according to reality itself.**

They act according to the model of reality they carry inside their minds.

And when that model fails to update, behavior fails with it.

---

## **VIII. My Own Blindness**

But what right did I have to judge them?

I had once been trapped in my own small world.

I believed beauty could protect me.

I believed talent could secure my future.

I believed that being admired by scholars meant that I somehow participated in the affairs of the world.

Perhaps, without war, I would never have discovered how narrow that world was.

Crisis forced me to learn.

And that may be one of the cruelest truths about human development:

**We often change only when reality becomes impossible to ignore.**

---

## **IX. The Last Pot**

On the night before the city finally collapsed, we were still cooking.

There was very little rice left.

The flame flickered beneath the pot.

Zhao suddenly asked:

*"If the Jin soldiers enter tomorrow, will we still cook?"*

I looked at the boiling water.

For a long time, I said nothing.

Then I answered:

*"If there is still a handful of rice, we cook."*

It was not heroism.

It was habit.

We had done it for so long that the action itself had changed us.

Something curious happens when people act repeatedly.

Action creates feedback.

Feedback changes belief.

Belief changes what a person thinks is possible.

And what a person thinks is possible changes the next action.

**Growth is not simply the accumulation of knowledge.**

Sometimes it begins with doing something before you fully believe you are capable of doing it.

---

## **X. What the Porridge Taught Me**

Later, the city fell.

I went south with the refugees.

The magnificent Kaifeng I had known became a memory.

Looking back, I think about those hundreds of pots of porridge.

They taught me something that poetry never had.

Most people do not fail because opportunities never appear.

They fail because their understanding of the world does not update quickly enough.

Before a system collapses, there are usually signals.

Before prices become unbearable, there are usually warnings.

Before war arrives, there are usually footsteps in the distance.

But people prefer familiar explanations.

They protect yesterday's assumptions.

They mistake the absence of visible catastrophe for the absence of catastrophe itself.

**What we cannot imagine, we are often reluctant to believe.**

But there is an important qualification.

I once thought that people could never act beyond the boundaries of their understanding.

I no longer believe that.

The truth is more subtle.

**A person cannot simply think their way into a world they cannot yet conceive.**

But they can act.

They can observe what happens.

They can receive feedback.

They can discover that their model was incomplete.

And then they can change.

Perhaps that is what growth really is.

Not suddenly becoming wiser.

Not acquiring some magical knowledge from outside oneself.

But allowing reality to tell us:

**"The world is larger than the model you were using to understand it."**

I learned that from a bowl of porridge.

And from the people who came to receive it.

And from the city that could not save itself.

---

### **Afterword**

I do not know whether history will remember me accurately.

Perhaps it will remember the famous courtesan.

Perhaps the poet's muse.

Perhaps the woman who fed the hungry before the fall of Kaifeng.

History has its own memory, and it is rarely the same as a person's own.

But I know what those days meant to me.

I began by believing that I was giving people food.

I ended by realizing that they were giving me something too.

They showed me the boundaries of my own understanding.

They showed me how poverty narrows the horizon of thought.

They showed me how old success can become a prison.

They showed me how words can imitate action.

They showed me how individual kindness can temporarily relieve suffering while remaining powerless against systemic collapse.

And, most importantly, they showed me that cognition is not a wall.

It is a moving boundary.

**We do not transcend our limits by pretending that the limits do not exist.**

**We transcend them by confronting reality, acting, receiving feedback, and allowing ourselves to change.**

The fire burned low that night.

But there was still rice in the pot.

So we kept cooking.
