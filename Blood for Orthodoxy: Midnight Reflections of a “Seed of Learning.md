# **Blood for Orthodoxy: Midnight Reflections of a “Seed of Learning”**

### —Fragments from the Prison Diary of Fang Xiaoru

### **Author’s Note**

This work adopts a historical rear-view perspective and employs a speculative method I call the **“counterfactual self-sampling assumption”**: imagine that, in the final moments of his life, a historical figure is suddenly granted the ability to glimpse the judgments of later generations and all the historical consequences of his actions. From that vantage point, he looks backward upon his own life and confronts the psychological and ethical consequences of his choices.

Written in the first-person diary form, this narrative incorporates concepts from cognitive psychology—particularly **cognitive dissonance theory** and **moral disengagement mechanisms**—to explore Fang Xiaoru’s psychological transformation from “dying for righteousness” to the devastating question: **What becomes of righteousness when others must die for it?**

---

### **20th Day of the Sixth Month, Hongwu Year 35 — Night, Overcast**

Nanjing in June is damp, like a piece of cloth that has been wrung out a hundred times and still refuses to dry.

There is no water clock in the prison. I can only estimate the hour by watching the changing patch of sky above the iron-barred window.

Another prisoner was dragged in today.

Through two walls, I heard him cry out once:

**“Elder Brother.”**

It was Xiaoyou.

My younger brother, who studied beside me from childhood and received the same stern instruction from our father, has now been thrown into this nameless prison. He did not call out a second time.

Perhaps they gagged him.

Or perhaps he simply did not want me to hear his fear.

I leaned against the wall, my back pressed against the cold stone. The chill seeped slowly through my clothes and into my bones. Strangely, it made my mind clearer.

Earlier today, a young scholar from the Hanlin Academy came to deliver a message from the man in the bright-yellow armor—the man whom everyone now calls **His Majesty**.

The young scholar was trembling when he entered. His voice trembled too.

He said:

“His Majesty says that if Master Fang will only write this one imperial edict, all past offenses will be forgiven. Your former office will be restored. Your learning—”

I remember smiling.

It must have been an ugly smile, because he trembled even more.

I asked him:

**“Where is the Jianwen Emperor?”**

He dared not answer.

**“Where is the Prince of Yan?”**

He took a step backward.

**“And where is the Prince of Yan’s younger brother?”**

He almost fled the room.

At that moment, something very familiar rose inside me—the moral certainty of a man who believes himself righteous when confronted by usurpation.

My back seemed to straighten.

Every wrinkle on my face seemed to proclaim the word **righteousness**.

Yao Guangxiao, that black-robed monk, once called me the **“Seed of Learning for All Under Heaven.”**

The phrase spread throughout the court.

I appeared humble whenever it was mentioned.

But secretly, I believed it.

If even this seed refused to sprout, who would remain to defend the last inch of moral order for the Jianwen court?

And yet tonight, now, when no sound comes from beyond that wall, another thought has entered my mind like a rat and begun gnawing at me:

**Is Xiaoyou dead too?**

---

### **21st Day of the Sixth Month — Rain**

It is raining.

Water is pouring through the slanted opening above the iron window and collecting in a shallow puddle of mud on the floor.

I looked down at it.

In the water was reflected the face of a stranger—unkempt beard and hair, hollow eyes, high cheekbones.

Is this me?

Is this the Fang Xiaoru whom **Song Lian** once called a phoenix among a hundred birds?

Is this the man whose writings were praised throughout the realm, whose former student, the Prince of Shu, personally inscribed the words **“Orthodox Learning”**?

Is this the same Fang Xiaoru whose essays were copied and recited by scholars across the empire?

I suddenly remembered my father.

My father, **Fang Keqin**, had served as prefect of Jining. He was incorruptible and loved the people as his own children. Yet he eventually died unjustly in prison during the notorious **Empty Seal Case**.

How old was I then?

Twenty-three?

Twenty-four?

I remember only the journey home with his coffin.

People lined the road, kneeling and weeping as we passed.

That was when I made a vow:

*A man who serves in office must serve his ruler by the right path.*

*A scholar must establish his character through integrity.*

After my father died, I studied under Master Song Lian.

He was the great literary authority of his age, whom the Hongwu Emperor himself called **“the foremost of the founding dynasty’s civil officials.”**

He taught me to read.

He taught me to write.

More importantly, he taught me how to live.

He used to say:

> “The way of learning begins with investigating principle; the key to investigating principle lies in reading.”

And:

> **“What makes a scholar worthy is integrity.”**

He died too.

During the investigations surrounding the Hu Weiyong case, his grandson Song Shen was implicated. Song Lian himself was exiled to Maozhou and died on the journey.

When I heard the news, I could not eat for three days.

This was the world I inherited—a world where **despotism and political legitimacy were intertwined**.

Zhu Yuanzhang cleansed his dynasty with blood.

The Jianwen Emperor attempted to heal those wounds through benevolent government.

And I, Fang Xiaoru, was the man who put that benevolent rule into words, giving it intellectual legitimacy.

I believed I stood on the side of Heaven's principle.

I believed I embodied the **Dao** itself.

But tonight, as rain strikes the iron window, each drop sounds like someone knocking at the door.

Or asking:

**Fang Xiaoru, what gives you the right?**

---

### **22nd Day of the Sixth Month — The Rain Continues**

When the jailer brought my food today, he looked at me for a long moment.

There was sympathy in his eyes.

And confusion.

Perhaps he was wondering:

What does this scholar actually want?

The Emperor's throne is already secure.

All Fang has to do is lower his head, dip his brush in ink, write a few hundred characters, and walk out of this prison alive.

Forget wealth and glory.

At the very least, he could save his family.

But the jailer does not understand.

If I were truly to write that edict, then all the books I have read throughout my life would become meaningless.

As a child I was called **“Little Han Yu.”**

I read one measure of text every day, and the villagers marveled.

Later I followed Master Song Lian, studying alongside the finest scholars of the realm.

My essays were said to be circulated throughout the empire whenever they appeared.

I compiled the *Xunzhi Studio Collection*, constantly invoking the governance of the ancient Three Dynasties and the teachings of Confucius and the Duke of Zhou.

I urged the Jianwen Emperor to reduce the power of the feudal princes and restore the ancient well-field system, believing that the realm could be governed through benevolence and righteousness.

**The Jianwen Emperor.**

That gentle young man.

He trusted me.

Because he trusted me, he appointed me.

Because he appointed me, I believed I owed him my life.

This was the Confucian ideal of loyalty:

> “When the ruler treats the minister with courtesy, the minister serves the ruler with loyalty.”

I had lived inside those words for forty-six years.

They were no longer an argument.

They were my breath.

My blood.

The foundation of who I believed myself to be.

And yet, in a dream, I seemed to hear the name of a scholar from a distant future—**Hu Shi**.

He once described me as a man who **“sacrificed his life for his ideals.”**

It is not wrong.

And yet it is too easy.

What is an ideal?

An ideal is the moon hanging in the sky. I look up at it and imagine that all suffering beneath it has meaning.

But when that moonlight shines down and illuminates **873 faces**, can I still look upward without shame?

**Eight hundred and seventy-three people.**

That is the number the jailer accidentally revealed today.

He spoke quietly, almost to himself:

“I heard the list fills several pages. From Ninghai to Nanjing, they arrested everyone. Your fellow students, your disciples—even people who merely heard you lecture…”

He stopped.

I did not ask him to continue.

I simply sat in the puddle of muddy water, motionless, like a piece of rotten wood.

---

### **23rd Day of the Sixth Month — Overcast**

I dreamed.

I returned to the courtyard of our old home in Ninghai.

Under the locust tree, Mother was sewing shoes.

Sunlight filtered through the leaves in fragments, like scattered gold.

Xiaoyou was reciting the *Analects*:

> **“A man of purpose and a man of virtue do not seek life at the expense of benevolence; they will sacrifice their lives to preserve it.”**

I walked toward Mother.

I wanted to touch her hand.

But my hand passed straight through her body.

She could neither see nor hear me.

She simply sat there, sewing one stitch after another, murmuring:

“Xiaoru has always been too stubborn. I fear that one day his stubbornness will bring him trouble…”

I woke violently.

My face was wet.

I do not know whether it was rain or tears.

Mother, you were right.

I was too rigid.

**What is too rigid will break.**

Yet every book of the sages you taught me to read seemed to say the opposite:

*Better to break than to bend.*

Mencius spoke of **“sacrificing life for righteousness.”**

Wen Tianxiang spoke of leaving behind a loyal heart that would illuminate history.

I believed I was following the sages.

I believed I was setting an example for every scholar under Heaven.

But an example—

**must it be painted red with the blood of others?**

I remembered my disciple **Liao Yong**.

He was young, humble, and talented.

Three years ago, when he became my student, I placed my hand on his shoulder and told him:

“A scholar must take the whole world as his responsibility.”

He looked at me with admiration.

Now his name is on that list.

And **Lin Jiayou, Zheng Gongzhi, Lu Yuanzhi**…

Their names rise one by one in my mind like nails driven into my chest.

They were my friends.

My students.

My fellow townsmen.

Some had merely heard me lecture.

Some had written letters discussing scholarship with me.

Some had done nothing more than say at a banquet:

“Master Fang is right.”

**Did they deserve to die?**

---

### **24th Day of the Sixth Month — Partly Clear**

Today I seemed to hear the sounds of the execution ground.

Not truly.

Only in my imagination.

My mind has become a storyteller without restraint, endlessly staging a massacre that has not yet occurred.

I see the executioner's blade rise.

Fall.

Rise.

Fall.

I see familiar faces lying in pools of blood, eyes still open, lips moving faintly as if asking:

**“Master, why?”**

Why?

I have asked myself that question for three days.

I have no answer.

The historian **Cai Dongfan** later wrote of me:

> **“Fang Xiaoru was merely a pedantic Confucian scholar.”**

He argued that my strategies for the Jianwen Emperor were neither useful nor successful; that refusing to draft the imperial proclamation could still be regarded as loyal, but that my death should have been enough to repay my former ruler. Why provoke the Prince of Yan further and thereby bring destruction upon ten generations? What crime had those people committed?

**Pedantic scholar.**

Two words.

Like a slap across the face.

I was pedantic.

During the Jianwen Emperor's final years, none of my strategies succeeded—from the reduction of the feudal princes to the mobilization of troops.

I urged the Emperor not to release Zhu Di's three sons.

He refused.

I advised him to defend Nanjing and await reinforcements.

He did not follow that either.

I was learned.

But my learning was useless on the battlefield.

I could write beautiful prose.

I could not write a strategy capable of preserving a dynasty.

But does being pedantic deserve death?

And even if it does—

does it deserve dragging **873 people** into death with me?

Again I thought of the proclamation I refused to write.

What if I had written it?

What if I had lowered my head?

Would all those people still be alive?

Would Xiaoyou still be reciting the *Analects*?

Would Liao Yong still be writing?

No.

I cannot think this way.

This is weakness.

Hesitation.

Betrayal of the sages.

And yet—

**is the Way of the sages truly a path that requires us to walk toward immortality over the corpses of our own families?**

---

### **25th Day of the Sixth Month — Clear**

Today the jailer told me that the list was final.

**Eight hundred and seventy-three people.**

I was silent for a long time.

Then I asked:

“Xiaoyou… Fang Xiaoyou. Is he on it?”

He nodded.

I asked again:

“My wife, Zheng… my two sons…”

His eyes shifted away.

“Your wife and your second son… on the day the city fell, they… **hanged themselves**.

Your two daughters… **threw themselves into the Qinhuai River**.”

I said nothing.

After the jailer left, I curled myself into the corner and held my knees like an abandoned child.

I had succeeded in becoming a “loyal minister.”

But the title had been purchased with the lives of my entire family.

My wife.

My children.

My brother.

My nephews.

My students.

My friends.

They had all become sacrifices upon the altar of my **loyalty**.

Then I remembered something.

In the third year of the Jianwen reign, Zhu Di's forces had already reached Shandong.

The court was in panic.

Some urged the Emperor to move the capital.

I opposed them.

I said:

> **“The Son of Heaven must defend the ancestral state.”**

The Emperor listened to me.

He stayed.

Later, when Jinan was in danger, Tiexuan defended the city.

I wrote proclamations calling upon the realm to rise in defense of the throne.

At the time, I believed myself an embodiment of justice.

Every sentence sounded righteous.

Every character seemed to carry the weight of moral truth.

But I never asked:

The men I called upon to fight—

did they also have parents?

Wives?

Children?

When they died, who would mourn them?

I simply sat in the court and used my brush to write beautiful words.

---

### **26th Day of the Sixth Month — Night, Stars**

There are stars tonight.

Through the narrow opening above the iron bars, I can see several faint points of light.

They flicker in the darkness, weak yet stubborn, as if trying to say something.

I remembered a monk named **Yao Guangxiao**.

He was Zhu Di's strategist, known as the **“Black-robed Chancellor.”**

Before Zhu Di set out from Beijing, Yao Guangxiao reportedly warned him:

> **“When you reach the city walls, he will certainly refuse to surrender. Whatever you do, do not kill him. If Fang Xiaoru dies, the seed of learning for all under Heaven will be extinguished.”**

**The Seed of Learning for All Under Heaven.**

That title came from him.

Yet now this seed has not merely failed to sprout.

It has **dragged an entire garden of plants into ruin**.

The people destroyed because they were connected to me—

their lives,

their talents,

their dreams—

all were uprooted and burned to ash.

What am I?

**Was I a good teacher?**

My students died because they studied with me.

**Was I a good brother?**

My brother was dragged toward execution because he shared my blood.

**Was I a good father?**

My children chose suicide because they bore the surname Fang.

I was merely a good minister.

A good minister loyal to the Jianwen Emperor.

Loyal to orthodoxy.

Loyal to the “Dao” inside my own mind.

But was that Dao truly the Way of Heaven?

Or merely my own obsession?

Hu Shi was right.

I sacrificed my life for an ideal.

But I finally understand something:

**The person who dies for righteousness is often not the person who suffers most.**

The greatest suffering belongs to those who are forced to die because someone else has chosen to be righteous.

They did not want to become martyrs.

**They only wanted to live.**

---

### **27th Day of the Sixth Month — Clear**

An unexpected visitor came today.

A eunuch of Zhu Di's court.

He brought a flask of wine and a small dish of food.

“His Majesty says that if Master changes his mind…”

I did not look at him.

Nor did I look at the food.

I simply stared at the patch of dampness on the floor.

It had dried and become wet again, dried and become wet again, leaving rings upon the blue bricks like the rings of a tree.

“Please thank His Majesty for his kindness,” I said.

**“My decision is final.”**

The eunuch left.

I know this was my last refusal.

The next visitor will not be a eunuch.

He will be an executioner.

Strangely, now that death is truly approaching, I am no longer afraid.

Perhaps because I have already died many times inside.

Every time I think of those who died because of me,

I die again.

The writer **Lu Xun** later described me as possessing a kind of **“Taizhou-style hard spirit.”**

The hardness was real.

But what lay beneath it?

Stubbornness.

Arrogance.

The belief that I myself embodied the Dao.

The belief that anyone who rejected my interpretation of the Dao was no more than an insect.

I forgot something:

Those “insects” were human beings.

They had fears.

Attachments.

Families.

And the right to live.

I picked up a twig from the floor and carved a line into the wall:

> **“Fang Xiaoru, a native of Ninghai, died in the sixth month of Hongwu Year 35.”**

After writing it, I felt that something was wrong.

Not:

**“He died for his crimes.”**

But:

**“He died because of me.”**

---

### **28th Day of the Sixth Month — Dawn**

Morning has come.

This will probably be the last day.

I looked again at the words on the wall.

Then I wiped away the phrase **“He died because of me.”**

I carved a new sentence:

> **“Fang Xiaoru, a native of Ninghai, studied the Way of the sages and lived according to the righteousness of a gentleman. He lived without shame before Heaven, yet with shame before humanity.”**

The jailer came.

His expression was strange.

There was admiration in it.

Confusion.

And perhaps—

pity.

“Master. It is time.”

I stood.

My legs were numb, but I tried to walk steadily.

When I passed Xiaoyou's cell, I stopped.

There was no one inside.

Only several dark-red stains on the floor.

I said nothing.

I continued walking.

I was going to attend a banquet called **Loyalty**.

There would be **873 guests**.

They had not come voluntarily.

**I had invited them.**

The sunlight was painfully bright.

I narrowed my eyes.

At the execution ground, a dense crowd had gathered.

I heard weeping.

Not one person's weeping.

Many voices—

men,

women,

children—

all intertwined like a requiem without words.

Then I remembered the four characters I had written in the court:

**“The Yan rebel usurped the throne.”**

When I wrote those words, my hand did not tremble.

My heart did not hesitate.

I believed those four characters represented Heaven's justice.

I believed they represented righteousness.

I believed they embodied the backbone of the Confucian scholar.

But standing here now, hearing those cries, I finally understood:

**Every one of those four characters had been written in human blood.**

The blood of Xiaoyou formed the character **Yan**.

The blood of Liao Yong formed the character **rebel**.

The blood of my wife and children formed the character **usurped**.

And the blood of **873 innocent people** formed the character **throne**.

---

# **Epilogue**

Fang Xiaoru was executed by dismemberment outside Jubaomen Gate in Nanjing at the age of forty-six.

The *Chronological History of the Ming* records:

> “After the nine kinship groups had been exterminated, he still refused to yield. His friends and disciples, including Liao Yong and Lin Jiayou, were then included as another group and punished as well. An imperial order was subsequently issued for him to be dismembered in the marketplace. Eight hundred and seventy-three people were executed, while countless others died after being exiled to remote frontier garrisons.”

Later historians have called him a **loyal minister**.

Others condemned him as a **pedantic Confucian**.

Some said he **“died as he ought to have died.”**

Others argued that he **“deserved even more than death.”**

But on the final page of this diary, I want to quote the words of an unknown writer—a voice I saw in a dream, seemingly coming from a distant future:

> **“Fang Xiaoru was an idealist. He was loyal to the Jianwen Emperor and loyal to his beliefs… In truth, he was simply a tragic man, an idealist who walked the road of faith all the way to death.”**

An **idealist**.

Yes.

I was an idealist.

But perhaps the greatest tragedy of idealism is not that the ideal collapses.

It is that, at the moment the ideal is fulfilled, one discovers that one has been standing upon the bones of others.

If there is another life, I do not want to be the **“Seed of Learning for All Under Heaven.”**

I only want to be an ordinary man beneath the locust tree in Ninghai, listening to the sound of my mother sewing shoes.

If there is another life, I will never again say:

**“What does it matter if they execute ten generations of my family?”**

I will say:

**“Let them go. Let all the blame fall upon me alone.”**

But there is no next life.

The drums at the execution ground begin to sound.

I raise my head.

The sun breaks through the clouds, radiant and immense.

Its light falls upon my face.

Warm.

Like the palm of my mother's hand.

I close my eyes.

## **Afterword: A Question for the Modern Reader**

The story of Fang Xiaoru has been debated for more than six centuries.

Some praise his loyalty and righteousness.

Some condemn his obstinacy.

Some mourn his fate.

Others defend him.

But imagine that you were standing on that execution ground.

Facing those **873 lives**.

**What would you choose?**

This is not ultimately a choice between “loyalty” and “treachery.”

It is a choice between **idealism and human life**.

When your ideal requires the lives of others as its price—

**do you still have the right to call yourself a soldier for justice?**

Fang Xiaoru never answered that question before he died.

Perhaps each of us must answer it once, silently, within ourselves.
