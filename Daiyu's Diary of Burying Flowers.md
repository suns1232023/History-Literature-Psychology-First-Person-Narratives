# Daiyu's Diary of Burying Flowers

## My Uncontrollable War with Grief

### A First-Person Exploration of the “Kill Line” and the Prison of Emotion

**Keywords:** [#Dream of the Red Chamber#](https://xueqiu.com/k?q=%23%E7%BA%A2%E6%A5%BC%E6%A2%A6%23) · [#Culture & Media#](https://xueqiu.com/k?q=%23%E6%96%87%E5%8C%96%E4%BC%A0%E5%AA%92%23)

## 📄 Preface to the Diary

**The 43rd Year of Qianlong · Spring · The 26th Day of the Fourth Month**

Today is Mangzhong, the season when the Flower Goddess relinquishes her throne and the flowers begin to fall.

I carried a flower hoe and a silk pouch and walked alone toward the bridge by Qinfang Sluice.

There was a faint smell of damp decay in the wind, mixed with the fragrance of fallen petals. It reminded me painfully of my own heart:

> **Damp. Decaying. And yet still carrying a small, stubborn warmth that refuses to die.**

Everyone tells me that I am *too sensitive*. They tell me to *let things go*, to *think more positively*.

But they do not understand.

**Grief is like a vine wrapped around the bones. The harder you struggle, the tighter it becomes. Everyone may tell me to control my sorrow—except myself.**

Lately I have often thought about the phrase *“the kill line.”*

*(Note: the term was introduced earlier in Jiao Da's diary as a metaphor for a social boundary.)*

They say I represent the beautiful women who stand *above* the kill line.

But what does *above* mean to me?

It is no more substantial than a reflection of flowers in a mirror.

An orphan living under another family's roof. A body that is constantly ill. A love I can never quite hold.

Where is the so-called *above*?

There is only an invisible line that traps me inside a strange prison:

**I appear privileged, yet I possess almost no power over my own life.**

Today I bury flowers.

But what am I really burying?

The flowers?

My fate?

My sorrow?

Or this uncontrollable life of mine, drifting away like fallen petals?

# 🌸 I. Fallen Flowers

## The Mirror of Fate and the Switch of Emotion

**Third Hour · By Qinfang Sluice**

The moment I reached the bridge, I saw the ground covered with fallen blossoms.

Peach. Apricot. Pear.

Layer upon layer of red petals covered the stone pavement, as though someone had overturned a box of rouge.

When the wind rose, the petals spun into the folds of my skirt. They clung to the damp, cold fabric.

They reminded me of the drops of blood my mother once coughed onto my handkerchief.

> “Miss Lin, burying flowers again?”

It was Little Red, one of the maids.

She was carrying a tea tray. She glanced at me, curled her lips, and walked away.

I heard her whispering to another maid:

> “She buries flowers every day. What's the point? Flowers bloom again after they wither. But when a person gets sick, there may be no coming back.”

### 🧠 Psychological Note

Little Red's words were like a needle piercing my fantasy that I could somehow *control grief* by burying flowers.

According to **Social Comparison Theory** (Festinger, 1954), people evaluate themselves partly by comparing their circumstances with those of others.

Little Red's perspective as a healthy person collided with my reality as someone chronically ill. The contrast triggered a form of **relative deprivation** (Stouffer et al., 1949).

Even though I am technically a young lady of the Jia household, I still feel deprived of something more fundamental:

**the basic right to live in a healthy body.**

That deprivation becomes the switch that turns my grief on.

I crouched down and brushed my fingers across a peach petal.

Its edges had already turned yellow.

Like the teeth of an old person coming loose.

Suddenly I remembered reading *The Romance of the Western Chamber* yesterday:

> *“Flowers fall, red petals drift upon the water, and countless idle sorrows remain.”*

At the time, I thought the line merely beautiful.

Now I understand it.

**The decay of flowers can be seen. The decay of a human life is silent.**

My grief began with that silent decay.

### The “Kill Line” as Metaphor

For me, the kill line is not really about social rank.

It is the invisible boundary imposed upon someone who is merely a **guest in another person's world**.

I may be a young lady of the Rongguo household, but I have neither parents to protect me nor a family foundation of my own.

That line separates me from those who are considered *truly one of the family*.

Grandmother Jia's affection feels like something granted.

Baoyu's devotion feels like a stroke of improbable luck.

Even the maids can casually discuss my illness.

As **Social Identity Theory** (Tajfel, 1979) suggests, when a person cannot securely belong to a stable in-group, their sense of self can gradually become unstable.

# 🧰 II. The Flower Hoe and Silk Pouch

## Vessels of Memory and the Illusion of Control

**Early in the Fourth Hour · Hoe and Pouch in Hand**

I carried the flower hoe toward the flower grave.

I brought this hoe from Suzhou last year.

Its wooden handle has been polished smooth by use, much like the calluses in my palm.

The silk pouch was a gift from Baoyu.

It is made of plain white satin, embroidered with several sprigs of ink-black bamboo.

He once told me:

> “It is perfect for carrying fallen flowers.”

But today the flowers seemed heavier than they had in previous years.

**Not because the flowers were heavier. Because my heart was.**

As I passed Liao-xu Pavilion, I heard several older women talking.

> “Miss Lin has lost her mind again. Perfectly good flowers, and she insists on burying them. Who knows whether she's being delicate or truly going mad.”

Another replied:

> “I heard she coughed blood again yesterday. The old lady is keeping it from the master. She's afraid he'll be displeased.”

### 🧠 Psychological Note

Their comments activated what psychologists call the **spotlight effect** (Gilovich, Medvec, & Savitsky, 2000)—the feeling that one's actions and appearance are being watched and judged by everyone else.

That feeling intensifies **self-monitoring anxiety** (Carver & Scheier, 1981).

I tried to use the ritual of burying flowers to impose some order upon my grief.

But the more deliberately I tried to control it, the more conscious I became of how others saw me.

This resembles the failure of certain **emotion-regulation strategies** described by Gross (1998): when a person tries too hard to suppress or perform control over an emotion, the emotion may return with even greater force.

I touched the ink bamboo embroidered on the pouch.

It reminded me of the first time I buried flowers.

I had only recently arrived at the Jia household then.

Baochai had sent me a box of bird's-nest soup and told me:

> “You are so frail, little sister. You should nourish yourself.”

I thanked her.

Then, when no one was looking, I poured it into the river.

**It was not because I despised the gift. I was afraid of owing someone something.**

Back then, I believed that as long as I owed no one a favor, I could preserve my own *purity*.

Now I understand:

**Who in this world can truly live without owing anyone anything?**

### The Kill Line Made Tangible

The flower hoe and silk pouch were supposed to be instruments through which I could control fate.

Instead, they became physical symbols of the kill line.

The hoe can bury fallen flowers.

It cannot bury the fact that I live under another family's roof.

The pouch can hold fallen petals.

It cannot hold the loneliness of having neither father nor mother.

This is the fundamental mismatch between **the tool and the problem**.

And perhaps that is where my uncontrollable grief begins.

I believe I can control my emotions.

In reality,

**I am the prisoner of those emotions.**

# 📜 III. *The Song of the Burial of Flowers*

## Existential Loneliness and the Question of Being

**Noon · The Song Is Written**

I chose a patch of earth sheltered from the wind and began to dig.

The soil was soft, mixed with the smell of decaying leaves.

It reminded me of my mother's coffin.

With every handful of earth, another line of poetry came to me.

Before I realized it, I was reciting aloud:

> *Flowers fade, flowers scatter, filling the sky;*
> *When their color fades and fragrance dies, who will mourn them?*
> *...*
> *Three hundred and sixty days in a year,*
> *Yet wind and frost, like blades, press hard against me.*

When I reached the words *wind and frost*, my throat suddenly tightened.

What are these *blades of wind and frost*?

The cold eyes of everyone in the Jia household?

The marriage between Baoyu and Baochai that everyone calls a perfect match?

Or my own lungs, growing weaker with every passing day?

**Existential psychology** (Yalom, 1980) argues that awareness of mortality can generate existential anxiety.

For me, that anxiety has long since seeped into every ordinary moment of life.

I continued writing:

> *Born pure, I shall return pure,*
> *Better than falling into the mud of the gutter.*

When I finished those words, my tears fell onto the petals and darkened them.

I remembered my quarrel with Baoyu a few days ago.

He had said:

> “Why are you always so pessimistic, Lin Mei-mei?”

I threw down my handkerchief and ran away.

The truth is, I am not pessimistic.

**I am simply too painfully aware.**

I know that I may not live long.

I know that Baoyu may eventually marry someone else.

I know that the splendor of this Grand View Garden is no more permanent than flowers reflected in a mirror.

### 🧠 Psychological Note

The composition of *The Song of the Burial of Flowers* can be understood as a form of **emotional externalization** (Pennebaker, 1997).

By transforming abstract sorrow into poetry, I try to give meaning to suffering.

Yet the act of expression may also intensify the emotion.

When I describe myself as someone who must remain *pure*, I simultaneously acknowledge the possibility of falling into the *mud*.

The ideal self—pure, dignified, untouched—collides with the actual self:

* physically fragile,
* socially dependent,
* emotionally insecure.

As described by **Cognitive Dissonance Theory** (Festinger, 1957), the conflict between the ideal self and the lived self can generate profound psychological tension.

Sometimes the mind resolves that tension not by changing reality, but by turning against itself.

### The Kill Line Becomes Internal

The *wind and frost* in the poem become an internalized version of the kill line.

External pressures—the rules of the Jia household, my physical limitations, the uncertainty of love—are transformed into weapons turned inward.

Perhaps I begin to think:

> *Maybe I am the problem.*

This is close to what psychologists describe as **internalized shame** (Tangney et al., 1996).

And so a cycle begins:

**The more I suffer, the more I believe I deserve to suffer.**

**The more I believe I deserve it, the harder it becomes to stop suffering.**

# 💔 IV. Meeting Baoyu

## The Sweetness of Attachment and the Terror of Losing It

**Late in the Eighth Hour · Baoyu Comes Looking for Me**

Just as I was burying the final handful of earth, I heard footsteps behind me.

I turned.

It was Baoyu.

He was out of breath and carrying my glass embroidered-ball lantern.

> “Lin Mei-mei, what are you doing here? Grandmother is looking for you.”

He crouched beside me and tried to wipe away my tears.

I moved away.

I held the silk pouch tightly against my chest.

> “Did you see everything?”

He nodded.

> “I heard you reciting the poem. I knew you must be here.”

There was a long silence.

Then he said:

> “Don't be afraid. I'll always be with you.”

### 🧠 Psychological Note

Baoyu's arrival activates my **ambivalent attachment** (Hazan & Shaver, 1987).

On one hand, his promise of companionship activates the attachment system's idea of a **secure base** (Bowlby, 1969).

For a moment, I feel safe.

But at the same time, I fear that very dependence.

**The deeper the attachment, the more uncontrollable the pain of losing it becomes.**

His words—*“I'll always be with you”*—remind me of what my mother once told me:

> *“I will always protect you.”*

But what happened afterward?

I pointed toward the flower grave.

> “Look at those flowers. How beautiful they were when they bloomed. Once they wither, they can only be buried. People are the same. No matter how beautiful a season is, it must eventually end.”

Baoyu became anxious.

> “Don't say such gloomy things! You will get better. We'll always be together.”

### The Kill Line Begins to Crack

Baoyu's presence creates a crack in my kill line.

If I am supposed to be one of the beautiful women *above* the line, someone admired from a distance, then why do I become something else in front of Baoyu?

A girl begging for love.

This reversal of power makes my sorrow even more intense.

The more deeply I love him,

the more terrified I become of losing him.

The more terrified I become of losing him,

the more meaningless my beauty and talent begin to feel.

**Self-Determination Theory** (Deci & Ryan, 1985) proposes that human well-being depends partly on the satisfaction of fundamental psychological needs, including autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

My grief comes from the collision of two needs I cannot reconcile:

**I long to belong to someone, yet I desperately want control over my own fate.**

I can have neither.

# 🕯️ V. Dusk

## The Uncontrollable Nature of Grief and the Emptiness of Existence

**Late Afternoon · Sitting Alone on the Way Home**

By the time I finished burying the flowers, the sun was already sinking.

I sat on the steps of Xiaoxiang Hall and watched the evening light stretch my shadow across the ground.

Zijuan brought me a cloak.

I did not refuse.

**I am used to being cold. One more layer or one less seems hardly to matter.**

Before leaving, Baoyu had slipped a packet of pine-nut candy into my hand.

> “Eat this. It will make you feel better.”

I unwrapped one piece and put it into my mouth.

It was almost painfully sweet.

Like his promise that we would *always be together*.

Suddenly I remembered that my mother used to love pine-nut candy too.

When I was young, I thought it was too sweet.

Now I understand how much unspeakable bitterness can hide inside sweetness.

### 🧠 Psychological Note

The contrast between the sweetness of the candy and the bitterness of memory illustrates an **emotional contrast effect** (Andrade, 2005).

I try to use sweetness to soothe sorrow.

Instead, the taste awakens an older layer of grief.

This is the cumulative nature of sorrow:

**One grief has not yet healed when another arrives.**

As research on **prolonged grief** suggests (Prigerson et al., 2009), unresolved grief can become increasingly persistent and interfere with a person's ability to process loss.

I touched the silk pouch.

There were still several petals inside.

They had dried completely.

They were as fragile as paper.

And suddenly I understood:

**Burying flowers cannot save flowers.**

Just as comforting words cannot necessarily save me.

Everyone can tell me:

> *Don't be sad.*

> *Try to think differently.*

> *Let it go.*

But they do not understand.

My sorrow is not merely an emotion.

It has become part of my body.

It is the blood I cough from my lungs.

The cold sweat of sleepless nights.

The sharp contraction in my chest whenever I see Baoyu speaking with Baochai.

### The Ultimate Metaphor of the Kill Line

My uncontrollable grief is ultimately an expression of the kill line's complete denial of my agency.

I cannot choose my birth.

I cannot choose the fact that I am a guest in this household.

I cannot control my body.

I cannot decide whom I will marry.

I cannot determine whether my love will survive.

And, perhaps most painfully,

**I am not even allowed to grieve in my own way.**

Others tell me to control my sadness.

But beneath those words is another command:

> **Accept the fate that has already been arranged for you.**

This is a form of **existential helplessness**.

As Frankl (1946) argued, human beings can endure profound suffering when they can still locate meaning and agency within it.

But what happens when both are taken away?

I become a prisoner of grief.

And the key to the prison

**was never placed in my own hands.**

## ✍️ Creative Note

This essay takes **Daiyu's flower-burial episode in Chapter 27 of *Dream of the Red Chamber*** as its narrative foundation.

It combines the metaphor of the **“kill line”** with perspectives from existential psychology and social psychology, using a first-person diary format to explore the deeper mechanisms underlying Daiyu's **uncontrollable grief**.

On the surface, Daiyu appears to stand *above* the kill line: beautiful, talented, cherished, and socially privileged.

Yet beneath that appearance, she remains trapped inside the identity of an outsider—a dependent guest whose body, family position, and romantic future are largely beyond her control.

Her grief therefore becomes more than sadness.

It becomes her response to a world in which **belonging, autonomy, health, and love are all uncertain at the same time.**
