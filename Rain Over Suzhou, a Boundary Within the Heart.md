# Rain Over Suzhou, a Boundary Within the Heart

### June 18, 2026 · Suzhou · Rain

It is 11:40 p.m.

The first day of the gaokao is finally over.

And yet I cannot sleep.

The rain is still falling outside.

Slowly.

Steadily.

Almost exactly like the sound my pen made against the answer sheet this morning—the faint, continuous *scratch-scratch* of nib against paper, with just the slightest resistance beneath it.

The sound is still brushing softly against my eardrums.

I have to write this down tonight.

While the memory is still warm.

While the ripples of emotion have not yet completely settled.

This pale-blue diary has accompanied me through three years of high school.

Tonight, perhaps for the first time, it has to carry something worthy of all three years.

---

They call me **Wang Yuyan**, the girl who “knows every martial art under Heaven.”

A walking encyclopedia of martial arts.

My cousin loves to tease me with it.

There is always something behind his tone—part amusement, part something more complicated that I have never quite understood.

But they do not know what it feels like when knowledge accumulates inside your head almost like entropy increasing without limit.

It is suffocating.

Like being trapped inside a room whose four walls are covered with mirrors.

Every reflection knows everything.

Every reflection can recite everything.

And yet none of them is really me.

I know every subtle shift of force in the Eighteen Dragon-Subduing Palms.

I can almost reconstruct the state of mind in which the great Qiao Feng might change his technique halfway through a battle.

But I cannot even bring myself to kill a chicken.

For years, knowledge was not freedom to me.

It was weight.

Disorder.

An ever-expanding burden that constantly dissipated my energy.

That was my **knowledge entropy**.

To everyone else, it was a treasure.

Inside me, it was a maze.

---

Until I decided to take this examination.

No.

More precisely—

until I began trying to connect all those scattered stars into a constellation of my own.

I chose Chinese because, among all the labyrinths of knowledge, only language allowed me to touch something ordered.

Something beautiful enough to inhabit.

It was not like the decisive violence of martial formulas.

It was not like the mysterious abstractions of inner cultivation.

Words—especially Chinese characters—are small, self-contained universes.

In every horizontal stroke, every vertical stroke, every turning brushstroke, there are mountains and rivers.

Affection.

Duty.

Memory.

Meaning.

I chose language.

Or perhaps language chose me.

---

And Suzhou—

the city that gave birth to me and raised me.

Its whitewashed walls.

Its dark tiled roofs.

Its little bridges over flowing water.

Its soft Wu dialect.

They have seeped into my bones.

The camellias of Mantuo Villa may bloom as magnificently as they please.

But they are still only flowers inside a villa.

The rain of Suzhou is different.

It falls upon the entire city.

Today's rain belongs to every rain I have heard in eighteen years.

It has fallen into the lotus ponds of the Humble Administrator's Garden.

Onto the gray flagstones of Pingjiang Road.

Onto the gardenia petals I brushed from the windowsill each morning.

And today, it fell upon the courtyard of the thousand-year-old prefectural academy where I sat my examination.

---

## 6:30 a.m. · The Initial State of Entropy

When the alarm rang, I was dreaming.

In the dream, I stood in the middle of a Scripture Pavilion.

Countless books were flying toward me like snow.

More and more.

Higher and higher.

Until they buried me completely.

I woke with a thin layer of sweat across my forehead.

Outside, the sky had the familiar grayness of the plum-rain season.

The air smelled damp, with the faint earthy scent of wet soil.

This is the smell of Suzhou's rainy season.

Like silk soaked through with water.

Heavy.

Clinging to the skin.

Mother had prepared rice porridge and a few simple side dishes.

Very light.

She was afraid my stomach might be upset.

I drank mechanically.

But inside my head, hundreds of voices were reciting at once:

*The Ode to the Red Cliffs.*

*Free and Easy Wandering.*

*The Memorial on Behalf of My Elder Brother.*

The lines fought to escape.

They collided.

They reverberated.

A great humming filled my skull.

I knew what it was.

Knowledge entropy.

During the final month before the examination, I had almost obsessively crammed every text, commentary, argument, quotation, and essay reference I could find into my head.

Like a miser trying to squeeze an entire martial-arts library into a single room.

Now everything had lost its order.

The information had become a storm.

I could barely hear Mother's gentle voice.

> “Yuyan, relax. Think of it as going to talk with an old friend.”

Before I left, she straightened my collar.

Her fingers trembled slightly.

An old friend?

I smiled bitterly.

This “old friend” carries almost everything I hope for.

And almost everything I fear.

---

On the way to the examination center, the rain began.

Fine.

Persistent.

Suzhou rain is always like this.

Never hurried.

Never dramatic.

It falls with the patient precision of an embroiderer's needle.

I did not open my umbrella.

I let the cool threads of rain touch my face.

I wanted something physical to push back against the invisible disorder inside me.

The car passed along Renmin Road.

The camphor trees on either side bowed beneath the rain.

Now and then, tires sent small sprays of water into the air.

I closed my eyes in the back seat.

And breathed.

Deeply.

This had become a habit over the past six months.

Whenever my “entropy level” became too high, I imagined myself as a sieve.

The chaotic particles of information were sand.

My only task was to let them settle.

Quietly.

Layer by layer.

In order.

I needed **flow**.

My therapist, Dr. Smith, had told me that flow was an “optimal experience”—a state in which consciousness becomes harmonious and ordered.

The opposite of entropy.

He told me:

> “Yuyan, your problem is not that you cannot remember.
> Your problem is that you do not know how to forget.”

Real wisdom, he said, is not accumulation.

It is retrieval.

Not how much you know—

but how much you can summon when you need it.

---

## 7:40 a.m. · The School Gate and the Pan Pond

The car stopped outside the eastern gate of Suzhou High School.

Rain had washed the four characters above the gate:

**智德之门 — The Gate of Wisdom and Virtue.**

They gleamed.

The blue-gray brick walls looked unusually still beneath the rain.

Camphor leaves drooped under the water.

Drops hung from the wisteria trellis.

The surface of the Pan Pond rippled in tiny circles.

One after another.

As though whispering something I could not quite hear.

This was my first time entering this campus.

I had read its history.

I knew that Fan Zhongyan had established a school here.

I knew how many remarkable people had passed through its gates.

But I had never actually walked inside.

Today, as I stepped along the wet stone path toward the examination hall, my feet felt steadier than I expected.

Under the camphor trees, students stood beneath umbrellas reciting their notes.

Their voices were softened by the rain.

They sounded as though they were coming from beneath the surface of the water.

And suddenly I thought:

Perhaps I was not walking into this school.

Perhaps this school—

and the thousand years of culture carried within it—

was walking into me.

For one brief moment, the noise inside my head became quiet.

Like a lake disturbed by wind becoming still again.

---

My examination room was the third classroom on the third floor of the Science Building.

Outside the window stood a plane tree whose branches nearly touched the sill.

Its leaves had been washed bright green by the rain.

The desks were old, dark yellow wood.

Someone from an earlier generation had carved a single character into the corner:

**静 — Stillness.**

I ran my fingertip over it.

It felt like receiving a silent blessing.

The invigilator read the rules without expression.

Her voice seemed to come through a layer of water.

Then the answer sheets were distributed.

The smell of fresh ink carried the peculiar authority of industrial civilization—

impersonal.

precise.

undeniable.

I inhaled deeply.

As though I were sealing this entire moment into memory:

**June 18, 2026.
Suzhou High School.
Science Building.
Rain against the plane trees.**

---

## 8:30 a.m. · The Ritual of Entropy Reduction

The announcement came over the loudspeaker:

> “The examination begins.”

At that instant, every noise inside my head disappeared.

All the fragments of knowledge.

All the competing voices.

Silenced.

There was only the sound of pages turning—

*flap, rustle, turn*—

and the scratch of pens against paper.

*Scratch.*

*Scratch.*

The sounds merged with the rain outside.

I could no longer tell which was which.

My ritual of entropy reduction began.

I did not answer immediately.

I closed my eyes.

For three seconds, I constructed an empty white room inside my mind.

Dr. Smith had taught me this.

His “cognitive clearing” method.

Then I opened my eyes.

The first modern-text passage was waiting.

It was an essay about the disappearance of handwriting in the digital age.

The author seemed to be a calligrapher, someone nostalgic for brush, ink, paper, and stone.

The essay itself was not extraordinary.

But the questions were clever.

Three months ago, I would have immediately activated everything stored inside my head about technology and humanity, tradition and modernity.

I would have fired arguments like a machine gun.

And then—

information overload.

Missing the question.

Missing the point.

Not today.

Today I became a patient chess player.

First the question.

Then the text.

I imagined myself as the author.

I tried to feel the hesitation and certainty behind every sentence.

He wrote:

> “The instant the brush touches paper is the first kiss between the heart and the material world.”

I trembled slightly.

For a moment, I felt as though I had once left the same kind of first kiss upon rice paper in Mantuo Villa.

Then I stepped back into the role of reader.

I dismantled the traps in his argument.

The rhetorical devices I knew by heart.

The methods of reasoning.

The structures of persuasion.

They were no longer isolated pieces of information.

They had become extensions of my perception.

I was no longer memorizing them.

I was **using** them.

Like finally understanding that *Lingbo Weibu*—the famed “Wave-Treading Steps”—was not merely a sequence of movements.

It was a way of understanding space and speed.

When I finished the first major section, I realized I had not used a single prepared template.

The answers had grown naturally from the text itself.

The feeling was extraordinary.

Like a blocked river suddenly finding a narrow channel.

The water began to move.

For the first time, my knowledge entropy was decreasing through orderly expression.

Outside, droplets slipped from the plane-tree leaves.

One occasionally struck the glass.

*Tap.*

I did not look up.

But I knew.

The school was keeping time for me.

---

## 10:00 a.m. · The First Peak: Resonance with *Xiangjixuan Zhi*

The real challenge came with the classical Chinese section.

The passage was unexpected.

Not the familiar historical biography or political essay.

It was an excerpt from Gui Youguang's **Xiangjixuan Zhi**, paired with another short piece from the Ming dynasty.

When I saw those three characters—

**项脊轩**

—I felt something move inside me.

It is a text about home.

And memory.

About tiny, ordinary things filled with warmth and quiet sorrow.

A mother knocking at the door:

> “Are you cold, my child? Do you want something to eat?”

A grandmother's instructions.

A wife's loquat tree.

When I read it in the past, I thought it was simply restrained prose carrying an inexhaustible sadness.

But now—

in this particular place,

on this particular morning,

when I remembered Mother's slightly trembling hands as she served me porridge,

when I remembered the osmanthus cake she had quietly slipped into my pencil case last night—

homemade,

sweet without being cloying—

the words written centuries ago suddenly came alive.

They had temperature.

They had a heartbeat.

---

The question asked us to compare how the two texts expressed emotion.

I put down my pen.

I did not answer immediately.

For ten seconds, I allowed myself to drift.

I thought of my mother.

I thought of Mantuo Villa—

the place I could never truly return to.

Those beautiful, enclosed years filled with flowers, books, rivalry, calculation, and silence had also been my own **Xiangjixuan**.

There, I had only books.

The books were my shield.

And my prison.

But now, sitting in this classroom in the Science Building, beneath the gaze of this plane tree, I was using the wisdom contained in those books to understand the meaning of *home* in another book.

It was a strange closed loop.

Gui Youguang read, remembered his mother, mourned his wife in Xiangjixuan.

I read, remembered my mother, and longed for home in an examination room in Suzhou.

Five hundred years separated us.

But we faced the same question:

**How can words give shelter to love that has nowhere else to go?**

---

When I picked up my pen again, the answer flowed almost by itself.

I did not compare rhetorical devices.

I did not compare sentence patterns.

I wrote:

> “Gui Youguang's sorrow and joy are entrusted to an image—the loquat tree standing in the courtyard. His emotion is inward, sedimented, moving alongside time. The other text is more outward, more argumentative in its lyricism. One is like deep water flowing beneath a still surface; the other like waves striking the riverbank. Neither is superior. The difference lies only in whether the reader's heart is capable of resonance.”

I do not know whether it was the standard answer.

But it was the most truthful answer I could give after speaking with the author across five centuries.

When I wrote the final character, a small, pleasant tremor moved from my fingertips toward my heart.

Dr. Smith had told me that this was one sign of flow.

When challenge and ability reach a delicate balance,

when consciousness becomes completely absorbed in the present action,

the self begins to disappear.

Time bends.

And something enormous and quietly abundant rises within you.

I think—

for a moment—

I had touched the edge of it.

---

## 11:15 a.m. · The Essay: My Eighteen Dragon-Subduing Palms

Finally—

the essay.

The part I had looked forward to most.

And feared most.

The National Paper I prompt presented a passage about **words**.

Words are vessels for expressing thought and emotion.

They are also windows through which we observe changes in society.

The world is changing.

The times are changing.

History is changing.

And young people are always becoming new.

During your growth, which word has changed in meaning for you?

How has that change marked your growth?

What special meaning does it now hold?

---

“Words.”

That was what the prompt said.

But for me, it was more than a word.

My entire understanding of the martial-arts world had been changing.

I put down my pen.

Closed my eyes.

The rain outside grew distant.

Very distant.

As though it were falling in another world.

My thoughts drifted back to Mantuo Villa.

Back to all those years bent over books.

I remembered the first time I encountered the character:

**侠 — xia.**

My cousin Murong Fu had taught it to me.

He said *xia* meant using force against injustice.

Rescuing people from danger.

Supporting those who could not support themselves.

I believed him.

I thought *xia* meant power.

Martial skill.

The Eighteen Dragon-Subduing Palms.

The Six-Meridian Divine Sword.

The Great Shift of the Stars.

But gradually I understood that the *xia* my cousin spoke of and the restoration of the former dynasty he carried in his heart were never the same thing.

He taught me how to recognize characters.

He never taught me how to recognize a heart.

---

So which word had changed for me?

**Know.**

*知.*

I had once drowned in an ocean of knowledge.

I thought “knowing” meant possession.

Memorization.

Putting every martial formula under Heaven into my head.

Becoming an endlessly expanding container.

But the more I knew, the more lost I became.

Formulas.

Techniques.

Inner-energy doctrines.

They collided inside my mind like birds trapped in a cage.

Flapping desperately.

Unable to find an exit.

My knowledge entropy had reached its maximum.

Information accumulated without limit.

Order collapsed without limit.

---

Then one day I read:

> “Those who know are free from doubt; those who are humane are free from worry; those who are brave are free from fear.”

And suddenly I understood.

The destination of *knowing* is not **omniscience**.

It is **freedom from confusion**.

True knowing is not possessing the greatest possible amount of information.

It is knowing how to summon information in the most ordered way when the moment requires it.

Just like today.

I am no longer Wang Yuyan drowning beneath knowledge.

I am Wang Yuyan allowing knowledge to flow through me.

Just as the rain falling on the plane tree outside does not require me to remember every single drop.

Each drop simply falls where it belongs.

---

On the draft paper, I wrote the title:

# *Boundless Knowledge, a Heart with Walls*

**知无涯，心有垣**

I knew what I wanted to write.

Not an academic essay filled with quotations.

Not a demonstration of how much I knew.

I wanted to write the history of my own escape from a cognitive prison.

I began with my cousin's maps of martial arts.

I wrote about how the endless techniques had formed an ocean of knowledge around me.

But that same ocean had built a wall around my mind.

I wrote about the suffocation of information overload.

The moment when my knowledge entropy was highest.

Then I wrote about the turning point.

I began trying to believe in certain things.

Trying to connect cold, objective knowledge with the warm, subjective experience of being alive.

I wrote about finally understanding Qiao Feng's:

> “Though a thousand men stand against me, I shall still go.”

Not merely heroic courage,

but the courage to extend the boundaries of one's own convictions.

I wrote about Su Dongpo's:

> “With a straw rain cape and a bamboo hat, I let the wind and rain pass.”

Not resignation.

But a poetic turning of the body at the boundary of fate.

A form of composure.

---

I wrote:

True wisdom may not lie in knowing where the boundaries of the world are.

Perhaps it lies in knowing how to build the boundaries of one's own **heart**.

A boundary is not necessarily a limitation.

Sometimes it is the foundation upon which the self becomes possible.

Just as now—

inside this classroom,

at this old wooden desk carved with the character **静**,

I use a pen to draw the borders of my own inner world.

I quote widely.

But no longer to display how much I have stored.

I reason carefully.

But every step leads toward the conclusion that is most truthful to me.

When I wrote:

> **“Only the heart that knows its own walls can draw, without fear or drowning, a single cup from the boundless sea of knowledge.”**

I felt as though my soul had gently separated from the chair.

It rose to the height of the plane-tree branches.

From there, with compassion and serenity, I watched the girl below writing furiously.

The girl in the pale-blue school uniform.

Her hair slightly damp from the rain.

A girl from Suzhou.

---

In that moment, knowledge was no longer an external force I had to struggle to control.

It had become part of my body.

The way I breathed.

The way I thought.

The way I existed.

All the formulas.

All the books.

History.

Philosophy.

Martial arts.

Memory.

They gathered into one warm and powerful current.

It passed through my heart.

Through my fingers.

Through the tip of my pen.

And spilled onto the page.

I forgot the time.

Forgot where I was.

Forgot that this was an examination capable of determining the direction of my life.

I was simply expressing.

Confessing.

Speaking with my past,

my present,

and my future.

Outside, the rain seemed to keep the rhythm for me.

---

## Noon · The Bell and the Quieting of Entropy

The bell rang.

I was almost dazed.

So soon?

It had gone so quickly.

When the invigilator took my answer sheet, I had the strange feeling that the ink was still wet.

The characters that had carried every emotional movement of the morning were leaving me.

They were entering a much larger system of judgment.

A faint sadness rose inside me.

Like mist after rain.

Like the shadow of Gui Youguang's loquat tree.

---

I walked out of the examination room.

I stood beneath the entrance of the Science Building.

The rain had stopped.

I did not know when.

The sky was still gray, but along the edge of the clouds there was a little light.

It looked like diluted ink spreading across wet rice paper.

The camphor and plane trees had been washed brilliant green.

The surface of the Pan Pond was still as a mirror.

It reflected the blue-gray outline of the Gate of Wisdom and Virtue.

The air smelled clean and sweet.

This is what Suzhou smells like after rain.

The entire world seems to have been redrawn.

The flagstones shine.

Somewhere in the distance came the bell of the tower.

Long.

Hollow.

Spacious.

Almost as though it had drifted here from the Song dynasty.

I breathed deeply.

My lungs filled with cool air carrying grass and wet earth.

Around me, other students were excitedly comparing answers.

Some were quietly crying.

Their voices were close.

And far away.

I felt only peace.

A vast, almost exhausted peace.

---

The “knowledge entropy” inside my head had entered a strange state.

A state of **宁**.

Stillness.

The information was still there.

Vast as an ocean.

But it was no longer restless.

No longer colliding with itself.

It was like a sky of stars washed clean by rain.

Every star had returned to its proper place.

Quiet.

Warm.

Steady.

I knew the order would not last.

Tomorrow's mathematics would bring another storm.

The comprehensive examination after that would bring another.

But at least for this moment—

on the battlefield I knew best—

I had completed a beautiful battle of entropy reduction.

I had exchanged all my loneliness,

all my love,

all my confusion,

all my searching,

for two and a half hours of perfect inner order.

Two and a half hours of flow.

---

My cousin always says:

I know every martial art under Heaven,

yet I possess barely enough strength to kill a chicken.

He does not understand.

Perhaps true strength is not measured by how many enemies you can defeat outside yourself.

Perhaps it is measured by how much chaos you can tame within yourself.

Today I had none of the force of the Eighteen Dragon-Subduing Palms.

None of the sharpness of the Six-Meridian Divine Sword.

I had only a pen.

A sheet of paper.

And the knowledge entropy that had once caused me so much pain.

With those things, I built an entire and self-consistent universe of the mind.

In that universe,

I am both my own martial-arts master

and my own only reader.

---

On the way home, a few drops of rain began to fall again.

I passed along Renmin Road.

I passed the century-old ginkgo tree outside Suzhou High School.

I passed a teahouse from which came the sound of *pingtan*.

They were singing *The Jade Mantis*.

The soft Suzhou dialect sounded especially lingering in the air after rain.

Suzhou was still Suzhou.

But I felt different.

For eighteen years, I had lived inside this city.

Among the flowers and trees of Mantuo Villa.

Among yellowing pages.

Inside worlds written by other people.

But never before today had I known so clearly—

so quietly—

so certainly—

who I was.

---

The road ahead is still long.

The gaokao is far from over.

But I think I will remember this rainy noon.

I will remember the bell of this thousand-year-old academy.

The droplets hanging from the plane-tree leaves.

The character **静** carved into that old wooden desk.

They are all my own loquat tree.

All coordinates I may spend a lifetime looking back toward.

Perhaps this is what growing up means.

Not how much water you manage to collect,

but whether you finally carve a channel through which the water can flow.

Not how many techniques you remember,

but whether you finally understand—

**when to strike.**

---

Tonight, let this diary become

the first riverbed

through which my heart can finally flow without fear.

Tomorrow will be another day.

— **Yuyan, Suzhou, beneath the lamp on a rainy night.**
