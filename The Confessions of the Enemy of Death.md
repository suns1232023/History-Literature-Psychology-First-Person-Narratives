# **The Confessions of the Enemy of Death**

### *The Memoirs of Xue Muhua*

I saved countless lives in my lifetime.

Yet at that gathering of heroes, I watched more than three hundred men march toward death—and turned their lives into an ingredient for a medicine of my own making.

That medicine could not cure the wound left by my expulsion from my master's school.

It merely poisoned the last of the healer's compassion within me.

**Keywords:** #Culture & Media#

---

## **Volume I: A Letter from Jiangnan**

**The Second Year of Shaosheng, Song Dynasty — Summer Solstice, a Thatched Cottage Beyond Hangu Pass**

The cicadas screamed like boiling water.

I was grinding a prescription of *Revival Powder*. The stone pestle rose and fell against the mortar in a slow, monotonous rhythm.

Then a young servant brought me a visiting card.

It had come by way of a merchant caravan from Jiangnan.

The card was made of ordinary bamboo paper, but the words had been written in cinnabar. Under the dry afternoon sun, they glowed an unnerving red:

> *A master of ingenious devices has emerged in Suzhou. This year he has convened the “Gathering of One Heart,” bringing together forty thousand craftsmen and workers from across the realm. Gold rewards will be distributed among them; renowned speakers and singers will be invited; ten outstanding individuals will be chosen to journey south and gaze upon the stars. One wonders: how does this compare with the Heroes' Gathering at Juxianzhuang in years past?*

**Juxianzhuang.**

The stone pestle slipped from my hand and struck the floor.

With those three characters, a wound thirty years old was torn open once more.

I am Xue Muhua, whom the martial world has mockingly—or admiringly—called the **Enemy of Death**.

Since I first took up the physician's needle, I have snatched perhaps a thousand lives from the hands of the King of Hell.

But there are two people I could never save:

one was myself;

the other was the Xue Muhua who died beneath the weight of his own remorse.

The cicadas outside screamed louder.

Their cries gradually merged with the clash of swords and shouts of battle I remembered from Juxianzhuang.

And suddenly I remembered the day my master, Su Xinghe, cast me out of his school.

The cicadas had sounded exactly the same.

He stood with his back to me, his voice as dry and brittle as autumn leaves.

> “Muhua, your heart is impure. The Way of Medicine leaves no room for selfish desire.”

I did not understand him then.

Now, perhaps, I do.

The cinnabar in my inkstone had not yet dried. It looked like a wound that would never heal.

Very well.

While these hands can still hold a brush, and while this heart has not yet become completely numb, I shall open every festering wound I have hidden all these years and lay them beneath the merciless summer sun.

Let the disease be seen.

Let the diagnosis be made.

---

# **Volume II: The Heroes' Invitation**

### *A Prescription for Temptation*

## **I. The Patient: A Man Cast Out**

What a splendid name we once had:

**The Eight Friends of Hangu.**

My senior brother Kang Guangling could make the qin sing like mountains and rivers.

Fan Bailin, the second among us, could command a chessboard as though it were the heavens and earth.

Gou Du, the third, carried an entire library within his mind.

Wu Lingjun could make brush and ink contain the universe.

I was the fifth.

My art was the art of bringing the dying back from the threshold of death.

The sixth, Feng A-san, could breathe new life into dead wood.

The seventh, Shi Qingluo, could coax flowers to bloom out of season.

The eighth, Li Guilei, could become anyone and everyone, acting out the whole theater of human sorrow.

We might have spent our lives among the pines and clouds of Mount Drum, drinking wine, discussing art, and enjoying the freedom of the world.

Then Ding Chunqiu betrayed us.

He coveted the secret teachings of the Xiaoyao School and attacked our master.

To protect us, Master destroyed his own martial arts, pretended to be deaf and mute, and cast all eight of us out.

That day he stood with his back to us.

His shoulder blades protruded sharply beneath his worn robe.

His voice betrayed neither anger nor sorrow.

> “From this day forward, you have no connection with the Xiaoyao School. Our debt to one another is settled. Go. And do not look back.”

I knelt on the gravel road and struck my forehead against the stones until it bled.

Not from resentment.

From fear.

For the first time in my life, Xue Muhua had become a man without a root.

“Divine Physician.”

What an impressive title.

People respected me, feared me, begged me—because I could steal lives back from the King of Hell.

But late at night, amid the fragrance of medicinal herbs, I would still see my master's back.

And hear those four words:

**“Our debt is settled.”**

For decades, one obsession became the poison lodged deepest in my heart:

**I wanted to return to my master's school.**

The more people I saved, the greater my reputation became—and the deeper that poison sank.

I needed a cure.

A medicine violent enough to shake the martial world.

A remedy powerful enough that my master would have no choice but to see me again.

---

## **II. The Prescription: Justice as a Physician's Formula**

When the You brothers came to see me, I was already growing desperate.

You Ji's eyes burned like wildfire.

You Ju spoke with the smoothness of a hook hidden beneath silk.

> “The Khitan dog Qiao Feng has murdered his father and mother, slain his own teacher, and committed crimes against heaven and humanity. My brother and I intend to issue a Heroes' Invitation and summon the warriors of the realm to destroy this monster and restore justice. We ask only that Divine Physician Xue add his name.”

**Restore justice?**

They needed my name.

I needed their gathering.

Each would serve the other's purpose.

There was no need for further explanation.

I dipped my brush into ink and wrote:

**Xue Muhua.**

You Ju smiled and added beneath it:

> *Xue Muhua, renowned throughout the martial world as the Divine Physician.*

“There are too many roughnecks out there,” he said. “They need to know that the Bodhisattva of Healing has endorsed this.”

My brush stopped.

The ink spread slightly across the paper.

Again I heard my master's voice:

> “Muhua, your heart is impure.”

I closed my eyes.

When I opened them again, they were as still as a deep pool.

“Very well,” I said.

The Heroes' Invitations spread across the realm.

More than three hundred “heroes” answered the call.

They arrived like three hundred different medicinal ingredients thrown into a boiling cauldron.

Elders of the Beggars' Sect.

Monks of Shaolin.

Tan Gong and Tan Po.

Zhao Qiansun.

The Shan family.

Each came with a different motive.

Some sought to cleanse the martial world.

Some wanted to defend their reputation.

Some wished to curry favor with a famous physician.

Others simply wanted to watch the spectacle.

Their motives were different.

Their moral properties incompatible.

Yet beneath my name, they were gathered under a single banner:

**Kill the Khitan. Uphold righteousness.**

It was, in effect, a magnificent-looking tonic of “righteousness,” carefully compounded according to the principles of physician and apothecary:

chief ingredient, supporting ingredients, assistants, and harmonizers.

I sat at the center of it all and watched.

I knew the medicine was poisonous.

But at the time, I believed I was the physician standing over the cauldron.

I did not yet understand that **I, too, was one of the ingredients being boiled alive.**

---

## **III. The Boiling: Compassion upon the Flames**

Then he came.

Qiao Feng.

He carried a woman in green whose breath was barely perceptible.

He walked through a forest of swords as calmly as a man crossing the courtyard of his own home.

Sunlight followed him into the hall, casting his long, solitary shadow across the stone floor.

“Divine Physician Xue,” he said.

His voice was hoarse from the loss of internal energy, yet there was not a trace of pleading in it.

> “I beg you to save this woman. Qiao Feng will spend the rest of his life repaying you.”

Silence descended upon the hall.

More than three hundred pairs of eyes turned toward me.

I recognized the injury.

A Shaolin Vajra Palm had struck her.

Her heart meridians were shattered.

If treatment began immediately, there might still be a faint chance.

Wait much longer, and even the immortals could not save her.

But how could I treat her?

Those “heroes” had gathered here to kill him.

If I saved her, I would stand against the entire martial world.

My grand plan to return to my master's school would collapse.

In that moment, I seemed to see my master's back receding into the distance.

My voice emerged cold and unfamiliar, as though someone had dragged it up from the bottom of a well.

> “Qiao Feng. You are a Khitan barbarian who has slaughtered our people. Why should I save a woman who travels with you?”

The instant the words left my mouth, I saw it.

Not the anger in Qiao Feng's eyes.

Not his despair.

Something else.

Something far more terrible.

A small flame had gone out.

The final spark of his faith in the goodness of other human beings.

**I had extinguished it myself.**

Later, he drank the wine of severance, bowl after bowl, cutting himself loose from the friendships of his former life.

Then the battle erupted.

I retreated behind the crowd.

I watched blood and flesh fill the air.

I watched the You brothers' iron shields shatter.

I watched them shout:

> “While the shield stands, we stand. When the shield falls, we fall!”

And then they took their own lives.

The shattered shield fell to the ground.

And with it, the mask of the “Divine Physician” fell from my face.

I finally understood.

I had never been the physician standing over the cauldron.

I was another ingredient trapped inside it—

being boiled by my own obsession,

filling the air with the poisonous vapor of hypocrisy.

---

## **IV. The Residue: Guilt That Refused to Die**

After the battle, Elder Bai Shijing of the Beggars' Sect came to me.

In exchange for seven movements of the *Silken-Coiling Capturing Hand*, he begged me to save the wounded woman.

I did.

Silver needles pierced her acupoints.

Rare medicines sustained her failing life.

I pulled her back from the gates of death.

But when my fingers touched her pulse, my physician's hand trembled.

I was not merely saving a life.

I was trying to repay a debt.

Trying to fill the hole in my conscience with the one thing I had always been proud of:

my medical skill.

Worse still, the moment she regained consciousness, I began demanding information about the identity of the masked man.

I turned my patient into a bargaining chip.

I turned a physician's bedside into a chamber of interrogation.

That was when I saw the ugliest folds of my own soul.

She was clever.

She struck my pressure points, disguised herself as me, and escaped.

I lay helpless upon the bed, unable to speak, unable to move.

And yet, strangely, I felt relieved.

For a brief moment, the burden of this body—the reputation of “Divine Physician Xue,” the expectations, the ambition—had been taken from me.

Then Ding Chunqiu came.

The enemy I had sworn to destroy.

The enemy whose name alone could make my blood boil.

And the first thing I did when I heard he was coming?

I ordered my servants to prepare a coffin.

**I pretended to be dead.**

When Master Xuannan of Shaolin came to see the “dying” me, I confessed everything.

He sighed.

> “So even a divine physician is a man of suffering.”

No, Master.

I was not merely a man of suffering.

I was cowardly.

I was hypocritical.

The “hero” who had cried out at Juxianzhuang for justice against the Khitan was me.

The coward who trembled at Ding Chunqiu's name and hid inside a coffin was also me.

The lifelong dream of returning to my master's school had collapsed the moment genuine fear appeared.

---

# **Volume III: The Winding Road Home**

## **I. Ding Chunqiu: The Mirror of My Cowardice**

Ding Chunqiu eventually found me.

My pathetic attempt at pretending to be dead was child's play before him.

He lifted me as easily as one might lift a chicken and ordered me to treat a monk named Huijing.

My hands trembled as I worked.

He stroked his long white beard and smiled.

> “So this is the famous Enemy of Death? It seems even the King of Hell has been giving you special treatment.”

I was humiliated.

But not because he insulted me.

Because he had seen through me.

All my dignity.

All my medical skill.

All my reputation.

Before absolute power and absolute malice, they were nothing more than bargaining chips.

Things that could be taken from me whenever someone wished.

He forced me back to Mount Drum.

And there I saw my master, Su Xinghe.

The moment I saw him, the blood froze in my veins.

He was older.

Far older.

He looked like an ancient pine that had spent its last reserves of life.

Silent, he sat before the *Exquisite Chessboard*, seemingly oblivious to Ding Chunqiu's arrogance and to the return of his former disciples.

“Master…”

I fell to my knees.

For decades I had dreamed of this moment.

I had imagined returning in triumph.

Perhaps I would bring the warriors of the martial world and destroy Ding Chunqiu.

Perhaps I would save the school with some extraordinary feat of medicine.

Perhaps I would stand before my master, proud and dignified, and say:

> “Master, your disciple has returned.”

But I did not return that way.

I returned as a captive.

A broken man.

A stray dog dragged home by his enemy.

Then a young monk named Xuzhu accidentally solved the *Exquisite Chessboard* and inherited the power of my grandmaster, Wuyazi.

My master died beneath Ding Chunqiu's poisonous palm while protecting him.

Eventually Ding Chunqiu was defeated.

The enemy of our school was destroyed.

In some sense, I had finally “returned.”

But when I knelt beside my master's cooling body, the dream I had carried for half a lifetime tasted like ash.

**I had finally come home.**

**But the man I had wanted to welcome me home was already gone.**

I had spent decades believing that my “school” was a mountain, a name, a lineage, a seal of acceptance.

It was not.

It had always been my master's broad back.

The laughter of my brothers and sister disciples.

The feeling of belonging.

And all of that had disappeared into the evening wind thirty years earlier, when he spoke those four words:

**“Our debt is settled.”**

---

## **II. Another Medicine: A Prescription from Jiangnan**

The letter from Jiangnan arrived while I was still trying to swallow that mouthful of ash.

It had passed through many hands, sent by descendants of people who had lived a thousand years after my own age.

They told of a new enterprise in Suzhou called **Tiangong Zaowu**—“Heavenly Craftworks.”

Its young master devoted himself to ingenious mechanisms.

He had built self-sweeping mechanical figures that had become famous throughout the realm.

The previous year, he had organized something called the **Gathering of One Heart**.

Forty thousand craftsmen and workers had gathered under one roof.

The letter described a vast workshop large enough to hold them all.

Famous speakers spoke.

Singers performed.

Mechanical birds flew in formation, weaving patterns of light across the night sky.

The young master distributed gold rewards according to the number of people who had contributed.

Ten outstanding craftsmen were chosen to travel to the far south to witness glaciers and the southern lights.

And he had even promised medical protection for his workers and for their parents, wives, and children.

At the end of the letter was a sentence attributed to the young master:

> **“My workers are people I cherish.”**

I sat motionless for a long time.

The pestle slipped from my loosened fingers.

**The Gathering of One Heart.**

**The Heroes' Gathering.**

The two phrases suddenly seemed to face one another across a thousand years.

They gathered forty thousand people for “unity.”

We had gathered three hundred for the destruction of an enemy.

They distributed gold to honor labor.

We distributed invitations to feed reputations.

They sent people to the ends of the earth so that they might see more of the world.

We sent people toward death in the name of righteousness.

They regarded their workers as people to be cherished.

We regarded one another as instruments to be used.

**How similar the spectacle.**

**How utterly different the soul beneath it.**

In that moment I suddenly understood something.

The commotion at Juxianzhuang and my lifelong obsession with returning to my master's school were, at their core, almost identical.

Both were attempts to use an external, grand, magnificent purpose to fill an internal emptiness.

To prove my worth.

To make someone else acknowledge me.

Yet that Jiangnan merchant seemed to be doing something much simpler:

**He wanted the people who worked beside him to live better lives and see more of the world.**

---

# **Volume IV: The Physician's Diagnosis**

## **I. The Disease of the Heart**

In my later years, living alone, I began reading strange books said to have come from lands far beyond the seas.

They spoke of the nature of the human mind.

Their ideas were strangely similar to the medicine I had practiced all my life.

One book described how a selfish desire could slowly disguise itself as righteousness.

I shuddered.

Was that not exactly what I had done?

I had hidden my desire to return to my master's school beneath the magnificent banner of “destroying the Khitan and restoring justice.”

What I wanted was not justice.

I wanted recognition.

I wanted my master to see my name.

I wanted the martial world to acknowledge my worth.

The Heroes' Gathering had been a medicine I concocted to cure the disease of my wounded pride.

And that medicine had poisoned other people.

Another book described the human tendency to distort reality when one's actions conflict with one's beliefs.

At Juxianzhuang, Qiao Feng had risked his life to save a wounded woman.

He had acted openly and courageously.

Everyone could see it.

Yet we insisted that he was treacherous.

That he had hidden motives.

That he could not be trusted.

It was not that we were unable to see the truth.

**We were unwilling to see it.**

Because if we admitted what was before our eyes, the entire structure would collapse:

the gathering,

the righteousness,

the cause,

and finally, ourselves.

So we collectively chose blindness.

I finally understood:

**The human heart can fall ill just as the human body can.**

And perhaps the saddest physician is one who can diagnose every disease beneath heaven, identify every poison, and yet refuses to examine his own heart.

I was such a physician.

My illness festered unchecked until it contaminated the very needles and medicines with which I treated others.

---

## **II. True Healing: Beyond Medicine**

The story of the Jiangnan merchant pierced a place in me that had been numb for decades.

That young master practiced no martial arts.

He was no physician.

Yet he eased the burdens of thousands.

He used gold to acknowledge labor.

Travel to broaden horizons.

Protection to quiet fear about the future.

He was treating something deeper than physical illness:

the poverty of circumstance,

the narrowness of vision,

the anxiety of uncertainty.

He was healing people as human beings.

And he was creating solidarity through **shared purpose**, not shared hatred.

That made me think of Qiao Feng.

At Yanmen Pass, he ultimately gave his life to prevent war between two peoples.

He treated an illness far larger than any wound I had ever dressed.

His medicine was his own life.

His compassion.

His willingness to bear the cost himself.

And what had I done?

I called myself the Enemy of Death.

Yet the lives I saved were mostly bodies.

At Juxianzhuang, driven by private ambition, I nearly allowed an innocent woman to die.

I eventually healed A'Zhu's wounds.

But before and after the treatment, I had subjected her to fear, suspicion, and exploitation.

**Is the physician's duty truly limited to healing the flesh?**

Perhaps not.

Perhaps the greater medicine is to relieve suffering caused by circumstances.

To ease the burdens of fate.

To bring people together rather than turn them against one another.

I had been obsessed with the title of “Divine Physician.”

Obsessed with returning to a particular school.

My vision had become terribly small.

---

## **III. *Danggui*: Where the Heart Belongs**

When my master cast me out, he said:

> **“Your heart is impure.”**

For fifty years I searched for a prescription that might purify it.

I believed that prescription was hidden in my master's permission to return.

In the praise of the martial world.

In a grand Heroes' Gathering powerful enough to force my master to notice me.

Now, perhaps, I finally understand.

A pure heart is not something obtained from outside.

It comes from looking inward.

It is not about returning to a particular place.

It is about becoming at peace with where one stands.

It is not about being recognized by others.

It is about knowing who you are without their approval.

Qiao Feng was a Khitan, yet his compassion and courage surpassed that of many who called themselves Han.

He needed no one to grant him belonging.

His conscience was his own measure.

That Jiangnan merchant needed no title either.

He simply believed that the people who worked with him deserved better lives.

And by acting upon that belief, he won their loyalty.

Their “school,” their “home,” their “belonging” were not places or labels.

They lived in the principles they chose to uphold.

It took me half a lifetime, and a painful circle through shame and regret, to understand this.

My school had never truly been the abandoned courtyard on Mount Drum.

It was these four words:

**The compassion of a physician.**

My belonging was never dependent upon whether my master accepted me again.

It depended upon whether, every day, I could still call myself a physician—and deserve the name.

**Danggui.**

*Danggui* is a medicinal herb, warm and sweet with a hint of spice, traditionally used to nourish and harmonize the blood.

Perhaps that is why its name feels so fitting.

My “blood” is the medicine I carry.

My “harmony” is the reconciliation I must make—with my obsessions, my sins, and a world that has never been perfect.

---

# **Epilogue: The Final Prescription**

When I put down my brush, twilight had already fallen behind the mountains beyond Hangu Pass.

The cicadas had fallen silent.

All that remained was the almost imperceptible sound of plants growing in the medicinal garden, mingling with the distant roar of the Yellow River.

My life was like a prescription compounded incorrectly.

The dosage was wrong.

The ingredients were wrong.

The fire was wrong.

For the first half of my life, I kept adding things to the cauldron called **Xue Muhua**:

the prestige of a “Divine Physician,”

the obsession with returning to my master's school,

the vanity of status in the martial world.

I boiled them over a fierce fire, believing that I could refine them into a perfect elixir.

Instead, I produced nothing but bitter residue.

Medicine that poisoned others.

In the latter half of my life, especially during these solitary years in the thatched cottage beyond Hangu Pass, I began removing those burdens one by one.

It hurt.

It was like scraping poison from the bone.

Those desires had grown so deeply into me that they could no longer be separated from the flesh without pain.

But once they were removed, the old cauldron slowly revealed what it had been all along.

Not large.

Not magnificent.

But clean.

And still useful.

The forty-thousand-person Gathering of One Heart in Jiangnan was like a final medicinal catalyst.

It awakened the last remaining properties of an old medicine and finally showed me what should remain inside the cauldron.

Nothing more than this:

**When you see the sick, treat them with compassion.**

**When you see your own faults, do not fear correction.**

**When you see suffering in the world, let your heart respond.**

**When you see another person's success, rejoice rather than envy.**

Outside the window, the stars began to emerge.

I have heard that the southern sky is extraordinarily clear, unlike anything seen in the Central Plain.

Perhaps those ten craftsmen, rewarded with their journey, were at that very moment standing upon the ice, gazing upward at stars I would never see.

Their eyes must have been filled with wonder.

And yet, here in my little cottage beyond Hangu Pass, after finally opening the wounds of my life and laying them bare, I too felt something I had never known before.

A quiet sky of stars within my own heart.

Tomorrow, perhaps, another patient will knock upon my door.

This time I will not ask whether he is Han or Khitan.

I will not ask whether he is hero or outlaw.

I will not ask whether he is rich or poor.

I will see only this:

**a human being who needs help.**

And the name **Xue Muhua** will no longer need to mean “Divine Physician.”

Nor “Friend of Hangu.”

Nor “Disciple Cast Out of Xiaoyao.”

He will simply be a man—

an old physician—

who finally learned how to heal himself.

**— Xue Muhua, written in his cottage beyond Hangu Pass**

**The Second Year of Shaosheng, Song Dynasty**

# **Postscript**

This manuscript was written while my mind remained clear and set aside when the lamp of life was nearly exhausted.

All my life I was trapped by appearances and imprisoned by the self.

I treated medicine as a ladder with which to climb toward an imagined summit, only to fall back into the dust.

It was only in old age that I finally understood:

the greatness of medicine lies not merely in preserving the body.

It lies in relieving human suffering.

And, in the process, saving oneself from one's own demons.

I leave these pages behind not to seek fame, nor to fish for praise.

If some future physician happens upon them and recognizes, within these words, even one of the illusions that once blinded me; if, when treating the sick or making choices in life, he carries a little more clarity and a little less attachment—

then perhaps, amid all the sins of my life, there may yet remain a small measure of merit.

As for the Jiangnan enterprise called **Tiangong Zaowu**, and its “Gathering of One Heart,” the account herein is an artistic construction drawn from fragments of the world and shaped for the purposes of this story.

It does not refer to any particular real-world company or household.

Good deeds deserve to be praised wherever they are found.

What matters here is the idea.

Let the reader judge.

**Xue Muhua**

**Final words**

---

## **A Few Words for Those Who Come After Me**

When I was young, I read in *The Essential Prescriptions Worth a Thousand Gold Pieces*:

> *“A great physician must first still the mind and settle the will, free himself from desire, and awaken a profound compassion for all living beings.”*

Those words stirred my blood whenever I read them.

I thought them beautiful.

Only after wasting an entire lifetime did I understand how heavy the words **“free from desire”** truly are.

Desire follows us like a shadow.

Fame.

Wealth.

Affection.

Attachment.

Any of them can blind a person.

The gathering at Juxianzhuang became the mirror of my life.

What I saw in that mirror was not a hero.

Nor a divine physician.

Only a frightened, greedy, cowardly ordinary man who disguised private ambition beneath the robes of public righteousness.

What pains me most is that more than three hundred others stood before the same mirror that day—

and none of us recognized ourselves.

How many such “Heroes' Gatherings” have there been since?

How many will there be in the future?

So I leave three questions for anyone who reads these pages.

**First:**

Is what my heart desires truly for the common good—or for myself?

**Second:**

Is what I am doing an act of love—or an act of using others?

**Third:**

When we gather together, are we building one another up—or marching together toward destruction?

If you can answer those three questions honestly, and your heart remains clear, then even if ten thousand stand before you, you may proceed without fear.

But if even the smallest doubt remains—

stop at the cliff's edge.

Think again.

And think once more.

**— Xue Muhua**

### **Author's Note**

This work is a literary reimagining inspired in part by Jin Yong's *The Demi-Gods and Semi-Devils*, particularly the character of Xue Muhua and the events surrounding the Juxianzhuang gathering.

The story combines elements of group psychology, individual obsession, moral rationalization, and reflections on human destiny across different eras.

The enterprise “Tiangong Zaowu” and its “Gathering of One Heart” are fictional devices created for the purposes of artistic comparison. They are not intended to identify or describe any particular real-world company or event.

The purpose of the story is not to reconstruct history, but to use an imagined voice from the past to ask a question that remains very much alive in the present:

**When we call something “justice,” are we certain we are not merely giving our own desires a more beautiful name?**
