# **Tea Merchant’s Diary: My Years with the Pipa Player in Fuliang**

**Keywords:** #Tea #Agriculture

---

### **The 23rd Day of the Eighth Month, the 11th Year of Yuanhe — Fair**

I was busy at the tea market again today until the sun had begun to sink.

In spring, the air in Fuliang is always heavy with the fragrance of tea—the scent of freshly roasted leaves, of tea aged for years, of tea slowly fermented. All these aromas mingle together.

I began following my uncle into the tea trade when I was sixteen. Ten years have passed, and I now have a tea shop of my own.

The neighbors say Chen Sanlang has done well for himself. At sixteen, I was only a boy hauling baskets of tea for other merchants. Now I have my own shop at Changnan Wharf, two cargo boats, and seven or eight men working for me.

My uncle often says, “We tea merchants don't need great wealth or glory. Do honest business, live a steady life, and that is enough.”

But I know how difficult that “steady life” really is.

In spring, we climb into the mountains to buy tea and sometimes stay there for ten days or half a month, coming back covered in mosquito bites.

In summer, we escort our cargo by boat. Lake Poyang can change its temper in an instant. When the winds rise and the waves turn violent, a man's life hangs by a thread.

In autumn, we bargain with tea farmers over prices, compete with other merchants, and make arrangements with tax collectors and transport officials. Miss one thing, and an entire season's work can go to waste.

Yet I have never regretted it.

Whenever I stand on the wharf and watch sacks stamped with the characters **“Chen's Tea”** being loaded onto the boats, I feel a quiet satisfaction.

The tea will travel down the Chang River, enter Lake Poyang, then reach the Yangtze. From there it may go to Jiangzhou, Ezhou, and perhaps even farther—to Yangzhou and beyond.

Somewhere in those distant places, ordinary people may be drinking tea from our mountains in Fuliang.

**That is the foundation on which I have built my life.**

But when night falls and I return to my little two-courtyard home, the empty main hall sometimes makes me feel strangely hollow.

I am twenty-six this year, and still unmarried.

It isn't that I don't want a wife. I simply haven't met the right woman.

Matchmakers have brought several proposals. Some were daughters of farmers, others girls from modest households. It isn't that I look down on them. I simply feel that...

Something is missing.

My uncle says I have too much pride.

“A tea merchant,” he tells me. “Do you really expect to marry a fairy?”

I know my own worth.

In the eyes of scholars, merchants like us will always stand a rung below.

But I refuse to believe that makes us lesser men.

I earned everything I have with my own hands, saving one coin at a time. I have nothing to be ashamed of.

All I want is someone I can truly talk to.

---

### **The 5th Day of the Ninth Month — Overcast**

Today I finished delivering a shipment in Jiangzhou.

It was already late, so I stayed at an inn by the Xunyang River.

A woman in her early thirties was staying next door. She was slender, with a quiet, indescribable quality in her eyes.

At dusk, while I was resting in the courtyard, I heard the sound of a **pipa**.

I know nothing about music.

But that sound seemed to grow legs and walk straight into my heart.

It wasn't cheerful.

It was slow and heavy, like the river on an autumn night—calm on the surface, hiding some sorrow beneath.

Later I asked the innkeeper about her.

He told me she was the wife of a merchant whose boat was moored nearby. When she was young, she had lived in the entertainment quarters of Chang'an and had once been renowned for her pipa playing.

Now she traveled year-round with her merchant husband.

Something stirred inside me.

---

### **The 6th Day of the Ninth Month — Fair**

I went back to the river today, almost as if possessed.

She was sitting at the bow of the boat, wearing a slightly worn blue robe and mending a garment.

The sunlight fell across her face, revealing the faintest down on her cheek.

I stood on the bank watching her for quite some time before finally gathering the courage to approach.

“Was that you playing the pipa last night?”

She looked up.

“Can I help you?”

“You play beautifully.”

The moment the words left my mouth, I thought they sounded foolish.

The corner of her mouth lifted slightly.

“Only something to pass the time.”

I stood there, suddenly unsure what to say.

She did not send me away. She simply lowered her head and continued sewing.

The river breeze carried the faint scent of soap from her clothes, mixed with something else—a trace of old perfume, perhaps, from another life.

For the next several days, whenever I had a free moment, I went down to the river.

She didn't talk much.

But she was willing to listen.

I told her about the tea market in Fuliang, the storms on Lake Poyang, and the hardships of running a business.

Sometimes she would answer with a quiet “Mm.”

Mostly, she simply watched the river.

Once, I asked her about Chang'an.

She was silent for a long time.

Then she began to speak.

She told me about spring in Chang'an, when people gathered along the Qu River to admire the blossoms.

She told me about the entertainment quarters—the endless rehearsals during the day, the performances at night.

She told me about the young aristocrats from the Five Mounds, who would throw away fortunes merely to hear her play a single piece.

As she spoke, there was a light in her eyes.

It was the light of someone remembering a vanished glory.

But the light faded quickly.

“When I grew older,” she said calmly, “and my looks began to fade, no one came anymore.”

She spoke as though she were describing someone else's life.

“Then someone introduced me to a merchant who was willing to marry me. So I married him.”

“Is he good to you?”

She did not answer.

She simply stared at the river.

I suddenly remembered the line from Bai Juyi:

**“Merchants value profit and care little for parting.”**

I had never liked those words.

We merchants have to care about profit. Otherwise, how are we supposed to feed our families?

But looking at her lonely figure sitting at the bow of the boat, I wondered if perhaps the poet had not been entirely wrong.

---

### **The 15th Day of the Ninth Month — Night**

I have made up my mind.

**I will marry her.**

This is not an impulse.

I have thought about it for three nights.

I know who she is.

She was born into the entertainers' register. She has been married before. She is older than I am.

I know people will gossip.

They will say Chen Sanlang married an actress.

They will say I have no ambition.

But so what?

I did not build my tea business by worrying about what other people thought.

I made my living through my own ability.

If I can turn myself from a boy hauling tea baskets into the owner of a successful tea shop, I can certainly protect the person I choose to protect.

Over the years, I have seen too many marriages in which the husband spends his life traveling by boat while the wife waits at home.

Sometimes they see each other only a few times a year.

Eventually, affection grows thin.

I don't want that kind of life.

I want someone who knows when I am cold or tired.

Someone who thinks of me when I am away.

Someone who leaves a lamp burning when I come home.

**She is that person.**

Today I went to her and told her plainly.

She stared at me for a long time.

Then she laughed.

There were tears in her eyes.

“You're mad,” she said.

“Do you know what kind of woman I am?”

“I do.”

“Do you know I'm older than you?”

“I know.”

“Do you know I've been married before?”

“I know.”

She looked at me for a long time.

Then she turned away.

Her shoulders trembled slightly.

“I have heard too many beautiful promises in my life,” she said softly.

“When I was in Chang'an, the men who came to hear me play could say things ten times, a hundred times more beautiful than anything you've said.

“But when morning came, who remembered whom?”

“I am not like them,” I said.

“I am a plain man. I don't know how to speak beautifully.

“But when Chen Sanlang gives his word, he keeps it.

“If you come with me, you will never again have to sit alone on some broken boat.

“You will never again have to play the pipa for people who don't care who you are.

“I want you to be my wife, openly and honorably.”

She said nothing.

She only cried.

And seeing her cry broke my heart.

---

### **The 3rd Day of the Tenth Month — Rain**

Things have proved more complicated than I expected.

Her husband—the merchant who runs tea cargoes to Qimen—refused to let her go at first.

I went to negotiate with him three times.

In the end, I paid him **fifty strings of cash**.

Only then did he agree.

Fifty strings.

That is nearly half a year's profit from my tea business.

When my uncle heard about it, he cursed me for losing my senses.

“With fifty strings,” he said, “you could buy a fine house in Fuliang. Why waste it on a woman like her?”

I did not argue.

Some things are better understood than explained.

Then came the matter of obtaining the official papers releasing her from her former status.

She had belonged to the entertainers' register. Although she had already left that life, the old record remained attached to her household registration and marriage documents.

I spent some money, called in a few favors, and eventually got everything settled.

The clerk looked at me with a knowing expression.

“Master Chen,” he said, “you certainly have unusual taste.”

I smiled and said nothing.

I knew what he was thinking.

He probably thought I was merely infatuated with her beauty—or that there was some other reason I would not speak of.

They don't understand.

**I don't want a beautiful woman.**

**I want someone who understands me.**

---

### **The 18th Day of the Tenth Month — Fair**

Today we were married.

There was no elaborate ceremony.

Only my uncle and a few close merchant friends attended.

She wore red and sat quietly beside me, pouring wine for the guests.

Someone shouted that she should play the pipa.

She looked at me.

I nodded.

She played **“Spring River, Flower Moon Night.”**

I know too little about music to judge it, but from the expressions on the guests' faces, I knew she played beautifully.

My uncle drank too much and pulled me aside.

“Sanlang,” he said, “your wife... she's not an ordinary woman.”

“I know,” I said.

I know she is extraordinary.

She has seen splendor I have never seen.

She has heard poetry I have never heard.

She has played music I could never learn.

But what of it?

After the guests had gone, she sat quietly on the edge of the wedding bed.

I walked over and took her hand.

Her hand was cold.

There were small calluses on her fingertips.

“From now on,” I told her, “this is your home.”

She looked up at me.

Suddenly, tears fell down her cheeks.

---

### **The 21st Day of the First Month, 12th Year of Yuanhe — Snow**

Three months have passed since our wedding.

Our house has begun to feel alive.

Every evening when I return from work, there is a lamp in the courtyard.

There is food in the kitchen.

There is hot tea waiting on the table.

She sits in the main hall doing needlework, and when she hears me come in, she rises and pours me a cup of water.

These things sound ordinary.

But for me, they are something I have never had in twenty-six years.

She knows how to run a household.

The clothes I leave lying around are neatly folded.

The sleeves of my favorite robes, worn thin at the cuffs, are carefully mended until the repairs are almost invisible.

When I cough at night, she gets up to make ginger tea and sits beside me until I finish it.

Sometimes I think:

**My life is complete.**

But she often cannot sleep.

Sometimes I wake in the night and find her sitting alone by the window.

When I ask what is wrong, she only says she had a dream.

I know she is thinking of Chang'an.

Thinking of a life she can never return to.

Once, after I had been drinking, I heard her playing the pipa in the courtyard.

It was the same piece I had first heard in Jiangzhou.

Slow.

Heavy.

Like someone speaking to herself in the darkness.

I walked over and sat beside her.

“Do you miss Chang'an?”

She didn't stop playing.

She only whispered:

“Yes.”

“Tell me about it.”

She told me about the night markets of Chang'an.

The bustle of the Eastern and Western Markets.

The lanterns that filled the city on the fifteenth night of the first month.

As she spoke, her eyes shone.

She looked almost like a child.

Listening to her, I felt something tighten in my chest.

“Do you think you've sacrificed too much by coming here with me?”

She stopped playing and turned toward me.

Moonlight fell across her face, revealing the fine lines around her eyes.

“What are you saying?” she asked softly.

“Chang'an may be beautiful,” she continued, “but it is someone else's Chang'an.

“This is my home.”

She said it seriously.

And yet I knew there was a place in her heart I would never be able to enter.

---

### **The 12th Day of the Third Month — Fair**

Today I drank with my uncle and finally told him what had been troubling me.

He slammed his hand on the table.

“You've read yourself stupid!” he shouted.

“What was your wife before? A performer!

“Were those men who praised her truly good to her?

“They only wanted entertainment.

“They could throw hundreds of strings of cash at her for one song, then walk away. Who remembered her name afterward?

“But you—you actually married her.

“You gave her a home.”

My uncle's words were crude.

But they were not entirely wrong.

I thought about it.

Those young aristocrats who once showered her with red silk—did any of them ever wonder what would happen when she grew old?

Did any of them give her a home when she had nowhere else to go?

No.

Only I, Chen Sanlang, a tea merchant from Fuliang, was willing to marry her openly and bring her home.

I gave her a name she could stand behind.

I gave her stability.

Thinking of it that way, I felt much better.

---

### **Dragon Boat Festival — Fair**

She woke early today to make zongzi.

I sat in the main hall watching her work.

Glutinous rice, red dates, reed leaves—her hands moved swiftly, and before long she had made a perfectly shaped little parcel.

Those hands could play the pipa.

They could also work in a kitchen.

“I never knew you could do this,” I said.

She smiled.

“I learned at the entertainment quarters.

“When festivals came, the older women made us prepare our own food. They said we should understand the hardships of ordinary people.”

She spoke calmly, as though describing someone else's life.

But I knew how difficult the road had been—from the entertainment quarters of Chang'an to the home of a tea merchant in Fuliang.

At lunch, she suddenly said:

“I want to plant an osmanthus tree in the courtyard.”

“Why?”

“Autumn in Chang'an is filled with the scent of osmanthus.”

She looked toward the empty patch of earth.

“I want to smell it here too.”

I agreed.

The next day, I bought a young osmanthus tree.

We planted it together.

She knelt in the earth, carefully pressing soil around the roots, concentrating as though she were caring for a child.

I know she will never forget Chang'an.

But so what?

**Chang'an belongs to the first half of her life.**

**I belong to the second half.**

Can't she have both?

---

### **Mid-Autumn Festival — Fair**

There was a lantern festival in Jiangzhou today.

I finished work early and took her to see it.

She was unusually happy.

She put on a new dress and applied just a little rouge.

As we walked through the streets, I watched her secretly.

She was still beautiful.

Not the dazzling beauty of youth.

Something quieter.

Like moonlight resting on the river.

When we reached the Xunyang River, she suddenly stopped.

I followed her gaze.

An official boat was moored by the river.

Several people stood on its deck.

Among them was a middle-aged man in a blue robe, his hands behind his back as he looked at the moon.

“That is Magistrate Bai,” she whispered.

I was puzzled.

“Which Magistrate Bai?”

“Bai Juyi.

“He was demoted to Jiangzhou last year.”

Then I remembered.

**The poem “Song of the Pipa Player.”**

Wasn't it about a woman like her?

She stood there for a long time.

Moonlight fell across her face, but I could not read her expression.

“Would you like to go over and see him?” I asked.

She shook her head.

“No.

“That belongs to the past.”

She turned toward me and smiled.

“Let's go home.”

The smile was faint.

But it was real.

---

### **The 23rd Day of the Twelfth Month, 12th Year of Yuanhe — Snow**

The year is coming to an end again.

I finished the tea accounts today.

We made thirty percent more profit than last year.

I gave each of the workers a red envelope and sent my uncle a jar of good wine.

Snow was falling heavily on the way home.

When I reached the entrance to our lane, I could see the warm light shining from our courtyard.

I pushed open the gate.

She was in the main hall making dumplings.

When she heard me, she looked up.

There was flour on her face.

“You're back?”

She stood and wiped her hands on her apron.

“Dinner will be ready soon. Sit by the fire first.”

I sat beside the brazier and watched her moving about in the kitchen.

And suddenly I knew:

**This is the life I wanted.**

Simple.

Steady.

Someone waiting for you.

A home to return to.

After dinner, she brought out her pipa.

“I've learned a new piece,” she said. “I'll play it for you.”

It was a cheerful little tune I had never heard before.

“What is it called?”

**“Going to Fuliang to Buy Tea.”**

She smiled.

“I made it up myself.”

I laughed.

“You're making fun of me.”

“No,” she said.

“I mean it.

“I have lived in Chang'an.

“I have lived in the entertainment quarters.

“I have lived on boats.

“But only here—in Fuliang, beside you—have I truly felt at peace.”

I said nothing.

I simply took her hand.

It was warm.

The calluses on her fingertips had softened.

---

### **The 8th Day of the Third Month, 13th Year of Yuanhe — Fair**

Today she suddenly said she wanted to teach me how to play the pipa.

I was startled.

“These hands?” I said. “I've hauled tea baskets for ten years. They're rough as tree bark. How could they ever play the pipa?”

“I'll teach you.”

And she did.

She started with the simplest finger positions.

One note at a time.

I was clumsy.

The sounds I produced were more like a dying rooster than music.

She laughed until she could barely breathe.

“If you played in the entertainment quarters,” she said, “every customer would run away.”

I laughed too.

“Good. Then they can all leave.

“I'll play only for you.”

After more than half a month, I could just about play a simple tune, **“Looking Toward the South.”**

It was still hesitant and uneven.

But at least it sounded like a song.

Last night, I played it for her in the courtyard.

When I finished, her eyes were red.

“What happened?” I asked.

“Was it really that terrible?”

She shook her head and wiped her eyes.

“No.

“It was beautiful.”

Only later did I understand.

She wasn't saying the music was beautiful.

She meant that **having someone willing to learn the pipa for her was beautiful.**

---

### **Mid-Autumn Festival, 13th Year of Yuanhe — Fair**

Another Mid-Autumn Festival.

The osmanthus is in bloom.

The whole courtyard smells sweet.

She prepared a table full of food, and we sat beneath the tree, drinking wine and watching the moon.

“Do you regret it?” I asked.

“Regret what?”

“Marrying me.

“If you had stayed in Chang'an, perhaps...”

She put down her wine cup and looked at me seriously.

“Let me tell you a story,” she said.

“Once there was a bird that had lived in a cage for a very long time.

“Then one day, the cage was opened.

“The bird flew out and eventually found the eaves of a poor family's house.

“There it built a nest.

“There it made a home.

“If you asked that bird whether it regretted leaving the cage, what would it say?

“It would say:

**No matter how beautiful the cage, it is still a cage.**

**No matter how humble the roof, it is still a home.**”

I listened quietly.

I thought of what my uncle had said.

How many of those men who once praised her had truly cared for her?

Probably none.

They had only wanted an evening's entertainment.

“You don't mind that I'm a rough man?” I asked.

“I don't know how to write poetry.

“All I know is how to sell tea.”

She smiled and reached up to touch my face.

“That is exactly what I like about you.

“You are honest.

“People who know how to write beautiful poetry often have too many thoughts in their heads.

“You listen to a poem today, and tomorrow the poet may already be gone.

“You sell tea.

“But you use that tea to keep a household fed and warm.

“That is enough.”

She paused.

“Bai the Magistrate wrote:

**‘We are both wanderers at the edge of the world; why should it matter whether we ever knew each other?’**

“I didn't understand those words before.

“I do now.

“We are both people who have been cast adrift.

“But when two such people find each other, perhaps they are no longer adrift.”

The moon climbed high above us.

She lifted her pipa and played a piece I had never heard before.

It was quiet.

Unhurried.

Like the autumn river flowing slowly beneath the moon.

“What is this one called?” I asked.

**“Night on the Xunyang River.”**

“I composed it myself,” she said.

“It is for you.”

I listened.

I watched her profile beneath the moonlight.

And suddenly I thought:

**My life has been worth it.**

I, Chen Sanlang, a tea merchant from Fuliang, have spent my life learning how to sell tea.

But the finest bargain I ever made was not tea.

**It was her.**

It is late.

She is telling me to go to bed.

Tomorrow I must rise early for the tea market.

I will end here.

**Written by Chen Sanlang,
at his home in Fuliang,
Mid-Autumn Festival, the 13th Year of Yuanhe**
