The Ledger and the Glass Slipper: Economics of a Stepmother
I married not a man, but a house about to be repossessed by the bank, and two daughters waiting to be fed.

Love is a luxury; survival is a necessity.

— Epigraph from the frontispiece of Lady Tremaine’s Ledger

Prologue: The Pressed Rose on the Ledger Page
Month-end reconciliation again.

The quill scratched against parchment, whispering in the late-night study like rats gnawing on timber. The hearth fire was dying. I pulled my shawl closer—bought three years ago, its wool worn shiny, the elbows patched with stitches so fine they were invisible unless you looked closely. Much like this household: immaculate on the surface, hollow and ruined within.

"Mother, still awake?"

My eldest, Anastasia, pushed open the door, holding a glass of warm milk. She was twenty-four, with fine lines creeping around her eyes. Her courtship last year had failed again—the widowed baron felt her dowry was far too thin. I took the glass. Her fingers touched mine, warm and steady. She was always so thoughtful, thoughtful enough to break one’s heart.

"Almost finished," I murmured, turning a page. On the column with fresh ink, I read: March 27th, Cinderella’s music lessons: 30 silver coins.

Thirty silver coins. Enough to purchase half a bolt of fine linen, pay a coachman’s wages for a fortnight, or tailor a respectable gown for Anastasia’s ball. I stared at those words until the ink bled slightly, like an old tear.

Outside, carriage wheels clattered over cobbles. It was Baron Duval’s carriage returning from Paris, laden with newly bought silk and perfume. The sound cut harshly through the silent night. I rose to close the shutter, my fingers brushing a deep scratch on the sill—left by Charlotte, my youngest, when she was ten. Her father was still alive then; she had sat on his shoulders, carving her name with a penknife.

C. T. — Charlotte Tremaine.

Beneath it was a smaller line added by her father: My little princess, born in the spring of 1712.

Seventeen-twelve. Sixteen years ago. The war had just ended, the economy was thriving, and this house was filled with banquets. The garden bloomed with roses. I wore custom gowns from Lyon, held his arm, and listened to guests praise us as "the most elegant couple in the city."

Now the roses were dead, the gowns pawned, the guests vanished. He lay in the churchyard, moss eating away at his headstone. And I sat in the study he left behind, calculating how many coins were missing to avert bankruptcy before morning.

The milk turned cold. I drank it in one breath. The faint tang of milk mixed with the parchment’s leather scent rolled down my throat, swallowed like a lump of cold iron.

Anastasia lingered. "Mother, Cinderella’s master said... she is very gifted. Next month, when the royal musicians come to select students, if..."

"I know," I interrupted, my voice colder than intended. "Go to sleep."

She pressed her lips together and turned to leave, her skirt brushing the doorframe. I watched her slightly stooped back—a posture ruined by long hours over embroidery, irreversible, much like our declining social standing.

Silence reclaimed the study. I opened the drawer and pulled out an older ledger. Bound in worn cowhide, its pages held memories rather than numbers—a different kind of account.

Flipping to a page, I found a dried rose. Its petals were yellowed, brittle to the touch. Beside it was my seventeen-year-old handwriting, delicate and spirited:

Met him today at the Versailles ball. He wore dark blue velvet and a pearl brooch. He asked me to dance, saying my eyes were like midnight velvet. Mother says he is Viscount Tremaine, heir to vast vineyards. Not the wealthiest, but full of promise. I pretended not to care, but my heart beat fast. His hands were warm.

I stared at those words for a long time, then took up the quill and added a line beside them. My current handwriting—dry, sharp, carved like a blade on stone:

Thirty years later, the vineyards were wiped out by blight, leaving mountains of debt. He died of consumption, clutching my hand and whispering "I'm sorry." It rained on the day of his funeral. Water filled the grave. When his coffin went down, it made a heavy splash, like a stone dropped into a well. I have remembered that sound for ten years.

I closed the book. Crumbs of the dried rose drifted down onto the fresh entry—30 silver coins—like a threadbare mourning flower laid upon a cold number.

Outside, the moon rose, casting a pale light over the ledger. Those columns of numbers looked like graves, burying my youth, my love, and every gentle vision I had ever held of the world.

Tomorrow, I would still have to rise early, haggle with the seamstress, turn away the debt collector's lawyers, and tell Cinderella: Your music lessons are canceled.

Because thirty silver coins could buy two lives—the lives of my own daughters.

Chapter 1: Marriage is a Limited Liability Company
I was twenty-one when I married Edward Tremaine. Neither too young nor too old. My father was a tax magistrate—a modest official. We were six daughters; I was the third. On the eve of my wedding, my mother took my hand and gave me three rules:

"First, never love too deeply. Men die and love fades, but land deeds and bank deposits endure."

"Second, bear children quickly, preferably sons. Daughters are a drain; marrying them off strips a family bare."

"Third, keep the ledger. Only if you control the money can you control the household—and your husband’s heart, though his heart is worth the least."

I carved those three rules into my mind like an epitaph. On my wedding day, wearing a rented gown and holding artificial flowers, I walked down the aisle. Edward waited at the altar. He was handsome, with dark brown hair, gray eyes, and a dimple on his left cheek when he smiled. When he took my hand, his palm was damp.

The guests applauded and whispered. I caught fragments: "...they say her dowry was only five thousand francs..." "...the Tremaines have fallen, too..." "...they’ll manage, in times like these..."

I smiled, lifting my chin to an angle practiced a hundred times before the mirror—neither arrogant nor subservient. I knew what they meant. Edward’s vineyards suffered year after year, and my father’s position was precarious. This marriage was two ruined families huddling together for warmth, two leaking ships tied together by a frayed rope, praying not to sink too fast.

On our wedding night, he kissed me gently, as if afraid to break something. I responded, but my mind was calculating: How much for house repairs? Servant wages? Next quarter’s taxes? When he entered me, I gasped from the pain, gripping the bedsheets until the silk creaked. He whispered "I'm sorry." I said "It's alright."

My eyes remained open, watching the elaborate embroidery on the canopy—roses entwined with thorns. So this is marriage, I thought. Two people sharing a bed, separated by debt, calculations, and the inevitable decline of family honor.

Still, I wanted to run it well—like a small shop. The capital was modest, but with thrift and shrewdness, perhaps we could break even, or even turn a small profit.

I learned the art of winemaking from the old estate hands, walking through the vineyard, examining soil, sunlight, pruning, and mold prevention. My hands blistered, tore, calloused, and tore again. Edward felt pity, saying, "Leave these chores to the servants." I shook my head: "If I do not understand it, how can I manage it?"

I studied endlessly, reviewing ledgers late into the night. When I discovered the steward inflating purchase costs, I dismissed him and took over the accounting myself. Suppliers tried to overcharge me because I was a woman; I compared prices and negotiated until their faces flushed, calling me "shrewder than a money-lender."

Edward was relieved at first, then uneasy. One evening, after drinking too much, he held me and said, "Mary, you don't need to exhaust yourself like this. I can..."

"You can what?" I poured him more wine, my voice gentle. "You invested in shipping last year and lost three thousand francs. The year before, it was mining shares that collapsed. Edward, it isn't that I don't trust you. It's that this family cannot afford another gamble."

He fell silent, burying his face in my neck. I caught the scent of wine mixed with the faint despair of a defeated man. I stroked his hair without emotion, filled only with a cold clarity: This man is my business partner, not my savior. Marriage is a limited liability company. We share the profits, but if it fails, I must ensure my own share is not dragged down by his folly.

When Anastasia was born, I suffered a difficult labor that lasted two days and nights. The child survived, but I hemorrhaged and lay unconscious for three days. When I awoke, Edward sat by the bed, his eyes sunken and stubble covering his chin. He wept, holding my hand: "No more children. One is enough, more than enough."

I looked at the infant in the cradle—tiny, red, like a featherless sparrow. I felt no joy, only panic: A daughter. A financial burden. A dowry to prepare, a respectable husband to find, a life to secure so she wouldn't be looked down upon—and all of it required money.

I had walked to the edge of the grave, nearly losing my life, only to bring forth a financial burden.

I closed my eyes and said, "Very well. No more."

Two years later, Charlotte arrived unexpectedly. I took remedies, bound my waist until I could hardly breathe, yet she arrived on a cold night anyway. This labor was worse—a breech birth that nearly cost both our lives. When the midwife brought the infant to me, I turned my head away: "Take her away."

Edward named them: Anastasia and Charlotte. Pretty names, like princesses in fairy tales. But ours was no fairy tale; we were declining figures in a ledger, impending debts, and vineyards producing less fruit with each passing year.

When Charlotte was three, Edward brought home a girl.

She was about six, thin as a reed, with golden hair, blue eyes, and a tattered rag doll in her arms. She hid behind him, peeking at me with timid eyes, like a frightened fawn.

"This is Cinderella," Edward said, kneeling to nudge her forward gently. "Her father... was an old friend of mine. He passed away recently. Her mother has been gone for years. From today on, she will live with us."

I observed the girl. Her dress was worn, but made of fine cotton with delicate lace along the collar. Her leather shoes were muddy, yet shaped in the latest Parisian cut. Most striking was her expression—clear, innocent, protected from the world.

Such innocence required a great deal of money to cultivate.

"What did her father do?" I asked calmly.

"...A merchant. In the spice trade," Edward said, avoiding my gaze.

"How much inheritance did he leave?"

"Mary!" He frowned, though his tone softened quickly. "She is only a child. We can discuss that later. Let her stay first, please?"

I asked no more questions, for I had noticed the necklace around her neck—a thin gold chain bearing a small, roughly cut, but high-quality sapphire. It was an item I had admired in jeweler windows many times, but could never afford.

I knelt and offered a smile, brushing my fingers past her curly hair. "Hello, Cinderella. I am Mary. You may call me... Aunt."

She blinked and murmured softly, "Hello, Aunt."

Her voice was gentle, carrying a southern accent. It sounded sweet—and very expensive.

Edward sighed with relief, picked her up, and said, "Let me show you your room." As he turned, I saw it: a fresh red scratch on the back of his neck, as if from a fingernail. Shallow, but distinct.

I stood motionless, watching their figures disappear around the stairhead. Then I turned, walked into the study, and opened the ledger. On a new page, I wrote:

June 15th. Additional dependent: Cinderella (claims to be six, likely seven). Estimated annual expenses: 30 francs for clothing, 50 francs for food, 80 francs for education, 20 francs for sundries. Total: 180 francs/year. Note: Verify her father's estate immediately. Also: Edward returned today with a neck scratch and the scent of musk (expensive variety). Suspect an affair. Maintain observation.

I closed the book. My fingers brushed the rough cowhide, like a dull blade scraping against skin.

Outside, the roses were in bloom—broad, blood-red petals. Edward had once mentioned that his mother loved roses, which was why the garden was filled with them. But roses were fragile; they required fertilizer, pruning, and constant care, or they would wither before your eyes.

Just like this house. Just like marriage. Just like this strange girl who had suddenly arrived, requiring one hundred and eighty francs a year to raise.

I stood and walked to the window. In the garden, Edward held Cinderella’s hand, pointing out the blossoms to her. Golden sunlight bathed them, resembling a tranquil painting.

How lovely it appeared.

How deceptively lovely—like the deficits in my ledger that could never be balanced.

I drew the curtains, shutting out both the light and the roses.

Chapter 2: Emotion is a Liability, Reason is an Asset
Three months after Cinderella’s arrival, the matter of the estate was settled.

Her father had indeed traded in spices, and he had indeed gone bankrupt—not dying of illness, but drowning himself in a river when the debts overwhelmed him. He left no fortune, only promissory notes. A thick stack bound with a silk ribbon was stuffed into the bottom of Cinderella’s small trunk—a grim rite of passage.

Edward burned those notes in the hearth, one by one. Flames licked the paper, curling it black before it turned to ash. He stood with his back to me, his shoulders slumping like an empty sack.

"She knows nothing," he said, his voice hoarse. "She believes her father merely traveled far away on business."

I remained silent, calculating: In these three months, Cinderella's expenses have already passed fifty francs. She is a picky eater, demanding tender chicken and fresh berries; she requires daily changes of clothing, washed and ironed with lavender scent; she suffers nightmares and requires company at night, which has already driven away two maids.

Fifty francs. Enough for three months of allowance for Anastasia and Charlotte. Enough for half a year's property tax. Enough to purchase a carriage horse.

Now, those fifty francs had bought a pile of ash and another man's daughter to raise indefinitely.

"She cannot stay," I said at last, my tone as measured as if discussing the weather. "Send her to an orphanage, or find a distant relative. We cannot afford her."

Edward turned around, his eyes red—whether from the smoke or from tears, I could not tell. "She is so young! Mary, have you no heart?"

"I have a heart," I walked toward him, step by step, my heels clicking sharply against the marble floor. "Because I must first reserve my heart for my own daughters. Anastasia is sixteen; it is time to prepare her dowry. Charlotte is frail; her medicine cannot be interrupted. The roof of the manor leaks; if we do not repair it, we will freeze in the winter. Edward, you have seen the ledgers."

He opened his mouth, but made no sound. I took another step closer, looking up at him. The gray eyes that had once stirred my heart were now clouded, weak, and helpless.

"Or," I softened my voice, reaching out to adjust his collar—a gentle gesture, like a dutiful wife, "tell me where we will find that extra hundred and eighty francs. Will you win it at the card tables? Borrow from usurers? Or..."

I paused, my fingers brushing his neck—the scratch had faded, but a faint mark remained.

"...will you borrow it from the widow of your 'spice merchant friend'?"

He froze. His pupils contracted sharply. Then he grabbed my hand tightly, his nails digging into my skin. "You investigated me?"

"I am your wife." I let him hold my wrist without resisting, even offering a faint smile. "Tracking your whereabouts to ensure you aren't swindled out of our remaining property by a mistress is both my duty and my right."

He released his grip, stumbling back against the mantelpiece. The brass candlestick wobbled, nearly falling. He stared at me as if looking at a stranger.

"She is not a mistress," he trembled. "She is Cinderella’s mother. We... loved each other. But her husband refused a divorce, so she had to give birth to Cinderella in secret and foster her in the countryside. Now her husband is dead, and she wanted to claim her daughter, but..."

"But she went bankrupt as well, could not afford her, and dumped her on you?" I finished the sentence, walking over to the ledger and tapping the newest red entry. "Edward, your love story is moving. But sentiment cannot pay bills, repair roofs, or secure dowries for my daughters."

I snapped the ledger shut with a crack that echoed in the silent room.

"Cinderella may stay," I said, each word falling like ice. "But from this day on, I run this household. Your allowance is halved, and you will touch no more investments. Anastasia and Charlotte’s needs come first; Cinderella receives what remains. Agree, and nod. Disagree..."

I walked to the window and pulled back the curtain. The sun had set. In the garden, Cinderella knelt by the rosebushes, trying to catch a butterfly. She laughed happily, her golden hair catching the fading light.

"...disagree, and I will walk down right now and tell her how her father died, and why her mother abandoned her. Then I will have the coachman drive her to the cheapest orphanage in the city—you know what those places are like. Rats, lice, a piece of moldy bread a day, and winters cold enough to freeze off your toes."

I turned back to Edward. His face was pale, drained of color.

"Choose," I said.

He lowered his head, his shoulders trembling. At first it was quiet, then it turned into uncontrollable weeping. A forty-year-old man, kneeling before the hearth, weeping like an abandoned dog.

I walked over, knelt, and held him like a child. My chin rested on his shoulder as I patted his back gently, humming a lullaby—the same one I sang to put Anastasia and Charlotte to sleep.

He wept harder.

I looked at the ashes in the hearth—the remains of those notes, now cold and black, like a decayed heart.

I knew I had won. A complete victory. I had gained absolute authority over the household, alongside a husband who resented me, and a burden who would consume my daughters' dowries.

Yet what of it? In this world, one either devours or is devoured. One is either cruel, or starves.

I chose the former.

That night, I opened a new page in the ledger under the heading: Cost-Benefit Analysis of Fostering Cinderella Tremaine (Provisional Name).

On the left column: Costs — Board, lodging, education, medical, dowry (future).

On the right column: Potential Returns — If trained well, may marry into a middle-class family for a bride-price of 500–1000 francs; if extraordinarily beautiful, may attract minor nobility, gaining connections (probability below 5%).

In the notes, I added: Subject displays a delicate nature; requires strict discipline to remove vanity. Education focused on practical skills (housekeeping, needlework); luxury items like music and dance to be reduced. Objective: Marry off before age eighteen, ensuring the bride-price covers accumulated costs with a net surplus. This is an investment, not charity. Emotional factors excluded.

I set down the quill. Outside, an owl screeched in the dark. I stood and walked to my daughters' room.

Anastasia and Charlotte slept together like two kittens, curled up for warmth. I tucked in their blankets, my fingers brushing Anastasia’s cheek—she resembled me, especially around the eyes, though hers lacked the sharp brightness mine had in my youth.

I moved to the adjacent room. Cinderella was asleep too, clutching her rag doll, tear tracks visible on her cheeks—she had fallen while chasing butterflies earlier, scraping her knee, and cried for hours. I stood by the bed, watching her for a long time.

Moonlight filtered through the curtains onto her face. So small, so soft, so blameless.

I reached out, my hand hovering above her cheek, wanting to brush away the tear mark. In the end, I pulled my hand back.

Turning to leave, I heard a quiet, cold thought echo within me:

I am sorry, child. But this world has never offered apologies to the poor.

And so, neither would I.

Chapter 3: Class is a Cage, Marriage is an Escape
Edward passed away late that autumn.

Consumption. He lingered for over half a year, coughing, spitting blood, until he was reduced to skin and bones. Before he drew his last breath, he clutched my hand—his fingers cold as ice. He whispered, "Mary... I am sorry... and Cinderella... I beg of you..."

Before he could finish, he breathed his last. His eyes remained open, staring at the ceiling as if demanding an answer from heaven.

I closed his eyes, my palm catching the last remnants of his warmth, which quickly faded. I sat by the bed for a long time until a servant knocked softly: "Madam, the lawyer is here to read the will."

The will was brief: all property was bequeathed to me until my remarriage or death, after which it would be divided equally among Anastasia, Charlotte, and Cinderella. Fair, yet foolish—he did not realize those "properties" had long shifted to negative numbers.

It rained on the day of the funeral. A fine, persistent drizzle that clung to skin like a damp web. The priest read the eulogy in a flat, monotone voice, as if reading from an account book. Few guests attended, standing in sparse rows with grim expressions—not for Edward, but out of concern for whether the debts owed to them would ever be recovered.

I wore a black gown and a veil, standing erect. Anastasia and Charlotte held my arms on either side, weeping softly. Cinderella stood at the front; she did not cry, but stared intently at the headstone, as if pondering an impossible question.

When the service concluded and the crowd dispersed, Baroness Duval—once a close friend, now distant—approached, took my hand, and sighed: "My condolences, Mary. Things will only grow harder now."

She was not mistaken.

During the first month, three creditors arrived simultaneously. I instructed the servants to serve tea, sat in the main chair, and spread the ledger before them, calculating line by line: Current assets, realizable value, monthly repayments. I spoke slowly and clearly, like a tutor teaching arithmetic. Finally, I said: "If you push me to ruin, you will receive nothing. Give me three years, and I will repay principal and interest. Otherwise, take it to the courts and see if I have the funds to pay legal fees."

They exchanged glances, muttered curses, and departed. But they did not return.

In the second month, the tailor sent a bill for a suit Edward had ordered before his illness. I returned it, paying a cancellation fee that was far less than the full price. I cleared out all of Edward’s wardrobe, selling what could be sold and altering the rest into new dresses for Anastasia and Charlotte. They required respectable attire to step outside, socialize, and eventually... marry.

By the third month, the direst moment arrived: the estate steward resigned, claiming the wages were too meager to support his family. The coachman followed, then the gardener, then the cook. Soon, only old Betty and her lame husband remained—he guarded the gate while she cooked and cleaned.

"Madam, I won't leave," Betty said, wiping her apron with red eyes. "I grew up on the Tremaine estate. The master was good to me. Besides..." She glanced at Cinderella playing in the yard and lowered her voice, "...that child is pitiful. No mother, and now no father. I can't bear to leave her."

I embraced her frail frame. Then I handed her that month's wages along with a small bonus. "Buy some meat," I said. "The girls are growing."

That evening, I added another page to the ledger titled: Cost-Reduction Plan. The left side detailed former staff expenses; the right showed the current figures. The difference was significant, but still insufficient.

I stared at those numbers until my vision blurred. Standing up, I walked to the window. The garden had long gone wild, most of the roses dead, the rest choking on weeds. Under the moonlight, the entire grounds resembled a frayed cloth covered in sores.

A footstep sounded behind me. It was Anastasia, carrying a cup of tea. "Mother, rest now."

I took the cup without drinking, looking at her. In the candlelight, her features appeared gentle, so much like Edward’s. Yet she possessed a quiet resilience he lacked—forged by poverty, like a stone smoothed by a river, stripped of its sharp edges yet growing harder beneath.

"Anastasia," I spoke suddenly, "have you considered marriage?"

She hesitated, her face flushing. "Why do you ask so suddenly, Mother?"

"Answer me."

She lowered her gaze, wringing her fingers. "I have. But... given our family's state, who would marry me? Unless..." She paused, her voice dropping, "...unless I require no dowry, or marry an older man, or..."

"...a widower with children?" I finished for her.

She nodded, tears brimming in her eyes.

A sharp pang struck my chest. I pulled her close, holding her slender shoulders as they trembled softly. "No," I said, my voice steady enough that even I believed it. "My Anastasia will marry a good man and live a good life. I promise you."

Yet how could I promise? With what backing?

I sat in the study until dawn. As the sky grew pale, I opened the window, letting in the autumn chill. In the garden, I saw Cinderella—she always rose early, kneeling by the dead rosebushes, digging in the soil with a small trowel.

Then I saw it.

She dug up a small, rusted iron box. Opening it, she took out a package wrapped in oiled paper. Inside were seeds, labeled carefully: Night-scented stock, violets, daisies...

Accompanied by a card bearing a woman's delicate handwriting: For my Cinderella: When spring arrives, plant these with your father and wait for mother to return. — With endless love, Mother.

Cinderella gazed at the card for a long time. Then she raised her eyes to the pale sky as tears fell silently. She bit her lip hard, refusing to make a sound, until a bead of blood appeared.

I closed the shutter, sat at the desk, opened the ledger, and wrote:

Adjusted Investment Strategy Plan.

Item 1: Anastasia. Strengths: Gentle temperament, skilled needlework, modestly educated. Target profile: Middle-class or above, reputable family, suitable age (up to thirty-five). Investment required: Two ball gowns (est. 80 francs), etiquette instruction (30 francs), social entry fees (20 francs/month). Expected return: Dowry/bride-price no less than 1,000 francs; long-term support for the household.

Item 2: Charlotte. Strengths: Attractive features, skilled in song and dance, lively. Weaknesses: Frail health, delicate. Target profile: Wealthy merchant or lower nobility (indifferent to fragile health). Investment required: Medical care (15 francs/month), refined arts instruction (30 francs/month), cultivating an aura of refined delicacy. Expected return: Bride-price above 1,500 francs (merchants pay a premium for noble grace).

Item 3: Cinderella.

My pen hovered. A drop of ink fell, spreading into a dark stain.

This burden, this girl who consumed my daughters' dowries—how was she to be managed?

Send her to an orphanage? Possible. But she was ten, old enough to remember, old enough to harbor resentment and seek retribution if her fortunes ever turned. Keep her here? She would continue to consume resources and displace Anastasia and Charlotte.

I recalled her expression as she unearthed the seeds, and how she bit her lip to stifle her cries. I remembered Edward’s dying plea: "Cinderella... I beg of you..."

And I saw the red figures glaring from the ledger.

Finally, I wrote:

Item 3: Cinderella. Role: Housemaid (apprentice). Duties: Assist Betty with cleaning, laundry, and kitchen work. Education: Basic literacy only (taught by Anastasia in spare hours); arts instruction suspended. Provisions: Equal to servant standards. Clothing: Altered hand-me-downs from Anastasia and Charlotte. Objective: Train as an efficient maid by age eighteen to reduce domestic labor costs, or marry a tenant farmer/artisan requiring no dowry. Note: Enforce strict discipline to eliminate lingering vanity.

I set down the quill. My hand trembled uncontrollably. Clenching my fist until my nails bit into my palm, I used the sharp pain to suppress the cold nausea rising within me.

I told myself: Mary, you have no choice. You are a mother; you must ensure your own daughters survive with dignity. As for another's child...

Looking out the window, Cinderella remained there, carefully rewrapping the seeds, placing them back in the iron box, and burying it in the earth. She patted the soil flat, pressed her hands together, and closed her eyes as if in prayer.

Praying for what? For spring to arrive early? For the flowers to bloom? For her mother to return?

Foolish child.

In this world, there is no spring—only a long, bitter winter. Flowers bloom only in the gardens of the wealthy. And her mother... her mother was dead, or worse, had abandoned her.

Yet I would not tell her this. Let her keep that small illusion, like those seeds that would never sprout.

At least, before the cold set in, she could still hold onto a dream.

Chapter 4: The Glass Slipper is an Entrance Ticket, the Ball is a Battlefield
Three years felt like thirty.

The estate was sold to clear most of our debts. We moved into a small town house—three stories with a courtyard. Anastasia’s gown was taken in three times as she grew thinner. Charlotte’s medicine remained constant, though her complexion improved slightly from modest food and daily movement. Cinderella... Cinderella grew taller, but thinner still. Her golden hair lost its luster, and her palms developed calluses.

She no longer played the piano, drew, or spoke of her mother. She rose at dawn to light fires, fetch water, sweep, wash, and cook. She learned quickly, like a weed growing from a stone crevice, surviving on soil and water alone. But she rarely spoke. Her eyes were quiet and hollow, like a dry well.

I told myself: This is proper. Only by accepting reality can one endure.

Yet sometimes, late at night, passing the kitchen, I would see her sitting by the hearth, reading an old piece of sheet music by the dying embers—pages scavenged from somewhere, yellowed and missing sections. Her fingers tapped silently against her knee, keeping time. In the glow of the fire, her blue eyes would occasionally flash with a lingering spark.

When she noticed me, she would swiftly hide the music, lowering her head to continue shelling beans, as if nothing had occurred.

I pretended not to notice either.

In the spring, word came that the palace would host a grand ball for the prince to choose a bride. The city erupted with excitement. Fabric prices soared, queues formed at jewelers, and dance instructors were booked weeks in advance. Anastasia and Charlotte were too excited to sleep, crowding into my bed at night, whispering:

"Mother, they say the prince is very handsome!"

"What dress shall I wear? Can last year’s gown be altered?"

"Will we... will we receive an invitation?"

I patted their shoulders, saying, "Yes, we will." But inwardly I was calculating: Two new gowns, at least one hundred francs. Jewelry could be borrowed (Baroness Duval might assist). A carriage rental, twenty francs for one night. Then shoes, gloves, fans...

Another immense expense.

Yet it had to be spent. It was an investment, a gamble—putting every chip on the table to wager for a rise in social standing. If we won, Anastasia or Charlotte would become a princess, elevating the entire family. If we lost, it was merely another debt added to a pile already too large to matter.

I began gathering funds. I pawned the last piece of jewelry I possessed—my mother’s ruby brooch. I approached old acquaintances, enduring cold glares and subtle insults, but managed to collect enough. I bought fashionable silk, cutting and embroidering it myself—working through seven nights until my eyes bloodshot, crafting two gowns that were presentable.

On the eve of the ball, I helped my daughters try on their dresses. Anastasia wore pale blue, enhancing her gentle demeanor; Charlotte wore pale gold, appearing lively and bright. They twirled before the mirror, their skirts fanning out like delicate blossoms.

"Do we look beautiful, Mother?"

"Very beautiful," I adjusted their hair, inserting borrowed pearl pins. "My daughters will be the finest at the ball."

They blushed and smiled, a spark of youthful radiance returning to their eyes—a sight I had not seen in a long time.

Stepping back to observe them, a surge of emotion rose within me—a mixture of pride, sorrow, and grim determination. I thought: Perhaps, just perhaps, fate will offer us a moment of mercy.

Then I noticed Cinderella.

She stood in the shadows near the doorway, holding a flatiron, having just finished pressing their gloves. She looked at Anastasia and Charlotte, at their fine gowns and sparkling ornaments. Her face held no envy or malice, only a deep, quiet stillness.

Yet her eyes—those eyes as blue as her mother's—shone with a startling intensity in the candlelight.

I walked over and took the gloves. "Go to sleep."

She did not move, but looked at me for a long moment. Then she spoke softly: "Madam, I wish to attend the ball as well."

I paused. "What did you say?"

"I wish to attend the ball," she repeated, her voice firmer now, carrying an unusual resolve. "To see the palace, to see the prince, to dance... just once."

I laughed—not out of amusement, but at the absurdity. "Cinderella, do you realize what you are asking? That is a royal ball for noble ladies. What is your standing?"

"I am a daughter of the Tremaine family," she said, holding her posture straight. "My father adopted me; legally, I am."

"Legally?" I stepped closer, dropping my voice so my daughters would not hear. "Law does not put food on your plate or clothes on your back. Law is a game played by the wealthy. Cinderella, know your place. You are a servant, an orphan living under our roof, kept from starving by our charity. The ball? Do you truly believe you belong there?"

The words were harsh, but they were necessary. Her unrealistic notions had to be broken so she would remain dutifully in the kitchen and cause no disruption.

Cinderella turned pale, her face white as parchment. Yet she did not cry; she merely looked at me, the warmth in her blue eyes freezing into ice.

"I understand," she murmured, offering a graceful curtsy—a gesture her mother had taught her years ago. "Goodnight, Madam."

She turned and walked away, her posture straight and her steps measured. But as she reached the stairs, I caught sight of her hand hanging at her side—clenched tightly, her nails biting so deeply into her palm that a tiny bead of blood welled up and dripped onto the floorboard.

Like a small, solitary red petal.

I opened my mouth to call her back, to say something, but no sound came.

That night, I recorded the ball’s expenses in the ledger. Turning to Cinderella’s page, I stared at it for a long time before adding a final note:

Subject shows signs of quiet obstinacy; caution required. Lock doors during the ball to prevent her from slipping out and causing a scene.

Setting down the pen, I listened to the distant church bell toll twelve times into the quiet night.

A new day had begun. An opportunity for Anastasia and Charlotte, and a gamble for myself.

As for Cinderella... her path lay elsewhere. In the kitchen, in the laundry, or wed to an artisan. Unremarkable, perhaps difficult, but safe.

Safety. For a woman, it was the most luxurious yet modest request.

I blew out the candle and lay down. Before closing my eyes, the image of Cinderella’s bleeding hand flickered in my mind once more.

I reassured myself: Mary, you did nothing wrong. You are merely fulfilling a mother’s duty.

Even if that duty felt as cold as the starless sky above.

Epilogue: The Other Slipper
You know how the story ended.

Anastasia and Charlotte were not chosen by the prince. They returned saying he was distant, dancing only a single dance before appearing distracted. Instead, a mysterious maiden arrived, impeccably dressed and dancing with such grace that she captivated the entire hall. But she fled at midnight, leaving behind a single glass slipper.

"The slipper was magnificent," Charlotte sighed, face down on her bed. "Crafted like starlight. I would die happy just to try it on once."

Anastasia remained silent, gazing out the window with a distant expression.

The next day, search parties roamed the city to find the maiden whose foot fit the slipper. When they arrived at our door, my heart hammered—not with hope, but with apprehension. I remembered Cinderella’s bleeding hand, and her quiet words: I wish to attend the ball.

Yet how could it be? She was locked in the attic, dressed in rags, her face smudged with ash. She possessed no gown, no carriage, no invitation. She had nothing.

Except her feet—slender, delicate, despite years of hard labor.

I bade Anastasia and Charlotte try the slipper. They forced their feet inside until they bled, but it would not fit. I knelt before the royal envoy, pleading: "My lord, give them another trial, my daughters can..."

The envoy waved his hand impatiently: "Next!"

Then, a faint song drifted down from the attic.

Soft and delicate—a nursery rhyme Cinderella’s mother had sung to her years ago. I had not heard it in a decade.

Amidst the song, the attic door opened. Cinderella descended, wearing a dress altered from old curtains, her hair neatly combed, her face lightly smudged with ash, but her eyes bright as lit ice. She walked barefoot, step by step, stopping before the envoy.

"My lord," she curtsied with a grace that rendered my daughters silent, "may I try?"

The envoy frowned: "Who are you?"

"A daughter of the Tremaine house, Cinderella," she offered a gentle smile. "My father was Viscount Edward Tremaine, and my mother..." She paused, looking at me with a profound gaze, "...my mother was a lady who loved me dearly."

The envoy hesitated, but duty compelled him to test every maiden. He nodded.

Cinderella sat and extended her foot. It was worn, marked by coal dust and faint scars, yet as she stepped forward, her foot slid effortlessly into the slipper. A perfect fit, neither loose nor tight.

Silence fell over the room.

Then, from her bodice, she drew forth the second slipper—the lost glass slipper, matching the first, sparkling brightly in the sunlight.

"My apologies, my lord," she whispered. "I departed in haste that night and left one behind."

What followed felt like a distant dream. The prince arrived in person, overwhelmed with joy, embracing Cinderella and declaring her his bride. Carriages, fine garments, jewels, and honors flooded into our modest home. Anastasia and Charlotte wept, questioning why it had been her.

I stood in the corner, watching Cinderella. She wore royal silk and diamonds, yet her eyes remained fixed on me. In those blue eyes, there was no triumphant pride or desire for vengeance, only an inscrutable depth I could not read.

She walked over and stopped before me. Then, she did something no one expected.

She knelt. Kneeling before me, she looked up.

"Mother," she spoke clearly, her voice steady, "thank you for all these years of... raising me."

She used the word raising, not housing. She called me Mother, not Madam.

Reaching into her gown, she placed an item into my palm. It was the packet of flower seeds, wrapped in oiled paper, preserved carefully though worn by time.

"Before father passed, he asked me to give this to you," she said softly. "He said you loved roses best. When spring arrives and they bloom in the courtyard, he will smell them from heaven."

I looked down at the packet. On the paper was Edward’s handwriting, faint and trembling, yet legible:

For Mary: I am sorry, and I love you. — Forever yours, Edward.

I gripped the paper tightly, its edges pressing into my palm. I lifted my head to speak, but my throat felt constricted, unable to utter a single word.

Cinderella stood, turned, and walked toward the prince. He took her hand and assisted her into the carriage. Just before the door closed, she turned back and cast one final glance at me.

It was a brief glance, lasting no longer than a sigh. Yet it was long enough to remain in my memory for the rest of my days.

The carriage departed, and the crowd dispersed. Anastasia and Charlotte continued to weep while Betty comforted them. I stood alone, clutching the packet of seeds and Edward’s final words.

Walking back to the study, I sat down and opened the ledger. Flipping to the last page, I saw the recorded expenses for the ball, the strategic plans, and every cold calculation I had made.

Taking up the quill, I wrote in the blank space:

Today, Cinderella Tremaine married the prince to become a royal bride. Return on investment: Incalculable. Note: All my life, I believed in reason, in calculation, and that survival preceded all else. I treated marriage as a enterprise, my daughters as assets, and sentiment as a liability. I miscalculated the vineyard harvest, the interest on our debts, and the resilience of the human spirit. But my greatest error was failing to see that the girl I deemed a burden carried seeds within her heart. While I guarded my ledger, believing it to be the entire world.

I set down the pen. Outside, the sky cleared. Sunlight poured in, falling across the ledger, blurring the dense rows of numbers until they dissolved like snow in spring.

I closed the ledger and locked it in the drawer, throwing the key into the hearth to watch it melt into a twisted piece of metal.

Then I walked out into the courtyard. The earth was damp, carrying the lingering chill of winter. Kneeling down, I dug a small hollow with my hands, placed the seeds inside, covered them with soil, and patted it firm.

Betty approached, asking softly: "Madam, what are you planting?"

"Roses," I answered, my voice raspy. "When spring comes, they will bloom."

She nodded without further question and turned to her work. I remained kneeling, my hands resting on the cold earth, sensing something slow and quiet awakening beneath.

Distant bells chimed from the direction of the palace, celebrating the new royal bride, a storied romance, and the promise of a happy ending.

And I sat in this worn courtyard, watching over seeds newly sown that might or might not sprout, beside two unmarried daughters, holding the quiet empty-handedness left behind by half a lifetime of calculation.

Yet curiously, my mind felt tranquil—like a still lake in late autumn, undisturbed by any ripple.

Perhaps it was because I no longer needed to calculate.

Perhaps it was because I finally acknowledged that certain things in this world defy calculation: how much truth or remorse lay in Edward’s dying words; whether resentment dwelt in Cinderella’s eyes when she knelt and called me "Mother"; or whether those seeds were truly left by Edward, or a gentle falsehood crafted by Cinderella to offer me a final measure of dignity.

Or perhaps I was simply tired—too tired to calculate, to contend, or to prove that I was right, strong, and unyielding.

I stood and wiped the soil from my hands. Traces of dark earth remained beneath my nails, stubborn and clinging, as if grown into the skin.

Let them remain.

As a quiet keepsake. A keepsake of the girl who climbed out from hardship believing beauty and cunning could conquer the world; of the woman who buried love, conscience, and gentleness within a ledger; and of this spring morning, where calculation finally came to an end.

A breeze drifted past, carrying the sweet scent of roses from a neighboring garden, rich and thick.

I raised my head, closed my eyes, and took a deep breath.

It was truly sweet.

Sweet as a dream from which one never needs to awaken.

Afterword: Beyond the Ledger
In fairy tales, the stepmother is framed as a flat antagonist, the foil to a young girl's romantic fantasy. Yet placed within her historical context—18th-century Europe, a widowed noblewoman with two unwed daughters facing social decline and bankruptcy—her severity assumes a desperate rationality.

She was not born cruel; she was driven by her era to become an accountant. In a society where women possessed no inheritance or independent livelihood, and where marriage was the sole vehicle for security, she conducted her life like a ledger—calculating returns on every expense and weighing the practical value of every sentiment. Her harshness toward Cinderella was fundamentally a struggle over scarce resources: a single dowry had to be reserved for her own blood, lest the entire family sink into ruin.

Tragically, she internalized the logic of a patriarchal society, becoming an instrument of the very system that constrained her. The standard she imposed on Cinderella was the same one imposed on her: that beauty must be exchanged for status, and a woman's worth defined by marriage. She viewed the glass slipper as a ticket for upward mobility, unaware that the slipper itself formed a cage.

Cinderella’s triumph reflects the grace of a fairy tale, yet carries its own irony—she still required a prince for rescue, her worth validated through marriage. The fitting slipper brought fortune, but also a new set of expectations.

True redemption begins with those seeds—with the small, subtle kindnesses that can endure between women beneath layers of calculation and hardship. It begins with acknowledging that life is not a ledger, and that certain things—remorse, forgiveness, and the courage to plant roses among ruins—defy measurement.

Whether spring will arrive or the flowers will bloom remains uncertain.

Yet someone planted the seeds.

And beyond the fairy-tale ending, beneath the promise of "happily ever after," a woman sits by the soil, waiting.

Waiting for a tomorrow where one may stand firm, even without a glass slipper.
