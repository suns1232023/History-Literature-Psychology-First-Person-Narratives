# What Is Carved into the Bone

### A Hongdong Man Remembers Yutangchun

> *“There was never such a thing as ‘no good people in Hongdong.’
> There were only people placing their own bets.”*

— **Inscription**

My name is Li Mancang. I am seventy-three years old, and my family has lived inside the South Gate of Hongdong County for generations. For three generations, my family has made a living running a small general store. We sell sewing thread, scraps of cloth, lamp oil, incense paper—whatever the neighbors need.

I started standing behind the counter when I was fourteen.

Nearly sixty years later, I am still here.

The county gazetteers contain all sorts of things I don't understand: Wang Yexiu's compilation in the thirty-third year of the Jiajing reign, the preface to the customs section written by Jin Chaochen, records of merchants, examinations, migrations, and the changing habits of the people.

I am no scholar.

But I know people.

And I saw Yutangchun.

No. To be precise, I saw that woman.

I saw her prison cart thunder down the long street. I saw the straight line of her back as she knelt in court and cried out that she had been wronged. I saw her hands trembling inside the wooden stocks as she pressed her fingerprint onto the confession.

I was eighteen that year.

I had just married. I was young enough to believe I understood the world, and proud enough to think I could stand up to anything.

Now, six decades later, when I think back on that case, that woman, and the way an entire county treated her, I finally understand what it means for something to be **carved into the bone**.

Over the years, I have seen all kinds of people.

Rich people. Poor people. Officials. Merchants. Beggars. Shopkeepers.

But the person who finally taught me what human beings are capable of was that woman.

And the case that swallowed her life.

Today, I want to tell it from the beginning.

---

## I. When She Arrived, Business Was Booming

In those years, Hongdong was a prosperous place.

To the north stood the ancient locust tree associated with the great migration from Hongdong. To the south ran the Fen River, broad and brown beneath the sky. Shops crowded against one another along the streets, their signboards almost touching.

People said Hongdong had prospered from the state's salt policies and the opportunities they created. Merchant families accumulated wealth and then turned toward education and officialdom. The economy changed. Society changed. Even people's manners changed.

My father put it more simply:

> “Hongdong people have silver in their hands, appearances in their eyes, and etiquette on their tongues.”

He was right.

I grew up in that Hongdong.

So when a wealthy merchant named Shen brought a famous courtesan back from Beijing and installed her as his concubine, the entire county became curious.

Who wouldn't?

For weeks, customers came into our shop talking about her.

“I heard Shen Yuanwai paid thousands of taels to bring Yutangchun back from the Suhuai courtyard in Beijing.”

“Yutangchun? You mean Su San?”

“That's her. The famous one.”

“People say she once sang before the emperor himself.”

I was young, but I remember those conversations clearly.

Every shop on our street had something to say.

The silk merchant called it “a romantic affair.”

The barber called it “the way merchants behave.”

Old Wang, who sold tofu, muttered once:

> “Poor girl. Who knows what she's been through?”

But before he could say more, someone interrupted him:

> “What does it have to do with us? We didn't buy her.”

You see, that was the first lesson I learned.

**When something brings you neither profit nor loss, the human mind is lazy.**

Someone else is involved. Someone else is in trouble. You listen, you gossip, you make a few remarks, and then you go back to your own life.

It isn't always cruelty.

Sometimes it is simply that the suffering of another person never enters your calculation.

Years later, a scholar explained it to me with an example that I have never forgotten:

> **The evidence you happen to sample determines the world you think you are seeing.**

At eighteen, I didn't understand what he meant.

Now I do.

---

## II. The Self-Truncated Sample

Then disaster came to the Shen household.

Shen Yuanwai died suddenly.

His principal wife accused Yutangchun of poisoning him.

The case went from the county office to the prefectural authorities. Pressure came down from above.

And Yutangchun was an outsider.

A former courtesan.

A woman without family in the county, without powerful relatives, without anyone willing to stand beside her.

What do you think happened?

**She was beaten until she confessed.**

The bamboo boards came down.

The interrogation continued.

The confession was written.

Eventually, she signed it.

Years later, an old constable who had once served in the county yamen told me what he had seen.

He said:

> “That woman was stubborn. Harder than anyone I had seen. When I brought her food, she spat out half of her own ear. The interrogation had torn it almost completely away.”

I never forgot that.

Many years later, I met a scholar who had studied Western psychology.

He told me about something called **self-truncated sampling**.

It took me a long time to understand.

He said that when people form judgments, they rarely examine the whole field of available evidence. They stop sampling when they feel they have enough.

The problem is that “enough” is not always determined by truth.

Sometimes it is determined by effort.

Sometimes by fear.

Sometimes by social pressure.

And sometimes simply by the fact that we do not care enough to look further.

When he explained it that way, something inside me suddenly clicked.

Think about how the people of Hongdong judged Yutangchun.

Who had actually heard her side of the story?

Who had spoken with her?

Who had entered the Shen household and examined what happened there?

Almost no one.

We knew very little.

Yet everyone felt qualified to judge.

Shen's principal wife was one of our own.

Yutangchun was an outsider.

Someone would say:

> “The man is dead. Someone has to take the blame. If it isn't her, then who?”

It sounds cruel.

But perhaps something even more disturbing was happening.

**People were choosing the explanation that required the least effort.**

It wasn't necessary to investigate.

It wasn't necessary to ask questions.

It wasn't necessary to risk anything.

For most people, the case was not theirs.

So why spend the time?

The scholar called it a kind of **self-truncation**: we stop looking when the cost of acquiring more information becomes greater than the benefit we expect from knowing the truth.

That realization frightened me.

We weren't necessarily evil.

We simply didn't want to pay the price of being good.

Because being good costs something.

It might cost your reputation.

Your livelihood.

Your relationships.

Your safety.

Sometimes, even your life.

---

## III. “There Are No Good People in Hongdong”

Eventually, the case reached the hands of a powerful judicial official from outside the county.

Wang Jinglong—the man who had once loved Yutangchun—had risen through the examinations and eventually returned as an investigating official.

He reopened the case.

The evidence was examined.

The original judgment collapsed.

The people responsible were punished.

The woman who had spent years in prison, who had nearly died in a case built on a false accusation, was finally released.

And then she left.

That is the part the opera remembers.

The woman is rescued.

The lovers are reunited.

The injustice is corrected.

The curtain falls.

But did anyone from Hongdong go to see her?

No.

Did anyone stand in the street and say:

> “She was innocent”?

No.

Not one person.

Later, people in Beijing heard a story about her.

A play was written.

Then another.

Eventually came the famous line:

> **“Su San leaves Hongdong County…”**

And later, the accusation that would haunt our county for centuries:

> **“There are no good people in Hongdong.”**

People here were furious.

I remember Zhang the butcher slamming his cup onto a tavern table one winter evening.

“Rubbish!” he shouted. “What's Hongdong ever done to her? It was the yamen that judged her, not me!”

I said nothing.

But I thought:

Yes.

You didn't judge her.

**You just never spoke.**

You heard the gossip.

You listened when people called her a wicked woman.

You heard people say she deserved what happened.

And you never once said:

> “Perhaps we don't know the whole story.”

That was us.

We weren't heroes.

We weren't villains.

We were floating weeds on water, drifting toward whichever direction demanded the least resistance.

But there was something deeper.

We were afraid that sympathy itself would lower our own social standing.

She was a courtesan.

A concubine.

An outsider.

If you defended her, what would people think of you?

Would they begin to wonder about you?

Would your neighbors gossip?

Would your business suffer?

So we did something remarkably efficient.

We closed our mouths.

That was our real social boundary.

Not ignorance.

Not hatred.

**Fear.**

---

## IV. What Is Carved into the Bone

Over the years, I began to think about something.

Being wronged is not extraordinary.

Who among us has never been misunderstood?

But what happened to Yutangchun was different.

She had been treated as though she were less than human.

As though her suffering did not belong to the world we inhabited.

My father once taught me something.

He said:

> “When a leaf falls in front of your eyes, you know it has fallen. If you never see it, then for you, it is as though it never happened.”

I understand that now.

Yutangchun suffered in Hongdong.

But we did not see it.

We did not see her being beaten.

We did not see her lying in the prison eating spoiled food.

We did not see the rats crawling across her face.

We did not see her fever.

We did not see the wounds becoming infected.

We did not hear her cry through the night.

Only when the case was reopened did we suddenly recognize her as a human being.

Before that, she was something else.

A scandal.

A courtesan.

A convenient suspect.

A story to tell over wine.

The scholar once explained the principle to me this way:

> **What we choose to see determines the world we inhabit. What we refuse to see does not disappear—it simply ceases to belong to our moral universe.**

That, I think, was the real distance between Hongdong and Yutangchun.

She lived inside our county.

But she did not live inside our world.

---

## V. The Moment Something Becomes Carved into You

Half a year ago, an old nun at the Guanyin Temple said something that stayed with me.

She said:

> “Peace of mind does not come from the bad things you avoided doing. It comes from remembering how many good things you failed to do.”

I thought about that for days.

Then I understood.

I was one of those people.

Twenty years after Yutangchun left Hongdong, I dreamed about her.

She was still wearing the bloodstained prison clothes.

She looked at me and asked:

> “Why didn't you say one word for me?”

I woke up terrified.

And I asked myself:

Why didn't I?

I was eighteen.

I thought I was fearless.

But I had said nothing.

Eventually, I understood why.

**Because speaking carried a greater risk than silence.**

If I had spoken for that woman, my father might have beaten me.

My neighbors might have shunned me.

People might have whispered behind my back.

What would I have gained?

A sense of righteousness.

What would I have lost?

My reputation.

My livelihood.

Perhaps my marriage.

Justice could not buy rice.

So I kept silent.

That, I think, was the secret behind the silence of an entire county.

**If you say nothing, you lose nothing.
If you speak, you risk everything.**

And so Yutangchun counted the iron links of her chains at night, wondering how many days she had left to live.

While we went about our ordinary lives.

We bought cloth.

We drank wine.

We bargained over prices.

We laughed.

We praised someone's new coat.

Our worlds continued as though nothing had happened.

I had seen constables beat people before.

Once, I watched a thief being whipped in the street.

I turned away.

I told myself:

He is a thief.

He deserves it.

But after Yutangchun's case was overturned, that excuse became impossible.

She had done nothing wrong.

And suddenly I had nowhere to put my conscience.

That was when something changed.

Not all at once.

It accumulated.

Year after year.

Memory after memory.

Until one evening, when I was already in my thirties, I stood outside the county yamen at sunset.

And I remembered a single object.

A shoe.

A little embroidered shoe that had fallen from Yutangchun's clothing as she was dragged into the courthouse.

I had seen it that day.

It lay alone on the stone pavement.

The sunlight caught the silk surface.

For some reason, I had never forgotten it.

People walked past.

Everyone stepped around it.

No one stopped.

**That shoe stayed inside me.**

Decades later, it became a thorn.

It pierced the complacency I had built around myself.

It pierced my ordinary life.

It pierced the comfortable belief that being neither cruel nor courageous was somehow enough.

From that moment on, I could no longer pretend that I had been a good man.

But neither could I pretend I had been entirely innocent.

---

## VI. When Silence Becomes a System

Now I understand something else.

Yutangchun's tragedy did not begin when Shen Yuanwai died.

It did not begin in the courtroom.

It began much earlier.

It began in the thousands of tiny moments when ordinary people decided:

> “This is not my business.”

One person turns away.

Another remains silent.

A third repeats a rumor.

A fourth decides that an outsider is probably guilty.

A fifth sees the injustice but decides not to become involved.

None of them believes they are killing anyone.

Yet together, their choices create a world in which someone can be destroyed without resistance.

That is what frightens me.

**A system does not always need evil people to produce an evil outcome.**

Sometimes it only needs enough ordinary people making individually reasonable decisions.

That is what happened in Hongdong.

Each person protected himself.

Together, we abandoned her.

---

## VII. Dawn

A few days ago, I asked my eldest grandson to look in the provincial library.

I wanted to know how much of the Yutangchun story was history, how much was opera, and how much had been changed by generations of retelling.

He brought back books.

I read slowly.

My eyes are old now.

There was a sentence that stayed with me:

> “When history is examined only through isolated incidents, one can easily mistake the fragment for the whole.”

Perhaps that is what we did.

We saw a woman.

We saw a rumor.

We saw an outsider.

We saw a convenient explanation.

And then we stopped looking.

I have lived seventy-three years.

I have spent nearly sixty of them behind the same counter.

But I still carry the thing I failed to say when I was eighteen.

So when people say:

> “There were no good people in Hongdong,”

I no longer feel offended.

I accept the accusation.

Because I was there.

I saw her.

And I said nothing.

The north wind is blowing tonight.

The branches of the jujube tree outside my window have lost half their leaves.

There is barely enough ink left in my bottle to finish this page.

So I will write the words I should have spoken six decades ago.

**She was innocent.**

I should have said it then.

I say it now.

Perhaps it is too late.

It is certainly too late for her.

But perhaps words spoken late are still better than silence carried forever.

People like to say:

> “I am a good person.”

I no longer trust that sentence.

Goodness that never speaks may be indistinguishable from indifference.

Justice that never risks anything may be nothing more than a private feeling.

And compassion that costs nothing may simply be sentiment.

I am seventy-three years old.

I have spent a lifetime thinking about one woman, one prison cart, one courtroom, and one little embroidered shoe lying alone in the sun.

I finally understand what was carved into the bone.

Not her suffering.

**Our silence.**

That is what remained.

That is what we inherited.

That is what we must remember.

I am Li Mancang.

Seventy-three.

A shopkeeper from Hongdong County.

And this is my confession.

> **“Su San left Hongdong County, walking alone into the street…”**

Many people can sing that line.

Far fewer are willing to ask what they themselves did while she was being taken away.

I know what I did.

I looked.

And I said nothing.

**That is my story.**

**Li Mancang, Hongdong County.
Seventy-three years old.
Guilty.**
