# **Jiaoxing: A Maidservant’s Ascent and a Meditation on Value**

### *The Accidental Investment That Changed Two Women’s Fates*

> *I was once only a maidservant in the Zhen household, known as Jiaxing. One year, I happened to look twice at a down-and-out scholar. And now, by a twist of fate, I have become the wife of a prefect, with a lady’s rank bestowed upon me.*
>
> *Yet our young mistress Yinglian, the legitimate daughter of the Zhen family, fell from the clouds into the mud. She became a concubine, a servant, and endured humiliation and suffering.*
>
> *On the chessboard of fate, one move was enough to make the difference between heaven and earth.*
>
> *Lately, I have overheard my husband and his advisers talking about “value investing,” “investment opportunities,” and “risk-adjusted returns.” It suddenly occurred to me that my entire life resembles one absurdly precise investment—except that when I made my first move, I had no idea I was making a bet at all.*

## I. Initial Endowment: Two Radically Different Balance Sheets

**A certain year of the Qianlong reign, Fifth day of the Third Month. Clear.**

Today I was sorting through some old things and found a faded red handkerchief—the kind Mistress Zhao once gave me when I was still in the Zhen household.

As I rubbed the coarse fabric between my fingers, the past returned like smoke.

I often wonder: if one were to imagine life as a company, what would the balance sheets of Miss Yinglian and me have looked like at the beginning?

The difference would have been enormous.

### Miss Yinglian’s balance sheet

Her **asset side** was extraordinarily strong.

She came from a respectable and prosperous family. Her father, Zhen Shiyin, was a man of standing in the local community. She was described as beautiful, clever, lively, and utterly cherished by her parents.

She was, in modern financial language, a **blue-chip asset** from birth.

Her expected future returns—marriage, family resources, social status—appeared remarkably secure.

Her **liabilities were minimal**.

Her parents were alive. Her household was harmonious. There were no obvious debts, no visible threats, no immediate exposure to catastrophic risk.

Her **moat was wide**:

family reputation, parental protection, social status, and the security of a wealthy household.

And then there was me.

### My balance sheet

Mine was miserable.

My **assets were thin**.

My parents were dead. I had no family fortune, no education, no property, no meaningful social standing. My only assets were youth, health, and the ability to work.

My **liabilities were enormous**.

I was a servant. My freedom was restricted. My future income was negligible and entirely dependent on the household that employed me. I possessed virtually no bargaining power and no protection against adversity.

My **moat was nonexistent**.

I could be replaced at any time.

Yet psychology tells us something strange: our initial circumstances do not merely determine what we possess. They shape the way we perceive risk itself.

Miss Yinglian grew up surrounded by security and affection. Her psychological account was full.

She could afford to trust the world.

Her risk preference may therefore have leaned toward what we might now call a **growth orientation**: an expectation that tomorrow would be better than today, accompanied by little reason to anticipate a black swan.

That very security, however, contained a hidden vulnerability.

Her sense of identity was deeply tied to her family. Once the family collapsed, so did the psychological foundation on which that identity rested.

I was different.

I grew up close to the survival line. My psychological account was permanently depleted, filled with uncertainty.

My risk preference was therefore profoundly conservative—perhaps even fearful.

My first objective was not growth.

It was survival.

I did not dare dream beyond my station.

My sense of self-worth was small. I learned dependence. I learned obedience. I learned to adapt.

And strangely enough, that made me more like grass than a tree.

A tree can be uprooted by a storm.

Grass bends.

Then rises again.

### The first lesson of value investing

**Understand the difference between intrinsic value and market price.**

Miss Yinglian possessed enormous intrinsic value.

But the “market price” assigned to a woman like her depended almost entirely on the institution that protected her: her family.

Once that market failed—once she was abducted—her price was determined by a far darker market: trafficking, violence, and the power of wealthy households.

Her intrinsic value remained.

But the system no longer allowed that value to be recognized.

My own intrinsic value, by contrast, was severely undervalued.

And then, by pure accident, I entered a market in which I could be “repriced”: the personal emotions, memories, and moral narratives of Jia Yucun.

# II. The Critical Decision: An Unconscious Asymmetric Bet

**A certain year of the Qianlong reign, Tenth day of the Fifth Month. Oppressively hot.**

Today my husband was speaking with several merchants from Jiangnan.

They discussed venture capital, angel investments, odds, and returns.

I stood nearby pouring tea.

And suddenly I thought of that moment.

The moment that changed my life.

The moment when I looked back at the impoverished scholar.

The more I think about it, the more it resembles what modern investors might call an **asymmetric bet**.

### The setting

The Zhen household study.

Jia Yucun was desperately poor, yet my master treated him with unusual kindness.

### My cost

Almost nothing.

A glance.

A few seconds.

A little curiosity.

No money.

No reputation at stake.

No expectation of emotional return.

### The potential upside

At the time, almost zero.

What connection could there possibly be between a servant girl and an impoverished scholar?

It was like paying a single coin for a lottery ticket whose odds of winning were almost infinitely small.

And yet the structure of the bet was radically asymmetric.

The downside was negligible.

The upside, however improbable, was enormous.

At worst, perhaps, I might be scolded for being impertinent.

At best?

Years later, I would become the wife of a prefect.

### Why did I make the bet?

Not because I possessed superior information.

Quite the opposite.

I knew almost nothing.

I knew nothing about Jia Yucun’s “fundamentals”—his ambition, intelligence, or political instincts.

I knew nothing about the “market”—the imperial examination system, officialdom, or the mechanisms by which a poor scholar might rise.

My glance was nothing more than primitive curiosity.

My master cared about this man.

I wanted to know why.

Nor did I consciously identify risk.

I had lived in an environment where insults, punishments, and uncertainty were ordinary. Small behavioral risks barely registered.

In that sense, my action resembles an accidental form of **deep-value investing**.

The market—the Zhen household—was not paying attention to the poor scholar.

I encountered him for reasons that had almost nothing to do with investment.

And yet that encounter established something crucial:

a connection.

A tiny connection.

Almost invisible.

But real.

### Miss Yinglian’s “decision” was completely different.

Her abduction was not an investment.

It was **forced exposure to catastrophic risk**.

The decision-makers were others:

Huo Qi through negligence, and the kidnapper through deliberate exploitation.

The risk was existential:

loss of identity, freedom, family, and physical safety.

There was no meaningful hedge.

The Lantern Festival crowds were chaotic. Her protection depended upon a single adult. The institutional safeguards were practically nonexistent.

It was like holding an enormously valuable asset in a market without property rights, contract enforcement, or security—and then leaving it exposed to theft.

### The second lesson of value investing

**Look for asymmetry, but insist upon a margin of safety.**

My “investment” succeeded because the cost of the initial move was almost zero.

But its success was accidental and cannot be replicated simply by copying the behavior.

Yinglian’s tragedy was the opposite:

her margin of safety was effectively zero.

All her beauty, intelligence, family status, and potential could be destroyed by a single systemic shock.

# III. Market Validation and the Illusion of the Moat

**A certain year of the Qianlong reign, Fifteenth day of the Seventh Month. Ghost Festival. Overcast.**

Today, while burning offerings to the ancestors, I thought of the great fire that destroyed the Zhen household.

Even the strongest walls cannot withstand an unexpected fire.

Perhaps that is what a moat really is:

something that looks permanent until the environment changes.

Miss Yinglian’s moat—family, parents, status—was **externally supplied and environment-dependent**.

Once the environment changed, the moat disappeared.

Abduction.

Fire.

Displacement.

Loss of family.

Each event drained another section of the moat.

Her intrinsic qualities—beauty and intelligence—did not protect her.

In the wrong market, they became liabilities.

Her beauty increased her vulnerability to exploitation.

Her intelligence could not compensate for the absence of legal protection.

Her value could not be converted into well-being.

Instead, it continuously depreciated.

Jia Yucun later told me that when he handled the case involving Xue Pan and Feng Yuan, the legal clerk showed him the famous **“Official Protection Talisman.”**

It listed the powerful families who could not easily be challenged.

That talisman was, in effect, a map of the political moats protecting the wealthy.

Yinglian’s value was sacrificed to that system.

Her tragedy demonstrated something more fundamental:

**when the rules of a market are distorted by power, conventional definitions of value become almost meaningless.**

And what was my moat?

At first, nothing.

After Jia Yucun recognized me, my moat became something much more fragile:

**symbolic value inside his personal narrative.**

I was the servant girl who had once looked back at him.

In his memory, that glance could become evidence of discernment.

Perhaps I was, in his mind, the woman who had recognized the future before anyone else did.

That story gave me value.

But it was an extremely fragile moat.

It depended upon his memory, his emotions, and his need to construct a moral narrative about his own rise.

After I became his wife, my moat changed again.

It became social identity.

I was now the wife of a prefect.

I learned to manage the household carefully.

I treated servants well.

I maintained relationships with official families.

I performed the role expected of me.

I tried to widen the moat.

But the foundation remained unstable.

My security still depended upon someone else.

Psychologically, this produced something resembling what modern psychology might call **impostor syndrome**.

I knew that my position had not been built upon an independent, durable competence.

I often felt that I did not truly deserve what I possessed.

My security was borrowed.

My valuation could be revised whenever my husband’s feelings changed.

### The third lesson of value investing

**Examine where the moat comes from—and whether it can survive a change in environment.**

Yinglian’s moat was protection by others.

Mine was dependence upon another person.

Both were fragile.

A truly durable moat must arise from internal competitive advantage—knowledge, capability, reputation, innovation, cost advantage, or something else that remains valuable when circumstances change.

Neither of us possessed such a moat.

# IV. Psychological Bias and the Parable of Mr. Market

**A certain year of the Qianlong reign, Ninth day of the Ninth Month. Double Ninth Festival. Cool wind.**

Today I climbed high with my husband.

He pointed toward the marketplace below and said:

“Look at those people. Chasing prices up, selling in panic when they fall. They are just like Mr. Market.”

I was startled.

Because suddenly I realized that Yinglian’s fate and mine had both been determined by different versions of Mr. Market.

### Yinglian’s Mr. Market

**The trafficking market**

It was irrational, predatory, and cruel.

Her value was reduced to that of a young woman who could be bought and sold.

Her intrinsic humanity was irrelevant.

Her price was determined by greed and violence.

**The Xue household**

This was a distorted market of domestic power.

Her value depended upon Xue Pan’s desire, Xia Jingui’s jealousy, and the helplessness of Xue Aunt.

Price and value diverged dramatically.

And the longer she remained there, the more her actual value was destroyed.

**The judicial market**

In Jia Yucun’s courtroom, justice itself became a commodity.

Yinglian was no longer a person.

She became a political liability.

Her suffering had a price.

Her rescue had a political cost.

Her truth competed against the interests of powerful families.

And truth lost.

Psychologically, Yinglian’s condition may resemble what modern psychology calls **learned helplessness**, together with elements of **dissociation**.

After repeated experiences of uncontrollable trauma, a person may stop expecting that action can change the outcome.

Forgetting can sometimes become a form of psychological survival.

Perhaps the girl called Yinglian had to disappear before the woman called Xiangling could survive.

She could not negotiate rationally with these different versions of Mr. Market.

She could only endure their quotations.

### My own Mr. Market

**The labor market of the Zhen household**

Stable, but exploitative.

My price—wages, treatment, social status—was low.

My bargaining power was negligible.

**Jia Yucun’s personal emotional market**

This was something like a private placement.

His valuation of me rested on a beautiful misunderstanding:

my glance became “foresight.”

My ordinary curiosity became “recognition.”

My existence became part of his story about himself.

He did not merely marry me.

He incorporated me into his personal narrative.

I became an asset in his moral accounting.

**The official-family social market**

This market was more orderly, but also more status-conscious.

My value depended upon my husband’s rank and my ability to perform the role of an official’s wife.

My psychological response was therefore one of strong **external locus of control**.

I believed that fate depended on powerful people and chance.

My strategy was caution.

Adaptation.

Obedience.

Preservation of the existing valuation.

I never imagined myself as an independent value investor.

I had no capital.

### Graham’s Mr. Market

Benjamin Graham’s famous metaphor tells us that the investor should exploit Mr. Market’s irrational quotations rather than become enslaved by them.

Yinglian was consumed by Mr. Market.

I was different only in appearance.

I was “purchased” at an enormous premium by a man who needed a particular story to explain his own rise.

I did not exploit him.

I became an instrument in his narrative.

That distinction matters.

A high valuation is not necessarily independence.

Sometimes it is simply dependence wearing the clothes of privilege.

# V. What Value Investors Can Learn from a “Jiaxing Opportunity”

**A certain year of the Qianlong reign, Twenty-third day of the Twelfth Month. Little New Year. Snow.**

The household accounts were being closed for the year.

My husband was pleased with the year’s “investments.”

I looked out at the snow and wondered:

If a value investor could somehow travel back to our time and observe Yinglian and me, what would he learn?

And how might he recognize a “Jiaxing opportunity” without repeating Yinglian’s tragedy?

The answer, I think, contains several lessons.

---

## 1. Study the fundamentals—but study the ecosystem first.

Yinglian had excellent individual fundamentals.

Beauty.

Intelligence.

Family background.

Education.

Affection.

But a rational investor cannot evaluate an asset independently of the system surrounding it.

A beautiful company operating in a market without property rights, contract enforcement, or rule of law is not necessarily a good investment.

The same was true of Yinglian.

Her environment was structurally dangerous.

Women possessed limited autonomy.

Kidnapping existed.

Legal protection was weak.

Powerful families could influence judicial outcomes.

Her individual quality could not compensate for a defective institutional environment.

### The lesson:

**Study the ecosystem before the asset.**

A high-quality individual in a predatory system may be more vulnerable, not less.

Unless you possess the ability to change the system itself, the environment may dominate the fundamentals.

Jia Yucun could have changed the outcome.

He chose not to.

---

# 2. Seek asymmetry and deep value—but demand a very large margin of safety.

My “investment” was almost free.

One glance.

One moment.

Almost no downside.

Potentially enormous upside.

That is the structure of an asymmetric opportunity.

But it was accidental.

It was not a repeatable investment strategy.

A genuine value investor seeks opportunities where:

**market price << intrinsic value**

while simultaneously ensuring that the downside is limited.

The critical question is not simply:

> “How much can I gain?”

It is:

> “What happens if I am wrong?”

My life happened to contain an enormous upside with almost no initial cost.

Yinglian’s life contained enormous potential but almost no protection against catastrophic downside.

The difference is not intelligence.

It is **risk architecture**.

---

# 3. Distinguish luck from skill—and build a portfolio accordingly.

If I were honest, I would say:

**Ninety-nine percent of my success was luck.**

Perhaps one percent was my behavior that day.

I looked back.

That was all.

I had no investment thesis.

No valuation model.

No information advantage.

No plan.

Yet the outcome was extraordinary.

That is dangerous because humans are naturally tempted to confuse successful outcomes with successful decisions.

A rational investor should therefore separate:

**decision quality**

from

**outcome quality**.

A lucky decision can produce a great outcome.

A sound decision can produce a terrible outcome.

The difference becomes obvious only over repeated trials.

Therefore, the prudent investor should seek repeatable judgment, not merely memorable success.

Diversification is another defense against uncertainty.

Do not confuse one extraordinary lucky outcome with a reproducible strategy.

---

# 4. Beware narratives and valuation bubbles.

Jia Yucun’s valuation of me was built upon a story.

The story was beautiful.

Perhaps I had “recognized” him when others had not.

Perhaps my glance proved that I possessed unusual discernment.

Perhaps I represented the one person who had believed in him before he became successful.

But stories are dangerous foundations for valuation.

What happens when the story changes?

What happens when the person no longer needs it?

What happens when another person fits the narrative better?

Then the valuation may collapse.

This applies to investments as much as to people.

A company whose valuation rests entirely upon a compelling story, rather than sustainable cash flow, competitive advantage, and durable fundamentals, is vulnerable to repricing.

### The lesson:

**Do not pay for a story when you can evaluate the underlying economics.**

A beautiful narrative is not a moat.

---

# 5. The final question: Does investment have a moral dimension?

This is the question that troubles me most.

Jia Yucun gained political capital by sacrificing Yinglian.

Her intrinsic value was immense.

But within the political market, her value was discounted to almost nothing.

His gain depended upon her loss.

What kind of “investment” is that?

What kind of market produces profit by destroying human value?

Perhaps value investing should contain a moral question that goes beyond financial return.

Not merely:

> What is this asset worth?

But:

> **What kind of value am I helping to create?**

And:

> **What kind of system am I helping to sustain?**

A genuinely constructive investment should create value rather than merely extract it from a distorted system.

It should improve the ecosystem rather than profit from its defects.

Perhaps the highest form of value investing is therefore not simply buying undervalued assets.

It is helping create a market in which real value has a better chance of being recognized.

---

# VI. Epilogue: An Apricot Blossom in the Wind

The snow was falling quietly.

I closed my diary.

I, Jiaxing, may be one of the rare “winners” in this game of fate.

But I know how fragile my victory is.

My life was built upon an accident.

A misunderstanding.

A memory.

A moment of luck.

I possessed neither the vision nor the certainty of a true value investor.

I was merely a bead thrown from the machinery of fate, which happened to land on silk rather than in the mud.

And Yinglian?

She was a priceless jewel.

Yet she was thrown into the filthiest part of the market.

Her brilliance was trampled.

Her value was destroyed.

Her story became one of the most painful examples of **value destruction** imaginable.

If a value investor could learn anything from us, perhaps it would be this:

Before investing, ask yourself:

**Is the market I am entering worthy of the word “value”?**

**Can the value I assign withstand the test of morality and time?**

**Is the opportunity I am pursuing the result of genuine insight and fair exchange—or merely luck, exploitation, or complicity?**

As for me, I shall spend the rest of my life carrying two feelings into these embroidered halls:

the dizziness of having been fortunate,

and the quiet guilt of knowing how little of that fortune was truly mine.

Sometimes, in dreams, I return to the rear courtyard of the Zhen household.

There is the scent of osmanthus candy.

I am still little Jia, the maidservant.

She is still Miss Yinglian.

The sunlight is warm.

The apricot tree is blooming.

And fate has not yet made its move.

**The game has not yet begun.**

### Author’s Note

This essay is a literary reconstruction rather than a historical claim about Jiaxing’s actual private thoughts.

The psychological and financial concepts—including asymmetric investment, intrinsic value, market price, competitive moat, Mr. Market, external locus of control, learned helplessness, and narrative valuation—are modern analytical frameworks applied retrospectively to the fictional world of *Dream of the Red Chamber*.

The purpose is not to turn Jiaxing and Yinglian into modern investors.

It is to use the language of markets to ask an older question:

**Why can two people begin from radically different positions, make almost equally small decisions, and end up living entirely different lives?**

Perhaps the answer lies somewhere between information, institutions, risk, luck, and the strange ways in which other people assign value to us.

And perhaps that is what Jiaxing understood only after she had already won.
