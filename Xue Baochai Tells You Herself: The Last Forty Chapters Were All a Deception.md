# **Xue Baochai Tells You Herself: The Last Forty Chapters Were All a Deception**

## **How Cruel Was Cao Xueqin’s Original Vision?**

### **A Night in Hengwuyuan: My Errata and Confession**

**The Fifteenth Day of the Eighth Month, Night — Hengwuyuan**

Outside the window, the bamboo shadows rustle in the wind. Moonlight lies across the pages on my desk like a thin layer of frost, cold and pale.

I have just closed the Cheng-Gao edition of *Dream of the Red Chamber*, the final forty chapters. Yet my fingers are still trembling slightly.

It is not anger.

It is a kind of **cold that reaches all the way to the bone**.

What kind of person have they turned me into?

A “replacement bride” who takes Daiyu’s place? A virtuous widow who quietly raises Baoyu’s posthumous child after he becomes a monk, waiting patiently for the day when “orchids and cassias will flourish together”?

I could not help laughing.

The sound echoed through the empty courtyard, so strange that even I hardly recognized it.

**They do not understand.**

Cheng Weiyuan did not understand. Gao E did not understand. Nor did that anonymous continuation writer understand.

They took the skin of Confucian morality and pasted it over my face, thinking that was Xue Baochai.

**But I am not a memorial archway. I am not a chess piece. I am not the backdrop against which some man achieves glory.**

I am the girl who overheard Hongyu’s secret affair outside Dicuifing and calmly escaped by invoking the name of “Pin’er.”

I am the woman who, while everyone in the Jia household was drowning in an illusion of prosperity, had already understood that **“when the moon is full, it begins to wane; when the water is full, it begins to overflow.”**

My composure is not coldness.

My tact is not flattery.

And my choices have never been passive.

Tonight, I have decided to write down the truth.

Not to defend myself, but so that those who come after us may see:

**What Mr. Cao Xueqin spent ten years of blood and tears writing was never a romantic tale of talented lovers finding happiness together. It was an utterly devastating tragedy of human nature.**

And in that tragedy, **I, Xue Baochai, was never a supporting character.**

---

## **I. They Turned Me into Someone Else**

Let us first look at how the continuation portrayed me.

After the eightieth chapter, I suddenly became a bride deceived by a “switching-the-bride plot.”

They said Wang Xifeng devised a scheme in which I would pretend to be Daiyu and marry Baoyu.

**Ridiculous.**

I had lived in the Grand View Garden for so many years. I knew perfectly well that Baoyu had eyes only for Lin Daiyu.

If I had possessed even the slightest desire to compete for him, why would I have waited until then?

**If I were willing to obtain a man through deception, would I still be Xue Baochai?**

Even more absurdly, the continuation has me becoming pregnant shortly after the wedding. Baoyu passes the civil examination and then becomes a monk. I, supposedly “with parents yet effectively without parents; with a husband yet effectively without a husband,” remain behind with his posthumous child.

Eventually, the Jia family supposedly rises again. My son Jia Gui and Jia Lan both achieve official success, restoring the family fortune.

**What is this?**

It is a cheap morality play.

They use my “virtue” and “sacrifice” to erect another monument to feudal morality.

But Mr. Cao Xueqin was writing of **“a thousand red beauties weeping, ten thousand fair women mourning.”**

He wrote of the end when **“all becomes clean and empty beneath a vast expanse of white.”**

How could he possibly turn me into the “survivor”?

How could he allow the Jia family to “return to its former prosperity”?

The commentator Zhiyanzhai had already warned us.

He wrote:

**“The golden hairpin lies buried in the snow.”**

I am the golden hairpin.

But I am destined to be buried in the snow—alone, silently, without a trace.

He also wrote:

**“With the help of a favorable wind, I shall rise into the blue clouds.”**

Those words appear in my poem about willow catkins.

They sound like ambition.

But they are also a preview of my tragedy.

What I thought was the “blue sky” was merely another prison.

The continuation writers either did not understand this—or perhaps they understood it and simply did not dare to write it.

**They did not dare to write a Jia family completely destroyed.**

They did not dare to write Baoyu truly reduced to a beggar.

They did not dare to write Daiyu dying after exhausting every tear, without resentment.

And they certainly did not dare to write me abandoned by the entire world after Baoyu became a monk.

Because such an ending is too cold.

So cold that every lesson about “rewarding virtue and punishing evil” would lose its meaning.

**But that is precisely the ending I was meant to have.**

---

## **II. I Saw the End Long Before the Eightieth Chapter**

Do you think I only became clear-eyed after the eightieth chapter?

**No. I saw the end earlier than anyone else.**

I still remember the night Yuan Chun returned to the Jia mansion for imperial visitation.

The Grand View Garden was brighter than day. Music filled the air. Even the cracks between the stones seemed stuffed with silk and brocade.

But what did I see in my elder sister’s eyes?

**Fear.**

She smiled and said that everything was “far too extravagant,” while tears silently ran down her face as she held Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang by the hands.

She said:

> “When I was sent to that place where no one could see me…”

That sentence pierced my heart like a needle.

What “imperial grace”?

What “favor of the imperial palace”?

They were merely a larger version of Hengwuyuan—magnificent and gilded, yet with no door leading out.

From that day onward, I began studying the accounts.

The family business declined year after year.

My brother Xue Pan was irresponsible. My mother was soft-hearted. The household managers clung to the Xue family like leeches, sucking the marrow from its bones.

Quietly, I learned to read ledgers, calculate interest, and manage money.

Mother told me, “A young woman should not trouble herself with such things.”

But if I did not worry about them, who would?

Should we wait until, like the Jia family, some eunuch came demanding a thousand taels in a so-called “loan,” and we were too frightened to refuse?

The incident in which Jia Lian was extorted by a eunuch—only Ping’er and I understood what had truly happened.

Wang Xifeng, formidable as she was, could do nothing more than rob Peter to pay Paul.

That was when I thought:

**This family is probably going to collapse.**

But I dared not say it.

If I did, I would be called inauspicious.

A crow bringing bad news.

So I became quieter.

More careful.

More “virtuous.”

I exchanged my silence and composure for everyone's final few years of peace of mind.

You read the first eighty chapters and think that I am endlessly accommodating, smiling at everyone, never offending anyone.

But do you know how much genuine emotion a fifteen-year-old girl must swallow to live without the slightest crack showing?

I am not without emotions.

**I have simply pressed all of them into those cold-fragrance pills.**

You ask why I take them?

**Because my heart is too hot.**

Too hot from seeing so much injustice, so much hypocrisy, so much prosperity already beginning to collapse—while being unable to say anything.

All I could do was swallow one cold-fragrance pill after another.

Make myself colder.

Colder still.

Until I became like the golden hairpin buried beneath the snow:

silent,

dignified,

**waiting for fate to bury me.**

---

# **III. What Was Mr. Cao Really Going to Write in the Final Forty Chapters?**

Now I must make this clear.

Based on the commentaries of Zhiyanzhai, the countless foreshadowings in the first eighty chapters—the judgments in Chapter Five, the Mid-Autumn poems in Chapter Seventy-Six, and the countless hidden threads scattered throughout the novel—

**I can say with certainty that Cao Xueqin’s intended ending was nothing like the continuation.**

Let me write down the truth, one piece at a time.

### **First: My Marriage**

In Mr. Cao’s original conception, I did not marry Baoyu because of some “switching-the-bride” trick.

**It was a transaction.**

The Jia family needed the Xue family’s money.

The Xue family needed the Jia family’s political power.

After Yuan Chun lost favor, the two families became like drowning ants desperately trying to grab hold of one another.

My marriage to Baoyu was something Lady Wang and my mother negotiated through tears beneath the lamplight.

It was not designed to harm Daiyu.

**It was designed to save two collapsing families.**

It was a political marriage.

And I was the person being traded.

But the continuation insisted on turning it into a conspiracy.

Why?

Because conspiracies are more dramatic.

Readers like them.

But they forgot something:

**Drama is not truth. And conspiracy is not tragedy.**

The real tragedy is this:

**You know perfectly well that you are entering into a transaction, yet you still walk into it with dignity because you have no other choice.**

---

### **Second: Baoyu**

What Mr. Cao wanted to write was Baoyu seeing through the mortal world completely after the Jia family was confiscated.

Not passing the civil examination and then becoming a monk.

**He was supposed to leave when he was at his lowest point, like Zhen Shiyin, following a lame Taoist into the wilderness.**

That is what “throwing oneself from the precipice” truly means.

A man stands at the edge of a cliff.

There is no road ahead.

Enemies are behind him.

And then he jumps.

That is enlightenment born of despair.

**It is not “seeing through the world” after achieving success.**

The continuation's decision to have Baoyu pass the examination is perhaps the greatest insult to his character.

The Baoyu who spoke of the “slaves of rank and salary,” who denounced those who worshipped official success as “traitors to the state and ghosts of wealth”—how could he possibly pursue the civil service examinations?

If he did, then what were the first eighty chapters?

Youthful ignorance?

**No.**

---

### **Third: Daiyu**

I must admit that the continuation’s scene in which Daiyu burns her poems and breaks her attachment to love made me cry.

But I cried because it was so wrong.

**How could Daiyu possibly die consumed by resentment?**

She came into this world to repay tears.

Her tears were for Baoyu.

Not for herself.

In Mr. Cao’s original conception, Daiyu was supposed to fall into decline after the Jia family was confiscated and Baoyu imprisoned.

She would weep day and night.

**Eventually, she would die after exhausting all her tears.**

She would never hate Baoyu.

She would understand that he had been powerless.

When she died, she would hold the old handkerchief Baoyu had once given her and whisper:

**“Do not worry.”**

Yet the continuation has her say:

“Baoyu, you…”

And then leaves the sentence unfinished.

What comes after that?

“You are so cruel”?

“You had better take care of yourself”?

**Whatever the missing words are, they are not Daiyu.**

All Daiyu ever wanted was someone who truly understood her.

If she knew that Baoyu had been forced to marry me, she would pity him.

**How could she hate him?**

---

### **Fourth: The Fate of the Jia Family**

**This is the most important point.**

What Mr. Cao intended was the complete collapse of the four great families:

**Jia, Shi, Wang, and Xue.**

Not merely the confiscation of the Ningguo household.

Not merely Jia She being punished while the Rongguo branch somehow survived.

It was:

**“Suddenly, the great mansion collapses; dimly, the lamp burns toward extinction.”**

The Jia family would be confiscated.

Its sons and grandsons would be exiled or enslaved.

Its women would enter government entertainment houses or become dependent upon others.

Wang Xifeng would be repudiated and sent back toward Jinling, dying on the road.

Grandmother Jia would die from the shock of the confiscation.

Tanchun would be married far away across the sea, never to return.

Yuan Chun would die violently amid a palace coup, like a golden bird whose neck had been crushed.

Qiaojie would be sold into a brothel by her “cruel uncle and treacherous elder brother,” only to be rescued by Granny Liu and eventually married to Ban'er.

Shi Xiangyun would fall into poverty and become a beggar.

At a ruined temple gate, she would encounter Baoyu, who had likewise become a beggar.

**“Two white-haired stars,” looking at one another in silence.**

And the Xue family?

Xue Pan would kill again.

This time, no one would be able to save him.

He would be executed.

Xia Jingui would torture Xiangling to death, only to meet her own miserable fate.

My mother would fall gravely ill.

**I would remain in the empty Xue family mansion, surviving by pawning whatever remained.**

**That is what “all becomes clean and empty beneath a vast expanse of white” truly means.**

---

# **IV. Why Did They Dare Not Write It?**

You may ask:

If Cao Xueqin’s original intention was so clear, why did the continuation writers change everything beyond recognition?

After thinking about it for many years, I believe there were three reasons.

## **The First Reason: They Did Not Believe It**

They could not believe that a great aristocratic family could truly collapse completely.

For readers of the Qing dynasty, “good is rewarded and evil punished” was part of the moral order.

The Jia family had committed so many wrongs.

Confiscation was enough.

Surely the entire family did not need to perish.

I, Xue Baochai, was virtuous.

Surely I should not end up alone.

Baoyu was rebellious.

But he was still a son of an honored family.

Surely he could not become a beggar.

**They did not believe reality could be more brutal than fiction.**

So they transformed tragedy into something “sad, but ultimately hopeful.”

But Cao Xueqin believed it.

He had lived it.

He knew what it meant to lose everything.

He wrote:

**“In winter, choking down sour pickled vegetables; on snowy nights, wrapped in a torn blanket.”**

That was his own life.

He was writing the fall of the Jia family because he was writing his own family history.

**The continuation writers did not possess that experience.**

So they could not reproduce that bone-deep cold.

---

## **The Second Reason: They Did Not Dare**

What does “the orchids and cassias flourish together” really mean?

**It is an appeasement of imperial power.**

They wanted to say:

Look.

As long as your family remains loyal to the emperor, even if you suffer temporary disgrace, your descendants can eventually restore the family.

But what Cao Xueqin wanted to say was:

**It does not matter whether you are loyal or disloyal. The system itself will eventually consume you.**

Yuan Chun was loyal.

Did she not die?

Jia Zheng was the model of obedient Confucian loyalty.

Did he not end in exile?

**Cao Xueqin was not criticizing one particular emperor. He was criticizing the entire feudal order.**

The continuation writers did not dare to write that.

If they had, the book might never have survived.

---

## **The Third Reason: They Did Not Understand Tragedy**

They believed tragedy meant good people suffering while bad people were punished.

**But true tragedy is that good and bad people suffer together, and no one escapes.**

The root of tragedy is not necessarily the evil of one individual.

It may be the evil embedded in an entire system.

Xue Pan deserved punishment.

But Xiangling did not deserve to die.

Jia Yucun deserved judgment.

But Daiyu did not deserve to die after exhausting her tears.

The Jia family may have deserved collapse.

But Qiaojie did not deserve to be sold.

**Yet fate does not care about fairness.**

It binds good and evil together and sweeps them away in the same flood.

Cao Xueqin understood this.

**He was among the first great Chinese novelists to write tragedy as tragedy of fate itself.**

Everyone's destruction came not simply because one villain schemed against them, but because everyone, in their own position, made choices.

Those choices accumulated.

And eventually they produced an irreversible outcome.

My choice was silence and composure.

Baoyu's was avoidance and rebellion.

Daiyu's was devotion and persistence.

Xifeng's was ambition and ruthlessness.

**Who was entirely wrong?**

**Who was entirely right?**

In the end, everyone sank together.

Only then did I understand:

None of us truly defeated anyone else.

Lin Daiyu did not lose to me.

I did not win Baoyu.

Xifeng did not truly lose to You Shi.

Tanchun did not truly lose because she married far away.

We simply lived together inside a mansion that was already collapsing.

Each of us guarded the thing we believed in most.

Someone guarded poetry.

Someone guarded ritual.

Someone guarded the ledgers.

Someone guarded love.

And when the building finally collapsed, we discovered that **none of us had escaped.**

**That is what makes *Dream of the Red Chamber* transcend every conventional tale of talented lovers and romantic destiny.**

---

# **V. My Confession**

It is nearly dawn.

Outside, the bamboo bends and rustles in the wind, like countless people whispering among themselves.

I know that if Mr. Cao were still alive, he would never have allowed me to write this diary.

He was too subtle.

All his deepest meanings were hidden beneath the “false words of village speech.”

**But tonight, I do not want to hide anymore.**

I have lived inside this story for too long.

So long that I can no longer tell whether I am Xue Baochai—

or merely a symbol endlessly judged by generations of readers.

I want to tell you this:

**I am not the perfect virtuous wife.**

I am not the stereotypical defender of feudal morality.

And I am certainly not the pitiful bride deceived by a wedding trick.

**I am Xue Baochai.**

A girl who, at fifteen, already saw the fate of her entire family—

and yet could only swallow cold-fragrance pills and continue smiling.

My tragedy was not that I married the wrong man.

**It was that I was too clear-eyed.**

Clear-eyed enough to see the truth.

Powerless enough to change nothing.

The morning Baoyu left, I did not cry.

I straightened his collar and said:

“Go.”

He stared at me.

“You are not going to stop me?”

“If I stop you, will you stay?”

He shook his head.

“Then go. It is cold outside. Wear another layer.”

After he left, I sat down beside the bed.

On the pillow lay a single long strand of white hair.

**I was only twenty-three.**

Later, I pawned the last piece of gold jewelry I owned.

It had been part of my dowry from my mother—a pair of gold hairpins decorated with kingfisher feathers.

The pawnshop clerk looked at it and said:

“Madam, this isn't worth much anymore.”

“I know,” I said.

“Take it.”

Later still, I moved out of Hengwuyuan.

I rented a small room in a narrow alley in the southern part of the city.

The neighbors did not know who I was.

They only saw a quiet woman who sat at her doorway every evening, sewing soles, her eyes fixed on the entrance to the alley.

Someone once asked me:

“Whom are you waiting for?”

I said:

“No one. I'm watching the sunset.”

But I knew what I was waiting for.

**I was waiting for the ending in which all becomes clean and empty beneath a vast expanse of white.**

I was not waiting for restoration.

Not reunion.

Not “orchids and cassias flourishing together.”

**I was waiting for the truth.**

The truth of Cao Xueqin's original vision—

that utterly ruthless ending,

that merciless truth

that would crush everyone.

Tonight, I have written that truth down.

Whether future generations believe me or not,

**my conscience is clear.**

Snow is coming.

I can hear grains of snow rattling in the wind.

The winter of the capital is long.

So long that one begins to believe spring will never return.

But I know that even if spring comes, the Grand View Garden can never return.

All of us—

Daiyu,

Baoyu,

Tanchun,

Xichun,

Miaoyu,

Xifeng—

**we all died inside that garden.**

Those who survived were merely empty shells.

And I—

**even my shell will be buried beneath the snow.**

**“The golden hairpin lies buried in the snow.”**

Beautiful.

Let it be buried.

**At least the snow is clean.**

…

**Before dawn, in Hengwuyuan, the brush falls silent. The tears are exhausted.**

### **Author's Note**

This work is a literary creation written from the fictional perspective of Xue Baochai. It incorporates textual interpretation, traditional commentary, and literary analysis to explore the tragic structure of *Dream of the Red Chamber*.

The historical and textual claims concerning Cao Xueqin's “original ending” represent a **literary interpretation rather than an established scholarly fact**. The surviving first eighty chapters and various commentarial materials provide important clues and evidence, but the complete original manuscript of the final forty chapters has not survived, and therefore its precise contents cannot be established with certainty.
