# **The Tea House at Gushan**

## *A Record of Tea, Islands, and Hearts That Could Not Turn Back*

> **My brother was Zhong Kui.**
>
> The one from the folk tale—the man who married off his sister.
>
> The story was invented.
>
> **The marriage was real.**

The scholar eventually became an official.

He treated me reasonably well.

In an age of chaos, when three children survived and two remained alive, that alone was a kind of grace.

The day my brother struck his head against the pillars of the Golden Hall and died, I did not cry.

Not because I did not hurt.

But because I had always known that such a day would come.

He had turned himself into a drum.

Anyone could strike it.

And he would answer.

He answered until the sound reached its absolute limit—

and the drum broke.

The drum was gone.

But its echo remained beneath the palace beams.

It lingered for three years and three months.

After he was gone, I began looking for similar sounds among the living.

Not ghosts.

Something else.

A particular kind of obsession—

the kind that turns a person into an island.

A volcano burning beneath ice.

A fire held down for so long that, when the ice finally cracks, the fire rises through the fissure—

burning itself,

and everyone standing too close.

**Zhou Zhiruo was one such island.**

---

# I. The First Cup

## Before She Became the Head of Emei

When I first met her, she was not yet the head of Emei.

She had been on the mountain only a few years, following behind Master Miejue like a faded shadow.

At the foot of Emei, I ran a small tea house.

I sold wild tea that I dried myself.

It was extremely bitter.

The people of the martial world did not care for it.

Occasionally, however, the masters from the mountain came down to buy some.

The first time she came, she had come for sewing needles and thread.

It was cold.

She wore a gray cotton robe whose collar had begun to fray.

The elbows of her sleeves had been carefully patched.

Yet the stitching was as fine as calligraphy on silk.

When she chose the needles, she held each one up to the light, examining them one by one as though she were judging a piece of ancient jade.

I poured her a bowl of tea.

She took a sip.

Her eyebrows drew together.

Yet she did not stop.

Little by little,

she drank the entire bowl.

“Is it good?”

“Bitter.”

“Then why finish it?”

She was silent for a moment.

Then she said:

> **“My mother used to say that bitter things nourish people.”**

I smiled.

Not because of what she said.

But because of the way she said *my mother*.

Too calmly.

As though she were speaking about someone else's distant past.

I had seen too many people like that.

People who bury their wounds so deeply that even they forget where the well is.

But drop a stone into it—

and the echo can still shatter the organs.

I poured her another bowl.

This time I added a spoonful of osmanthus honey.

> **“This bowl won't be bitter.”**

She accepted it.

She lowered her head and drank.

Then, suddenly,

her eyes reddened.

---

That was her first visit.

Afterward, she came often.

Usually she had some errand below the mountain and would take the longer road.

She would sit for a while.

I did not ask questions.

She did not volunteer answers.

We simply sat across from each other.

Drinking tea.

Watching travelers move along the mountain road like ants.

Listening to the wind whisper through the bamboo.

Sometimes she spoke.

Always about small things on the mountain:

which senior sister had been punished for being lazy,

which junior sister had cried in the middle of the night because she missed home,

whom Master Miejue had praised that day,

and whom she had scolded.

I listened carefully.

Not because these things were interesting.

But because I knew she needed someone to listen.

There was no one else on the mountain.

Miejue was her master, not her friend.

Ding Minjun was an enemy, not a sister.

Jingxuan was too solemn.

Bei Jinyi was too young.

She was trapped somewhere in the middle.

Like a thorn growing backward in the throat—

neither swallowable,

nor removable.

---

One day, she suddenly asked:

> “Auntie, what kind of person was your brother?”

I was startled.

I had never told her about him.

“He was a fool,” I said.

She looked at me.

“He tried to take care of everything.

He tried to carry everything.

He could not stand the smallest injustice.

Whatever everyone else avoided, he would walk straight toward.”

I paused.

> “In the end, he walked himself into the grave.”

She hesitated.

“Did he ever…”

She stopped.

“Did he ever regret it?”

“I don't know.”

“When he died, I wasn't beside him.”

That was the truth.

The day my brother's blood stained the Golden Hall, I was at home sewing the sole of his shoes.

He wore through shoes faster than ordinary men.

I made his soles twice as thick.

Even so,

they never lasted.

---

She lowered her eyes.

> “Sometimes I wonder…”

Her voice was as soft as falling snow.

> “If you knew beforehand that something could never end well, would you still do it?”

I thought for a long time.

Then I said:

> **“It depends on whether your heart is full or empty when you do it.”**

“What do you mean?”

“If your heart is full, you may do it, lose everything, and still accept the loss.”

I looked at her.

> **“But if your heart is empty, then once you lose, there is truly nothing left.”**

She did not ask again.

That day, before leaving, she drank every drop of the osmanthus tea.

---

Only much later did I learn that it had been the day Master Miejue forced her to swear the oath.

She had gone down the mountain.

Taken a road that added half an hour to her journey.

All for one reason:

> **to sit in my tea house for a single cup of tea.**

A bowl of tea that was not bitter.

That was when I knew.

> **Something was going to happen to this girl.**

---

# II. The Cocoon

## What the Mountain Gave Her

I had seen too many people like this.

My brother was one.

She was another.

Both carried a fire inside.

A fire pressed down since childhood.

And the longer it was suppressed,

the hotter it burned.

Eventually, such a fire does one of two things:

> **It burns others.**

> **Or it burns its owner.**

My brother burned himself.

As for her—

she burned the person she had once been.

Looking back many years later, I realized that Zhou Zhiruo's tragedy did not begin on Spirit Snake Island.

Nor did it begin at Bright Summit.

It began the day she first climbed the stone steps of Emei.

Miejue needed an heir.

Someone obedient.

Someone intelligent.

Someone without distracting desires.

Zhiruo fit perfectly.

She was obedient because no one had ever taught her how to refuse.

She was intelligent because without intelligence she would not have survived this long.

And she appeared to have no distracting desires because those desires had been buried so deeply that she herself had come to believe they did not exist.

Miejue gave her a cocoon.

Its walls were:

**the righteousness of Emei.**

Its roof was:

**the debt owed to one's master.**

Its floor was:

**the absolute division between orthodox and unorthodox.**

From the outside, it looked solid.

Warm.

Almost like home.

But what is a cocoon?

A place in which wings cannot grow.

A moth trapped inside long enough may eventually break through—

and still be unable to fly.

But Zhou Zhiruo was never a moth.

> **She was an eagle.**

And an eagle trapped inside a cocoon has only one direction left to tear:

> **inward.**

---

# III. The Eyes of the Eagle

## After Bright Summit

The first time I saw the eyes of an eagle in her face was after Bright Summit.

The Six Great Sects had been defeated.

Miejue was dead.

Zhiruo returned to Emei wearing the iron ring of the new leader.

The senior disciples refused to accept her.

Ding Minjun mocked her publicly.

> “You have hardly any seniority.”

Zhiruo neither argued nor defended herself.

She simply looked at Ding Minjun.

Then, calmly, she said:

> **“If Senior Sister objects, we may test our swords.”**

I had seen that look before.

My brother had worn it before his death.

It was not anger.

It was not even determination.

It was something colder.

The look of a person examining an object—

rather than another human being.

---

She began to change.

Her mastery of the Nine Yin White Bone Claw advanced rapidly.

The martial world began whispering about the new Emei leader and her strange techniques.

Her white python whip cracked against stone.

Rock shattered.

She began wearing bright colors.

Gold hairpins.

Powdered face.

Jewelry.

Some praised her beauty.

Others called her excessive and seductive.

She did not care.

She no longer listened to anyone.

Not her senior sisters.

Not her fellow martial artists.

Not even Zhang Wuji.

She listened only to herself.

But then came the question:

> **What, exactly, was the voice inside her telling her to do?**

---

# IV. The Night of Red Silk

One night, long after midnight, someone knocked at my door.

When I opened it, I was startled.

She was wearing a brilliant red dress.

Jewels filled her hair.

Her face was heavily painted.

For a moment, I could barely recognize her.

“Auntie.”

“Yes?”

“Do you have wine?”

“I do.”

I poured her a bowl of homemade rice wine.

It was mild.

Slightly sweet.

She emptied it.

I poured another.

She emptied that too.

A third.

Then she sat still.

Her eyes fixed on the candle flame.

She did not move.

“He is getting married tomorrow,” she said.

I knew who she meant.

> **“The bride is not me.”**

I said nothing.

She did not need an answer.

She only needed someone to hear the words.

There was no one else on the mountain.

Her senior sisters feared her.

Her juniors revered her.

Miejue was gone.

There was no one left in the world who could tolerate hearing even half of what she really thought.

She had turned herself into a temple.

There was a Buddha inside.

But she was not the Buddha.

She was the mortal kneeling before it.

And if a person kneels long enough,

the bones in the knees eventually break.

Sometimes,

they never stand again.

---

“Auntie…”

Her voice was almost weightless.

> “Did your brother ever regret it?”

She had asked me this before.

Long ago.

Back then, she had just taken the oath.

There had still been hesitation in her.

Struggle.

Perhaps even the faintest thought:

*Maybe I should not do this.*

But now her question sounded different.

She was no longer asking because she was confused.

She was asking because she needed confirmation.

She wanted an answer:

> *If you know something cannot end well, should you still do it?*

She wanted me to say:

> **“No regret.”**

She wanted me to tell her:

> **“My brother never regretted it, not even at the end.”**

Then she could tell herself:

> **Neither do I.**

But I could not lie to her.

“I don't know,” I said.

“I told you before—I wasn't beside him when he died.

I don't know what he thought in his final moment.

But I do know one thing.”

“What?”

“He had many opportunities to retreat.

He could have withheld that memorial from the court.

No one would have blamed him.

He could have refused to strike the pillar in the Golden Hall.

No one forced him.”

I looked at her.

> **“He did those things not because he could not regret them.”**

> **“He did them because not doing them would have hurt more than dying.”**

Her eyes filled with a thin layer of water.

“You are different from him, Zhiruo.”

She said nothing.

“He acted with a full heart.

He knew what he was doing.

He knew why he was doing it.”

I paused.

> **“Do you?”**

She did not answer.

She placed the wine bowl down.

Straightened her clothes.

Opened the door.

And walked away.

---

The moonlight lay across the mountain path like white silk.

Her red dress turned dark beneath it—

the color of dried blood.

She walked slowly.

At the bend in the road, she stopped.

She stood there for a long time.

I thought she might turn around.

She did not.

She stood like a tree struck by lightning.

Its roots still buried in the earth.

Its branches already dead.

Yet unwilling to fall.

Not because it was strong.

But because it no longer knew which way to fall.

Ahead was a cliff.

Behind was a wall.

On either side were thorns.

She had become trapped inside a prison she had built with her own hands.

The prison had one name:

> **Head of Emei.**

And another:

> **I cannot lose.**

But had she won?

---

# V. The Architecture of Obsession

Years later, she truly did win.

Her martial arts became formidable.

Her position as head of Emei became secure.

The martial world spoke her name with respect.

But she visited my tea house less and less.

When she came, she spoke only a few hurried words before leaving.

Even her language had changed.

Once, she had said:

> “I heard…”

> “I think…”

> “I'm a little afraid…”

Now she said:

> “I know.”

> “I have decided.”

> “They must.”

Her voice became harder.

Her eyes colder.

---

During those years, rumors spread that she had grown close to Song Qingshu.

I had met the young man.

A disciple of Wudang.

Polite.

Soft-spoken.

An apparently honest man.

But honest men can hide things too.

He did not approach her simply because she was beautiful.

Nor because she was intelligent.

He approached her because she was the head of Emei.

Because she possessed extraordinary martial power.

Because she could help him obtain what he wanted.

Did Zhou Zhiruo not know this?

Of course she did.

She was more perceptive than anyone.

But she did not care.

She cared about only one thing:

> **What would Zhang Wuji think?**

She was using Song Qingshu to wound Zhang Wuji.

But Zhang Wuji never saw the wound.

He was living happily with Zhao Min.

They had children.

They taught them martial arts.

Sometimes they went down the mountain to buy sewing supplies or sweets.

He had no idea what Zhou Zhiruo was thinking.

What she was doing.

What she hated.

Her revenge was a fist striking cotton.

> **There was not even an echo.**

---

My brother once told me:

> **“It is more exhausting to hate someone than to love them.”**

“Love allows you to rest.”

> **“Hatred follows you even into your dreams.”**

Zhou Zhiruo was living exactly like that.

She was exhausted.

But she dared not stop.

Because stopping meant pain.

All the thoughts she had forced underground would rise like weeds:

*Was I wrong?*

*Should I have stolen the sword?*

*Should I have framed Zhao Min?*

*Should I have killed Yin Li?*

There would be no end to them.

She feared the pain.

So she kept moving.

Kept fighting.

Kept winning.

As long as she remained in motion,

she did not have to turn around.

But the farther she walked,

the narrower the road became.

Until finally she entered a dead end.

No road ahead.

No road behind.

Four walls.

A piece of sky above.

But the sky was too high to reach.

---

# VI. The Last Cup

## The Question She Could No Longer Answer

The last time I saw her was two winters ago.

Heavy snow covered Emei.

She came down the mountain alone.

Wrapped in white fox fur.

“Auntie.”

“Yes?”

“I would like a cup of tea.”

I boiled the water.

Warmed the cup.

Took a small amount of fresh tea.

The leaves floated in the boiling water,

sinking,

rising,

unfolding.

Like a person struggling against fate.

---

“Auntie…”

“Yes?”

“Sometimes I wonder…”

She looked into the tea.

> “If I had never climbed Emei…”

A pause.

> “If I had never become Miejue's disciple…”

Another pause.

> “If I had never met Zhang Wuji…”

Her fingers tightened around the cup.

> “If I had never done any of those things…”

She looked up.

> **“What kind of person would I have become?”**

“You cannot imagine it.”

She smiled faintly.

“No.”

> **“I cannot.”**

That was her tragedy.

She could no longer imagine *if*.

Her mind contained only *because*.

Because I climbed the mountain, I must become its leader.

Because I became its leader, I must restore Emei.

Because I must restore Emei, I must obtain the sword.

Because I obtained the sword, I must master the scripture.

Because I mastered it, I must defeat everyone.

Every step began with:

> **Because.**

And every step ended with:

> **I must.**

But who had chosen the first *because*?

Not her.

It belonged to Miejue.

To Emei.

To an entire life she had never truly chosen.

---

She held the tea bowl and looked down at her reflection.

“My brother used to say something,” she suddenly said.

“Before acting, think it through.

After acting, do not regret it.”

She smiled sadly.

> **“But I never had the chance to think it through.”**

I said nothing.

She finished the tea.

Then stood.

“Auntie.”

“Yes?”

“I may not come here often anymore.”

“Why?”

“I am going into seclusion.

There is a new sword technique I must master.

It may take a very long time.”

I looked at her.

White fox fur.

White snow.

She looked like a column of ice.

Beautiful.

Transparent.

And terribly fragile.

“Zhiruo.”

She turned.

> **“Did my brother ever tell you that regret is not shameful?”**

She froze.

For a moment, she did not move.

Then she smiled.

Very faintly.

Like a mark left on fresh snow.

But I knew what it was.

> **The first sincere smile I had seen from her in years.**

“Auntie,” she said,

> **“regret belongs to those who still have a road ahead of them.”**

She looked toward the snow-covered mountain.

> **“My road ended long ago.”**

Then she left.

Her white coat disappeared slowly into the snow.

Until there was nothing left but the mountain.

I never saw her again.

---

I heard that her martial arts reached perfection.

That her position as head of Emei became unshakable.

That when people in the martial world heard the name **Zhou Zhiruo**, they raised a cup in respect.

But did she really win?

A person may win everything—

and still have no one left to sit across from them over a cup of tea.

No one with whom to exchange a few ordinary words.

Is that victory?

---

# VII. The Brother Who Became a Drum

After my brother died, someone once asked me:

> “Was Zhong Kui's life worth it?”

I answered:

“I cannot decide that for him.

Only he could.”

But I do know one thing.

When he was alive,

> **his heart was full.**

What he believed, he held until the end.

What he did, he believed he had to do.

He was not without regret.

Rather—

> **he never needed regret to prove that he had lived.**

And Zhou Zhiruo?

Was her heart full?

Or empty?

---

Full.

But full of:

**hatred,**

**resentment,**

**humiliation,**

**and the question—**

> **“Why should it be me?”**

Such things cannot nourish a heart.

They ferment.

They rot.

They become poison.

She drank so many bowls of my tea.

She swallowed so many spoonfuls of osmanthus honey.

But none of the sweetness ever reached her heart.

Her heart was already occupied by something else.

> **Sweetness could not enter.**

> **Bitterness could not leave.**

---

Sometimes I wonder:

If she had met my brother earlier,

would things have been different?

My brother was useless in almost every practical sense.

His words could cut like knives.

But he knew how to tell a person one important thing:

> **You may step back.**

Stepping back is not shameful.

You can retreat to a road you can actually walk.

It may be narrow.

It may be dark.

But:

> **it is your road.**

Zhou Zhiruo never found her own road.

She had only the road on Emei.

A road that became narrower with every step.

Darker with every step.

Until finally—

a wall.

She walked into it.

The wall did not fall.

Neither did she.

But something inside her shattered against its base.

No one saw it.

---

# VIII. The Tea That Remains

That night, I sat outside the tea house until very late.

The moon was bright.

The wind was cold.

I remembered the girl who had first come down the mountain for needles and thread.

Gray cotton robe.

Worn collar.

A bowl of bitter tea.

Her eyebrows drawn together—

yet she finished every drop.

> **“Auntie, this bowl isn't bitter.”**

That was the first unguarded sentence she ever said to me.

Sadly, the tea that came afterward grew more bitter with every bowl.

So bitter that she could no longer taste sweetness.

So bitter that she forgot what sweetness felt like.

---

I closed the door.

Inside, a kettle of water was still warm.

At the bottom of the tea jar remained one final pinch of fresh tea.

I took it out.

Wrapped it carefully.

And placed it back on the shelf.

Perhaps one day she will return.

Perhaps never.

But I will keep it there.

I will keep preparing that cup.

The one with osmanthus honey.

The one that is not bitter.

For someone who once lived inside a cocoon—

and could never quite learn how to fly out.

---

# Afterword

## The Island and the Cocoon

An island and a cocoon are, in the end, two forms of the same thing.

An island is **geographical imprisonment**.

A cocoon is **cognitive enclosure**.

Zhou Zhiruo was trapped between the two.

Her body belonged to the isolated mountain of Emei.

Her mind belonged to the cocoon of obsession.

She was imprisoned by place.

And then imprisoned by meaning.

---

When tea leaves awaken for the second time in boiling water,

perhaps they remember that once—

they were leaves beneath the sun.

And perhaps that is what the person watching the tea has been waiting for all along.

Not for the butterfly to emerge.

Not for the cocoon to break.

But for the person inside it to finally say,

quietly,

to herself:

> # **“This cup of tea is not bitter anymore.”**
