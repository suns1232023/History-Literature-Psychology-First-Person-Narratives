Snow
Old Zhou's Recollection
A Plainspoken Memoir of Memory, Silence, and a Woman Named Ding Ning

Hefei, Late Autumn, 2026

Prologue

I am eighty-two years old.

When you reach this age, you wake before the dawn does.

Around four every morning, my eyes open on their own. I lie still and listen. Outside the window, the wind moves through the plane trees, their leaves rustling like someone turning the pages of an old book.

A thick book.

A fragile book.

One whose pages must be turned carefully.

I cannot remember the last time I held a book myself. My eyes have dimmed. My hands shake. I can barely keep a teacup steady anymore.

But this morning was different.

A young community worker came knocking.

She told me the provincial library was holding an event downstairs. Someone from the library would be giving a talk about preserving ancient books.

Would I like to attend?

I said no.

She said there would be souvenirs.

I still said no.

After she left, I sat in my wicker chair for a long time.

Those four words refused to leave me:

Ancient Book Preservation.

And another phrase:

Provincial Library.

That thorn has been buried in my heart for sixty years.

It still hurts when touched.

So I have decided to write.

Not for publication.

Not for forgiveness.

Not even for my children.

Just for myself.

Because at eighty-two, if I do not write this now, it will go into the ground with me.

And disappear.

I. The Summer of 1966

I was twenty-two.

What does a twenty-two-year-old understand about the world?

Not much.

I had left school early and worked repairing tractors in a factory. Every day I came home covered in machine oil.

The city was overflowing with slogans.

Posters.

Speeches.

Marches.

Everyone talked about destroying the "Four Olds."

Old ideas.

Old culture.

Old customs.

Old habits.

Everything old was supposedly rotten.

Everything new was supposedly righteous.

I did not know what "old culture" really meant.

My family owned no books.

My father pulled a cart for a living.

My mother glued cardboard boxes together at a neighborhood workshop.

The most valuable thing in our home was a sewing machine.

We had nothing cultural enough to destroy.

Yet I shouted the slogans anyway.

Everyone did.

If you remained silent, you became suspect.

One day, a coworker named Lao Ma told me we had a mission.

He did not explain.

I followed.

There were perhaps a dozen of us.

Workers.

Students.

Neighborhood activists.

Some carried iron rods.

Others brought fuel.

I carried nothing.

The destination was the library on Wuhu Road.

I had passed that building countless times.

I had never entered.

That day would be my first visit.

And my last for sixty years.

We walked through a narrow corridor.

Glass display cases lined the walls.

Someone swung an iron bar.

Glass exploded across the floor.

The sound still echoes in my ears.

At the end stood a heavy wooden door.

Behind it was the rare-books collection.

In front of it stood an elderly woman.

Thin.

Very thin.

Short gray hair.

A faded blue jacket.

Worn cuffs.

She stood there with both hands at her sides.

Not aggressive.

Not dramatic.

Just standing.

Like an old tree rooted in the wrong place.

"Move aside," Lao Ma shouted.

She did not move.

He shouted louder.

Still nothing.

Finally she spoke.

Her voice was not loud.

Yet everyone heard it.

"You cannot enter this room."

Someone began smashing the lock.

The old woman suddenly lunged forward.

She grabbed the man's wrist.

They shoved her away.

She fell.

Her knees struck the floor hard.

She stood again.

Blood appeared beneath her nose.

And she lunged forward once more.

I watched.

That is the fact that has stayed with me all these years.

I watched.

I did nothing.

I was young.

Strong.

Far stronger than she was.

Had I wanted to remove her, it would have taken one hand.

Yet I did not move.

Not because I agreed.

Not because I disagreed.

Because I was confused.

Terribly confused.

If destroying old things was right, why was she willing to suffer for them?

If these books were worthless, why was she risking everything?

The question appeared only briefly.

The crowd's shouting drowned it out.

Then someone proposed another idea.

If she would not surrender the key, we would search her home.

The old woman's posture changed.

Only slightly.

A tiny collapse of the shoulders.

Then she said:

"Take what I have.

But not these books."

II. The Fire

Her house was nearby.

A single-room dwelling packed from floor to ceiling with books.

Books on tables.

Books on chairs.

Books on the bed.

Books stacked against walls.

Every corner filled.

The books were carried into the courtyard.

Then the fire was lit.

I remember the smell more clearly than the flames.

Old paper has its own scent.

Dry.

Dusty.

Almost medicinal.

When it burns, that smell changes into something unforgettable.

Something bitter.

Something final.

The books curled and blackened.

Bindings split open.

Pages shrank into ash.

Characters I could not read appeared briefly in the fire before vanishing forever.

The woman stood beneath the roofline and watched.

She did not scream.

She did not beg.

She did not cry.

Her hands were clenched so tightly that her knuckles turned white.

Her lips formed a hard line.

And her eyes...

I have never forgotten her eyes.

Many years later I encountered the phrase:

Cold anger.

That was what I saw.

Not rage that explodes.

Not fury that burns wildly.

But something colder.

Like embers buried beneath ash.

Quiet.

Yet capable of burning anyone foolish enough to touch them.

When the crowd finally dispersed, I remained behind.

I turned once before leaving.

She was still standing there.

The fire was extinguished.

The courtyard was black.

The house was empty.

And the setting sun stretched her shadow all the way across the alley.

Almost to my feet.

My mother once told me:

"When a person dies, their shadow becomes heavier."

But that woman was still alive.

Why, then, did her shadow feel so impossibly heavy?

III. Learning Her Name

Decades passed.

I married.

Raised children.

Retired from the factory.

And told no one about that day.

Then, years later, I found an article on an old smartphone my grandson had discarded.

It described a scholar, poet, and librarian.

A woman who had protected rare books at the Anhui Provincial Library.

A woman beaten for refusing to surrender a key.

A woman who sacrificed her own collection to save hundreds of thousands of historical volumes.

For the first time, I learned her name.

Ding Ning.

The article quoted one of her poems:

"My tender heart is broken, yet the tears refuse to run dry."

I know little about poetry.

Yet that line lodged itself inside my bones.

She had lost parents.

Lost a daughter.

Lost a marriage.

At some point she poured all that love into books.

Those books became family.

Purpose.

Companionship.

Meaning.

And on that day in 1966, she stood before the library doors protecting them.

Not as objects.

As children.

And we burned them.

More than a thousand of them.

IV. Was I a Good Man?

For many years I asked myself a question.

Was I evil?

I did not strike her.

I did not light the fire.

I did not break the lock.

Legally, I committed no crime.

Yet morally?

I am no longer sure.

What is a person who watches injustice and says nothing?

A good man?

A neutral man?

A coward?

When I was young, I believed evil belonged only to murderers and thieves.

Age has complicated that certainty.

A person may commit wrong not only through action.

Sometimes through silence.

Sometimes through presence.

Sometimes through the decision not to step forward.

She stood alone.

I did not.

That fact cannot be rewritten.

V. Returning to the Library

This autumn I finally entered the provincial library again.

For the first time in sixty years.

The building was bright.

Modern.

Quiet.

A young librarian helped me register for a reader's card.

Then she directed me to the rare-books reading room.

I stood outside the glass cases and looked at the old volumes inside.

Yellowed paper.

Fragile bindings.

Centuries of memory.

I do not know whether these particular books were among those Ding Ning saved.

But I know this:

They are still here.

And she is not.

A sixty-four-year-old woman absorbed the loss.

The books survived.

History remembers the books.

But someone must remember her.

Epilogue

Tonight the wind has stopped.

The plane trees are quiet.

I sit alone beside this page.

My handwriting is shaky now.

Some characters nearly unreadable.

But the meaning remains clear.

I did not write this to seek forgiveness.

Forgiveness changes nothing.

The books burned.

She was beaten.

The past remains the past.

I wrote this for remembrance.

To remember her name.

To remember her face.

To remember her shadow stretching across the alley in the summer of 1966.

I am eighty-two now.

Soon enough, I will become only a shadow myself.

But mine will never carry the weight of hers.

Never.



An old man returns to the Anhui Provincial Library sixty years later, carrying the memory of a librarian who stood between fire and civilization.
