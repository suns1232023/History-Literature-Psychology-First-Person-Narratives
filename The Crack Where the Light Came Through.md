# The Crack Where the Light Came Through

### — The Diary of Guo Xiang on Her Sixteenth Birthday

**Xiangyang, the Guo Residence — My Room**

Mother has just left.

The faint, bitter-green scent of herbs and leaves that clings to her still lingers in the room. The window is open. Outside, the osmanthus trees are in full riotous bloom, their fragrance almost imperious as it pours through the window in waves, tangling with the steam rising from the bowl of longevity noodles on my desk.

Sunlight slants through the lattice and falls across the back of my hand.

Warm. Solid. Real.

I lower my eyes to the patch of light, yet inside I feel utterly cold.

Every word Mother spoke a moment ago was like a tiny shard of ice, driven silently into my heart. Only now are they beginning to melt, releasing their chill.

Today is my sixteenth birthday.

Mother said that when a girl reaches this age, she is considered grown. There are things she ought to know.

She made me sit beside her on the bed and closed her hand around mine. Those hands—hands that can draw a bow and wield a sword, steady as stone—were trembling faintly in my palm.

Her eyes were fixed on some empty point beyond the window. Her voice was very calm, very slow, as though she were telling me a story that had nothing to do with her.

But I knew.

She was enduring something.

More than twenty years of storm and thunder were beating against the seawall of her memory.

She told me that when I was still an infant, I had nearly been killed by a mad monk.

I am sitting here now with all four limbs intact, my heartbeat steady, the sweet fragrance of osmanthus filling my lungs. It is difficult to imagine that, in this same world, in the life of my mother, there once existed a moment when—had one hand tightened just a little more—everything before me would have ceased to exist.

The flowers outside.

The light in my palm.

Even the person called **Guo Xiang**.

Mother finished speaking and rose to leave, telling me to sit quietly.

I cannot sit quietly.

I spread out the paper and lift my brush. The ink in the inkstone has already been ground for the third time.

I have to write this down.

Not because I am afraid of forgetting.

Because I am afraid I still do not understand.

What, exactly, is this life of mine hanging from?

How impossibly thin was the thread of cause and consequence that once separated my life from death?

---

## I. The Valley of Passionless Love: Monk and Demon

The story begins in the Valley of Passionless Love.

At the time, I was not yet a year old.

I could not walk. I could not speak. I spent my days wrapped in swaddling clothes, waving my tiny fists and making meaningless little sounds, believing that the whole world was no larger than Mother's arms.

My parents had gone there in search of the **Passionless Love Pill**, hoping to save Brother Yang Guo.

Grandfather was with them.

So was Master Yideng.

And one of Master Yideng's disciples—a monk named Ci'en.

Mother told me that Ci'en had once been **Qiu Qianren**.

My heart tightened at the name.

I knew it.

In Father's *Miscellany of the Martial World*, the Iron Palm Sect's leader, Qiu Qianren, was described in terms so terrible that even a single page seemed unable to contain them.

There had been massacres. Villages destroyed. Bloodshed wherever he went.

And at the very bottom of one page, in tiny handwriting, I once read:

> *Once injured an infant with his palm. The child died soon afterward.*

I had been so furious that I threw the book across the room.

To raise a hand against an infant—

Could such a person still be called human?

But Mother told me:

That monk, Ci'en, was that very man.

Master Yideng had once saved him from himself. He had shaved his head, entered the monastery, and taken the Buddhist name **Ci'en**—Compassion and Grace.

Yet being redeemed does not mean the past has been erased.

It means only that the evil dragon has been forced beneath the depths.

The instinct to kill is like a venomous serpent.

It may sleep.

It has not died.

In the depths of the Valley of Passionless Love, Ci'en encountered his younger sister, Qiu Qianchi—a woman who had spent more than a decade imprisoned, her hatred sharpened into a blade.

Every word she spoke was a needle driven into an old wound.

She accused him of betraying his origins.

Mocked him for changing sides.

Called him a coward.

Mother said those words were like boiling oil poured across Ci'en's face.

Yet he did not rage.

He did not strike back.

His eyes simply emptied, little by little.

The light went out.

What replaced it was the bewilderment of an animal.

And then—

he moved.

So quickly that he seemed no more than a shadow.

And suddenly, I was in his hands.

---

## II. Those Hands

Mother told me she would remember that moment for the rest of her life.

Ci'en's hand closed around my neck and lifted me into the air.

I was only a few months old.

My neck was soft and fragile beneath his iron grip. His fingers tightened around it like metal clamps. My cry caught in my throat. My face turned blue-purple. My tiny legs kicked uselessly in the air.

He stood there like stone.

Like wood.

There was nothing in his eyes.

No hatred.

No anger.

Not even a trace of humanity.

The man standing there was no longer Ci'en.

He was Qiu Qianren from twenty years earlier—the Iron Palm Sect master whose hands had been stained with blood, who had not even spared an infant.

Mother's heart stopped.

She rushed forward.

Father rushed forward too.

Ci'en struck once with his palm and sent Father staggering three steps backward.

Mother dared not seize me.

She was terrified that if his fingers tightened by even the slightest degree, my throat would break.

So she fell straight to her knees.

Her voice cracked as she cried:

> **“Master Ci'en, please spare the child. Take my life instead.”**

But he did not hear her.

All he could hear was Qiu Qianchi's shrill laughter.

All he could see was the old banner of the Iron Palm Sect snapping in the wind.

All he could feel was the blood of the countless people he had killed, rising inside his mind like an endless sea.

Master Yideng sat cross-legged upon the ground and began softly reciting the Buddha's name.

Then he said:

> “Ci'en, have you forgotten the vow you once made?”

Ci'en shook his head.

His eyes were unfocused.

> “Master… I cannot control it…
> That thing… it has come back…”

**That thing.**

Mother told me that was how he referred to his murderous instinct.

As though he were possessed by an evil spirit.

As though he could watch himself commit evil with perfect awareness, yet could no longer reclaim his own body.

Just when everyone believed I was certain to die, Mother suddenly thought of something.

An old memory.

Years ago, Qiu Qianren had struck the child of Aunt Yinggu with his palm.

The baby had died.

Yinggu had hated him for the rest of her life.

She had pursued him for the rest of her life.

Her eyes had been burned red by grief.

Mother screamed at him:

> **“Qiu Qianren! Yinggu's son…
> He's come for you!”**

---

## III. One Sentence, One Life

Mother said that sentence was like a poisoned dagger.

It found the deepest, most rotten wound in his heart and drove straight into it.

His hand trembled.

And loosened.

I began to fall.

Father shot forward like an arrow and caught me in his arms.

And Ci'en—

the monk who only moments before had been as terrifying as a demon—

suddenly crouched down, clutching his head.

His entire body shook violently.

A wounded animal's whimper came from his throat.

Again and again he murmured:

> “It wasn't me…
> I didn't want to…
> I didn't mean to…
> That child…
> He was so small…”

He cried.

The former master of the Iron Palm Sect, a man who had killed countless people, curled up on the ground and wept like a lost child.

Master Yideng walked over and gently placed his hand upon his head.

His chanting flowed slowly, softly, like a stream.

After a long while, Ci'en finally began to calm.

He lifted his face, wet with tears, and looked at Master Yideng.

> “Master…
> I almost did it again…
> I almost…”

Master Yideng said:

> “But you stopped.”

Ci'en's eyes were hollow.

> “It was Yinggu…
> It was the child…
> They would not forgive me…”

Master Yideng sighed.

> “It is not they who will not forgive you.
> It is you who will not forgive yourself.”

Mother told me that from that day onward, Ci'en never again lost himself to his murderous rage.

He followed Master Yideng across the land, living a life of austerity and spiritual practice until the very end.

Later, he died at the hands of the Golden Wheel Dharma King—

No.

Mother corrected herself.

She said the proper word was **“entered Nirvana.”**

At the final moment of his life,

he found himself again.

---

## IV. Between Death and Life, Only One Breath

After Mother left, I sat alone for a long time.

Outside, osmanthus blossoms fell in a soft, endless drift. The wind lifted the petals and scattered them like fragments of gold.

I looked down at my hands.

Ten slender fingers.

Hands that can hold a brush.

Hands that can wield a sword.

Hands that can touch a flower petal and feel the delicate ridges beneath its surface.

I drew a deep breath.

The cool air entered my lungs, carrying the sweetness of osmanthus with it.

And in that moment, for the first time in my life, I understood—not merely in my mind, but in my flesh and blood—

that being alive means that one might just as easily have **not been alive**.

I grew up in Xiangyang.

The sound of war has always been the sound beside my pillow.

New graves appear on the city walls every day.

I know what war is.

I know that swords have no eyes.

I have seen death.

But all of that was always **someone else's story**.

Until today.

Today I learned that I myself had once stood so close to death that I could have breathed its breath—

separated from it by nothing more than the pressure of a single finger.

Had that monk tightened his hand by the smallest measure, there would be no sixteenth birthday today.

No mother sitting beside me, speaking softly.

No father in his study, reading military reports.

No grandfather on Peach Blossom Island, drunkenly complaining about his “Little Eastern Heretic.”

The world would be missing one person.

**Guo Xiang.**

Just as Aunt Yinggu's baby died before he ever had the chance to grow.

He remained forever a few months old.

Forever frozen in that little blue-purple face.

Forever held in a mother's arms that could no longer make him warm.

A story with no continuation.

I thought of Yinggu.

I have met her a few times—a thin old woman with sparse white hair, eyes hardened by ice, words as rigid as iron, never a smile upon her face.

I once thought she was simply strange.

Now I understand.

There is a piece missing inside her.

Her child never grew up.

He never turned sixteen.

He never smelled the fragrance of osmanthus.

He never waited for his mother to take his hand and tell him the story of the past.

And I—

I had almost become **another one of them**.

---

## V. Monk and Demon: A Single Thought Away

I asked Mother:

> “What happened to Ci'en afterward?”

She told me that he followed Master Yideng across the world, walking from place to place.

It became a long journey of self-redemption, almost like a sentence he had imposed upon himself.

He never killed again.

He eventually died in the Stone Maze of the Valley of Passionless Love, fighting the Golden Wheel Dharma King. A devastating blow shattered his skull.

At the edge of death, he recited the Buddha's name.

Then he looked toward Master Yideng and said:

> “Master… this time… I did not break the precept against killing.”

Until the very end,

he was fighting himself.

I do not know how I should judge him.

Should I hate him?

He nearly killed me.

And yet Mother told me that the reason his hand loosened was those five words:

**“Yinggu's son.”**

In other words, he spared me precisely because he had once killed another infant—and had suffered from that crime for more than twenty years.

He had suffered so deeply that merely hearing the child's name was enough to shatter his mind.

His hands were stained with blood.

And his heart had been burned by that blood, day after day, year after year.

Grandfather once said:

> “An evil man is not necessarily ignorant of his own evil.
> The deepest pain of an evil man is knowing that he has done evil.”

Qiu Qianren—

Ci'en—

He knew.

He knew his crimes.

He knew the child had been innocent.

He knew that he deserved the deepest punishment of the underworld.

He became a monk not to escape punishment.

He was trying to escape—

not from other people,

but from himself.

And he could not escape.

He never could.

If I were to meet him one day on the road to the underworld, and the Judge asked him to account for his crimes, I imagine he would lower his head and say:

> “I killed an infant.”

Then the Judge might turn to me.

> “He belongs in hell. Do you have anything to say?”

What would I say?

Perhaps I would remain silent for a very long time.

And then:

> “The moment he loosened his hand was the greatest good he ever did.
> But that good cannot outweigh the child he killed.
> So I have nothing to say.
> Let the child speak.”

---

## VI. I Owe This World My Life

Just as Mother was leaving, she suddenly turned back.

She embraced me tightly.

She said nothing.

But in her arms was all the force of someone who had survived a catastrophe.

I knew what she was thinking.

Had she not shouted those words in the Valley of Passionless Love, there would be nothing in her arms tonight but empty air.

And suddenly I understood:

**This life of mine does not belong to me alone.**

It was bought by Mother's desperate flash of insight.

It was recovered by Father's leap.

It was left behind by the last fragment of remorse still alive inside Ci'en as he stood upon the edge of madness.

And, in some terrible and distant way,

it was also bought by the life of Yinggu's child.

Without that child's death, there would have been no demon haunting Ci'en.

Without that demon, the child's name would not have broken through the darkness and made him loosen his grip.

Perhaps every person who is alive owes something to many of the people who are dead.

This is not some mysterious metaphysical law.

The chain of cause and consequence was forged there in the Valley of Passionless Love.

It was as real as blood.

One person's evil planted another person's pain.

That pain endured for years and years.

And then, one day, by some cruel accident of fate, it stopped another act of evil from being completed.

Evil did not become good.

Evil remained evil.

But evil tore open a crack.

And through that crack,

**the light came in.**

I was not killed.

**I am that light.**

---

## VII. The Night of My Sixteenth Birthday

The sky is completely dark now.

I put down my brush.

I blow out the candle.

I lie in the darkness, staring at the vague outline of the canopy above me.

I try to imagine myself as an infant—

lifted into the air by hands like iron bands,

my face swollen and purple,

my legs kicking wildly,

my cry trapped somewhere inside my throat.

The image is terrifying.

And yet, strangely,

I am not afraid.

Because I know what came afterward.

Afterward, those hands loosened.

Someone caught me and held me tightly.

It was Mother.

Her heartbeat pressed against my ear.

**Thud.**

**Thud.**

**Thud.**

Heavy and steady.

Like a war drum.

Like the first pendulum of life.

That might have been the last sound I ever heard in this world.

But it was not.

I would hear many more.

Hooves striking the earth.

A sword cutting through the wind.

Someone singing beneath the moon.

Someone laughing in the snow.

Autumn wind moving through the forest.

Fine rain tapping against the roof tiles.

Osmanthus blossoms falling softly to the ground.

I will live for a long time.

I will travel far.

I will meet many people.

I will love someone—

perhaps more than one.

All of it began, quietly,

in the instant that hand loosened.

Thank you,

to the hand that finally opened.

Thank you,

to Mother, for shouting those words.

And thank you,

to the child I never knew,

who died before I was old enough to know that he existed.

Though I will never have the chance to stand before him and say it,

I am grateful.

I am alive.

**I must live well.**

<br>

**Guo Xiang**
*Written on the night of my sixteenth birthday*
*The Guo Residence, Xiangyang*

> *A young girl's first understanding of life, good and evil, and the strange architecture of cause and consequence—after learning how close her own life once stood to death.*
>
> **The light enters through the crack.
> And after surviving the darkness, a person learns to see.**
