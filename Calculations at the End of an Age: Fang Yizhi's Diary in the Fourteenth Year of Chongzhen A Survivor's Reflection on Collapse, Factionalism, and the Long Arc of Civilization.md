Calculations at the End of an Age: Fang Yizhi's Diary in the Fourteenth Year of Chongzhen
A Survivor's Reflection on Collapse, Factionalism, and the Long Arc of Civilization

Keywords: Culture, Intellectual History, Historical Psychology, Ming-Qing Transition

Prologue: A Boat on the Rapids of Wan'an

Autumn, 1671 (Kangxi Year 10), aboard a boat in Jiangxi

As I write these words, our vessel drifts through the waters of the Fearful Rapids.

Outside my window, the river flows as it always has. The current rushing past the hull sounds no different from what Wen Tianxiang must have heard nearly four centuries ago when he passed this same stretch of water.

I am sixty-one years old.

My body is failing.

Government escorts accompany me south toward Guangdong for questioning. Officially it is an investigation. Unofficially, everyone understands what such journeys often mean.

Yet I feel no fear.

Only a strange calm.

For thirty years, certain questions have followed me from Nanjing to Beijing, from court chambers to mountain monasteries, from political struggle to spiritual retreat.

At last, I think I understand the answers.

The trouble is that some answers take an entire lifetime to digest.

So before the river carries me away, I wish to set down my reflections, not as a minister, nor as a monk, but as a scholar who lived through the end of a world.

I. The Restoration Society Years (1634–1641)
When We Thought We Could Save the World

In 1634, I was twenty-four years old.

My family had relocated to Nanjing.

The city possessed a peculiar atmosphere. Banquets, music, poetry, and scholarly gatherings filled the streets, yet everyone sensed the same underlying truth:

The Ming dynasty was weakening.

To the north, rising Manchu forces pressed steadily forward.

Across central China, peasant rebellions spread.

Inside the palace, the Chongzhen Emperor worked tirelessly, but the machinery of government seemed unable to reverse the decline.

My family belonged intellectually to the Donglin tradition.

My father, Fang Kongzhao, had opposed Wei Zhongxian and suffered political persecution for it. Later he served the dynasty with distinction, winning victories on the battlefield only to fall victim to court intrigue.

It was in this atmosphere that I joined with Hou Fangyu, Chen Zhenhui, Wu Yingji, and other members of the Restoration Society (Fushe).

We imagined ourselves defenders of justice.

Guardians of public morality.

Heirs to Donglin ideals.

We debated politics.

Reviewed scholars.

Discussed reforms.

Dreamed of saving the empire.

Many nights ended with tears, wine, declarations of loyalty, and grand promises to devote our lives to the realm.

At the time, it felt noble.

Looking back, I see something else.

Psychological Note I: Group Polarization

The gatherings of the Restoration Society resembled what modern psychology calls group polarization.

Under pressure and uncertainty, people increasingly reward those who express the strongest commitment to group values.

Moderate voices become suspect.

Nuance looks like weakness.

Compromise becomes betrayal.

The more passionately we spoke, the narrower our field of vision became.

Complex political crises were reduced to moral battles between the righteous and the corrupt.

The seeds of future factional warfare were planted in those very meetings.

As conditions worsened, we spoke endlessly about enemies within the court.

Far less about practical solutions.

Moral denunciation brought prestige.

Administrative competence brought little glory.

Only later did I realize that many of us were playing a familiar game.

We spoke of public good.

We calculated private advancement.

We condemned corruption.

We competed for influence.

Different banners.

Similar incentives.

Psychological Note II: Individual Rationality, Collective Failure

Every member of such a system acts rationally according to personal incentives.

Public moral posturing generates status.

Political attacks strengthen factional standing.

Difficult reforms carry enormous risks and uncertain rewards.

As more individuals choose politically rewarding behaviors, the entire group drifts away from solving actual problems.

What appears rational at the individual level produces irrational outcomes collectively.

The result:

Much discussion.

Little resolution.

II. The Fall of Beijing (1644)
The Collapse of Illusions

In 1644, Li Zicheng entered Beijing.

The dynasty effectively ended.

I wept before the emperor's spirit.

I was captured.

Tortured.

Interrogated.

Yet I refused to submit.

Later, amid the chaos, I escaped south.

For the first time in my life, I confronted death directly.

Not merely personal death.

Civilizational death.

The death of assumptions.

The death of certainty.

A question haunted me:

Why had the Ming collapsed?

Most people point to external causes.

Manchu expansion.

Rebellions.

Fiscal crisis.

Natural disasters.

All of these mattered.

Yet something deeper troubled me.

When the state faced extinction, the educated elite rarely acted as a united class.

Instead, many continued calculating personal advantage.

When the Chongzhen Emperor pleaded for financial support to sustain the war effort, many wealthy officials hesitated.

Men who spoke endlessly of loyalty became extremely careful accountants when sacrifice became necessary.

This was not limited to one faction.

It was systemic.

Everyone was calculating.

Everyone was hedging.

Everyone was searching for the safest path through catastrophe.

Psychological Note III: Cognitive Scarcity and Short-Term Thinking

When uncertainty becomes overwhelming, cognitive resources become scarce.

Long-term strategy gives way to immediate survival concerns.

Individuals focus on personal security, family protection, and preservation of assets.

Grand collective goals are pushed aside.

At scale, this creates institutional paralysis.

A ruling class loses the ability to think beyond the next crisis.

What emerges is not strategy, but reaction.

Not planning, but improvisation.

III. Southern Ming Politics (1644–1650)
Winning Factions, Losing the Country

After escaping south, I witnessed both the Hongguang and Yongli regimes.

Their names differed.

Their internal dynamics did not.

Factional conflict consumed immense amounts of energy.

Political rivals were often viewed as more urgent threats than external enemies.

This reality eventually exhausted my hopes.

Repeated summons to high office were met with repeated refusals.

Not because I lacked ambition.

Because I had begun to understand what was happening.

Many political actors still approached events primarily as struggles for influence rather than struggles for survival.

The empire burned.

And people fought over seating arrangements.

Psychological Note IV: The Tyranny of Immediate Identities

Social identity theory suggests people often gain stronger emotional satisfaction from competing against nearby rivals than confronting distant threats.

Internal opponents are visible.

External dangers are abstract.

As a result, groups under severe pressure sometimes intensify internal conflict rather than reduce it.

This tragic inversion characterized much of the Southern Ming experience.

Everyone sought advantage.

No one controlled the outcome.

And eventually the outcome controlled everyone.

IV. Captivity, Monastic Life, and the Burden of Survival
"The Death I Owed"

In 1650, I was captured.

A Qing commander placed two options before me:

Official robes.

Or a sword.

I walked toward the sword.

Impressed by this gesture, he ultimately allowed me to become a monk instead.

Thus I became Hongzhi, later known as the Medicine-Ground Monk.

Many scholars later described the emotional condition of Ming loyalists using a haunting phrase:

"The death one owed."

I understood it well.

I had not died with the dynasty.

Yet I could never fully forgive myself for surviving it.

Monastic life became both refuge and struggle.

Withdrawal and resistance.

Despair and reconstruction.

Psychological Note V: Post-Traumatic Growth

Modern psychology recognizes that some individuals respond to devastating events not by returning to who they were, but by developing entirely new frameworks of meaning.

For me, scholarship became that framework.

When political action failed, intellectual exploration remained.

The search shifted from saving a dynasty to preserving civilization itself.

V. Dongxi Jun and the Search for a Larger Perspective
Beyond Factions

The central intellectual project of my later years became Dongxi Jun (On the Equalization of East and West).

At its heart lay a simple question:

How do we escape narrow perspectives?

I developed a framework often summarized as:

Following (Sui)
Transcending (Min)
Integrating (Tong)

Most people live in the first mode.

They identify completely with one position.

One faction.

One identity.

One side.

The second step requires recognizing the opposing perspective.

The third requires integrating both into a larger understanding.

The tragedy of the late Ming was not merely military defeat.

It was cognitive imprisonment.

Too many people remained trapped inside their respective camps.

Very few could step outside them.

Fewer still could see the whole.

Psychological Note VI: Cognitive Defusion and Integrative Thinking

Modern psychology offers parallel concepts.

People become fused with identities, ideologies, and group loyalties.

Growth requires developing distance from those commitments and eventually integrating competing perspectives into more sophisticated frameworks.

Such capacities are difficult under conditions of extreme stress.

Which is precisely why they become so important.

VI. The Long Cycle
What Actually Endures?

Sitting aboard this boat, I find myself asking a final question.

What survives?

Not temporary victories.

Not appointments.

Not promotions.

Not factional triumphs.

Not court intrigues.

Time erases all of them.

Yet something remains.

Ideas remain.

Knowledge remains.

Questions remain.

The human search for truth survives the collapse of regimes.

My writings covered philosophy, history, language, science, music, medicine, mathematics, and cosmology.

Not because I believed myself important.

But because I increasingly believed that civilizations endure through accumulated understanding rather than political success alone.

A dynasty may fall.

Inquiry continues.

Psychological Note VII: Legacy Motivation

Psychologists describe a powerful human drive to create things that outlast one's own lifespan.

Knowledge.

Values.

Institutions.

Art.

Ideas.

After political hopes disappeared, I invested entirely in that form of continuity.

By contributing to a longer intellectual tradition, life regained meaning beyond contemporary defeat.

Epilogue: A Final Message to Posterity

If there is one lesson I would leave behind, it is this:

When systems enter crisis, personal calculation becomes instinctive.

But instinct is not always wisdom.

The officials who preserved their wealth ultimately lost it.

The faction leaders who outmaneuvered rivals ultimately lost their world.

The cleverest calculations often perish alongside the system that made them possible.

What endures are the things that transcend immediate advantage.

Knowledge.

Thought.

Moral imagination.

The pursuit of truth.

The preservation of human dignity.

The river outside still rushes forward.

Empires rise.

Empires fall.

Parties form.

Parties vanish.

Yet the wheel of civilization continues turning.

And so, at last, the Fearful Rapids no longer frighten me.
