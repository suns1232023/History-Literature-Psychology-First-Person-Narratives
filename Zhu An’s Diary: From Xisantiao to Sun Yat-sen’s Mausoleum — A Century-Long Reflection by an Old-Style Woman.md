# **Zhu An’s Diary: From Xisantiao to Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum — A Century-Long Reflection by an Old-Style Woman**

**Date:** The 18th day of the twelfth lunar month, Year 25 of the Republic of China (December 18, 1936), night

**Place:** No. 21 Xisantiao Hutong, Beiping — the Zhou residence

**Recorded by:** Zhu An, 58, the lawful wife of Lu Xun (Zhou Shuren)

**Background:** More than two months have passed since “Mr. Da” died; Sai Jinhua died half a month ago. Today, while sorting through Mr. Da’s books, I came across his correspondence and newspaper clippings concerning young people in Taiwan. My emotions surged. Amid the static of the radio at midnight, I seemed to hear a voice from the future — a woman speaking through tears at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum. Was it real? Was it an illusion? I shall record it nonetheless.

---

## **I. The Voice from the Future on the Radio at Midnight**

The kerosene lamp has grown dim again.

I added some oil, and the flame flickered before brightening slightly. This is still the lamp that once stood in Mr. Da’s study. I have polished its glass shade until it shines, but no matter how bright it becomes, it cannot illuminate the growing coldness that fills this house.

It has been seventy-three days since Mr. Da left us.

Xu Guangping is in Shanghai handling the funeral arrangements, and Haiying is with her. Now only Xu Ma and I remain in this courtyard, two old women speaking to four walls.

During the day, Xu Ma went back to the countryside to visit relatives and will not return for three to five days. So I am alone, guarding this empty courtyard and the books Mr. Da left behind.

Today, while sorting through his collection, I found a rattan trunk at the bottom of the bookshelf. A thick layer of dust covered it. It must not have been opened for many years.

Inside were letters, newspaper clippings, and magazines, all carefully arranged.

When I looked through them casually, I discovered that many concerned Taiwan.

There was a bundle of the *Taiwan Minpao*, its pages yellowed and brittle. Mr. Da had apparently asked someone to send them from Japan. Among them were articles by Zhang Wojun, as well as reprints of Mr. Da’s writings.

One letter was from Zhang Wojun to Mr. Da. The handwriting was neat, and the tone respectful:

> “Dear Mr. Lu Xun: Reading *Call to Arms* and *Wandering* was like seeing a lamp in the darkness. The young people of Taiwan have suffered under colonial rule for so long. Your words are like a knife, cutting open the spirit of servility and awakening the human heart…”

Although I cannot truly read, after so many years of hearing people read aloud, I have learned to recognize some characters. Xu Ma often read newspapers to me when she was here.

I read that letter one character at a time. It took me half an hour.

The night deepened.

The winter wind in Beiping blew fiercely, like countless hands beating against the window lattice.

I sat in the rattan chair — the chair Mr. Da often used, its back slightly collapsed from years of his leaning against it — staring blankly at the pile of materials concerning Taiwan.

Suddenly, the radio in the corner began to speak.

It was a Japanese-made radio Mr. Da had bought while he was alive. After his death, I had never turned it on.

Yet tonight it came alive by itself.

First came a harsh crackling sound, like countless people whispering somewhere far away.

Then the static gradually faded, and a woman’s voice became clear.

Her voice trembled with emotion, yet she struggled to remain composed. Each word struck my heart:

> “On March 12, 1925, Father of the Nation Sun Yat-sen passed away. Thirty thousand Taiwanese people took to the streets, shouting, ‘Down with imperialism! Down with the warlords!’…”

My entire body trembled.

The woman’s voice continued, intermittently breaking with emotion:

> “At that time Taiwan had already been a Japanese colony for thirty years… The identity of the Taiwanese people was complicated, and their circumstances were difficult. They could not express their grief over the death of Dr. Sun as openly as Chinese people on the mainland…”

> “On the mainland, commemorating Dr. Sun Yat-sen was natural and entirely legitimate. But in Taiwan at that time, commemorating Dr. Sun required extraordinary caution — people had to find ways to do it quietly and secretly…”

> “Because China was weak, Taiwan became a colony after the defeat in the First Sino-Japanese War…”

I suddenly stood up, but my legs gave way and I collapsed back into the chair.

The voice continued, speaking of “peace across the Taiwan Strait,” of “planting seeds,” of “a towering tree…”

I listened and listened.

At some point, tears had filled my face.

This was not the voice of the present.

It was 1936.

Dr. Sun Yat-sen had died in 1925, when Taiwan was already under Japanese colonial rule.

Yet the woman on the radio had said “Taiwan at present.”

What did “present” mean?

Who was she?

When was she speaking?

I rushed to the radio.

The dial was turned off.

The power cord hung loosely. It was not even plugged in.

Yet the voice continued.

Clear.

Real.

Spoken in Mandarin with a Nanjing accent, carrying the peculiar echo of the Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum.

I collapsed onto the floor.

I stared at the silent radio and listened to that voice crossing time.

Suddenly I understood.

This was a voice from the future.

A voice from some moment that had not yet arrived, spoken by a woman at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum, traveling across decades to reach this cold and lonely room.

Heaven had allowed me to hear it.

---

## **II. Taiwan Through Mr. Da’s Eyes: The Suffering of Colonial Youth**

I steadied myself, got up, and spread all the materials from the rattan trunk across the table.

The kerosene lamp cast a warm circle of light over the yellowed pages.

I read them one by one. Although I could not understand everything, by piecing together fragments, I gradually grasped the larger picture.

Mr. Da cared about Taiwan.

It was not vague sympathy.

It was concrete concern.

From his correspondence with Zhang Wojun, it was clear that he understood the details of Taiwan’s colonial circumstances, the Japanese restrictions on culture, and the profound dilemma of young Taiwanese who were neither fully accepted as Japanese nor able to openly assert themselves as Chinese.

One newspaper clipping from the *Shen Bao* in 1930 reported on student movements in Taiwan.

Mr. Da had underlined a passage in red:

> “The Japanese authorities have prohibited Taiwanese schools from teaching Chinese and have forcibly promoted Japanese as the ‘national language.’ Young Taiwanese who wish to study the language of their ancestral homeland must do so secretly, as though they were criminals.”

Beside it, Mr. Da had written in hurried handwriting:

> “This is what cultural annihilation means. Destroy their written language, and you destroy their history; destroy their history, and you destroy their nation.”

Another was a translated article from the Japanese left-wing magazine *Kaizo*, analyzing the Taiwanese anti-colonial movement.

Mr. Da had underlined:

> “The Taiwanese intellectual elite’s admiration for Sun Yat-sen’s revolution reflects their yearning for national liberation. Under colonial rule, this yearning can only be suppressed and transformed into literary resistance.”

His note read:

> “Literature is a weapon. Thunder can be heard in silence.”

I suddenly remembered something Mr. Da once said.

Not to me, but to a visiting friend.

I had brought tea into the room, and they were discussing literature from Northeast China under occupation.

Mr. Da said:

> “Colonial literature is resistance literature at its core. On the surface it may write about flowers and moonlight; beneath that surface are blood and tears. Taiwan is the same. So is the Northeast.”

The friend asked:

> “Have you ever thought about Taiwan being restored to China?”

Mr. Da remained silent for a long time before answering:

> “If China remains weak, Taiwan will never be restored. The humiliation of the First Sino-Japanese War and the pain of the Treaty of Shimonoseki are not merely the loss of an island. They are the weakness of an entire nation. To save Taiwan, China must first become strong.”

At the time, I did not understand the weight of those words.

Now, hearing the woman on the radio say that Taiwan became a colony because China was weak, I suddenly understood.

Mr. Da did not see Taiwan simply as a distant island.

He saw it as a mirror.

A mirror reflecting China’s weakness.

A mirror reflecting national humiliation.

A mirror reflecting the responsibility of intellectuals.

His relationship with Zhang Wojun and other young Taiwanese was not merely literary friendship. It was a form of intellectual solidarity.

They hated colonial rule.

They longed for national strength.

They wanted to awaken people through writing.

Zhang Wojun wrote:

> “When young Taiwanese read your *The True Story of Ah Q*, they see the spirit of Ah Q’s self-deception reflected in themselves. They are ashamed, and then they rise in anger.”

Mr. Da replied:

> “The ability to feel shame is the beginning of awakening. The most frightening thing is when people no longer know how to feel shame.”

These letters, these clippings, these handwritten annotations — beneath the kerosene lamp, they seemed suddenly alive.

They were no longer merely piles of old paper.

They were the breath of an era.

The pulse of a nation.

And I, an old-style woman who had never truly been educated, was touching through these pages a territory within Mr. Da’s heart that I had never entered:

his concern for Taiwan,

his pain over colonial peoples,

his anxiety about the destiny of the nation.

---

## **III. The Confusion of an Old-Style Woman: Who Was She, and Why Was She Crying?**

The voice on the radio gradually weakened.

At last, it dissolved into static and disappeared.

Silence returned to the room.

Only the wind outside grew stronger.

I sat at the table, staring into the darkness, my mind filled with the woman’s voice.

Who was she?

What was her name?

What did she look like?

How old was she?

Why was she speaking at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum?

And why had she choked back tears eight times?

“Zheng Liwen.”

A name suddenly appeared in my mind.

I do not know where it came from.

But there it was — clear and certain.

Zheng Liwen.

A woman from the future.

A woman whose name I had never heard.

From her speech, I could infer several things.

First, she was Taiwanese, or deeply concerned with Taiwan. She understood Taiwan’s colonial history intimately and seemed to feel the historical circumstances surrounding Sun Yat-sen’s death as though they were her own.

Second, she was speaking at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum.

That was the tomb of the Father of the Nation.

Someone speaking there could hardly be an ordinary person.

Perhaps she was an official.

Perhaps a representative.

Perhaps some important public figure.

Third, she had choked with emotion eight times.

Why?

Was it because of history?

Because of the present?

Because of Taiwan’s past?

Or because of relations across the Taiwan Strait?

Fourth, she had spoken of “Taiwan at present.”

When was her present?

From her tone, it clearly was not 1936.

She seemed to suggest that Taiwan was still in a situation where commemorating Sun Yat-sen required caution, where people had to act secretly.

Could Taiwan still not have been restored to China?

Or had it been restored, only to encounter new problems?

I could answer none of these questions.

But I could feel her emotions.

That profound grief.

That suppressed anger.

That pain before history.

That longing for peace.

As an old-style woman, almost every woman I had known in my life had been like me:

bound feet,

little education,

married according to family arrangements,

expected to obey her husband,

expected to accept whatever fate assigned her.

The best possible life was to become a virtuous wife and mother.

The worst was to become a widow in all but name.

I had never seen a woman stand in a place like Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum and speak publicly about national affairs.

Nor had I ever seen a woman speak about history until she choked with emotion — not for herself, but for an island, a people, and the pain of colonial history.

This was beyond anything I could imagine.

In my limited understanding, women were supposed to be quiet, obedient, and hidden behind men.

Among Mr. Da’s progressive acquaintances, there were indeed several modern women, such as Xu Guangping. They read, wrote, and participated in society.

But even Xu Guangping had never stood at a place like Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum to deliver such a speech.

And this Zheng Liwen — she not only spoke publicly, but spoke about the grandest subjects imaginable:

cross-Strait relations,

peace,

history,

the future.

What kind of woman was she?

I could not imagine her appearance.

But I imagined that she must have bright eyes, capable of seeing through the fog of history.

She must have a strong heart, capable of bearing the weight of her era.

And she must possess a voice courageous enough to say what many others dared not say.

And what about me?

I had never raised my voice in my entire life.

During my thirty-seven years in the Zhou household, I always spoke softly and timidly, afraid of saying something wrong, afraid of displeasing someone.

My voice was like the chirping of an autumn insect — faint, brief, and unheard.

When Mr. Da was alive, my greatest act of courage was standing outside his study listening to him speak.

My greatest expression of affection was making him a pot of tea every morning and silently placing it on his desk.

My world was no larger than this courtyard.

My concerns were limited to Mr. Da and Mother Zhou.

My deepest pain was an unloving marriage.

But Zheng Liwen — how large was her world?

How many people did she care about?

How deep was her pain?

I do not know.

But I know this:

She and I are both women.

We both possess hearts that can hurt and eyes that can shed tears.

Her tears were for millions of Taiwanese people.

Mine had been only for myself.

Her choking voice was history choking on its own grief.

My silence was the silence of an individual woman.

The distance between us was like the distance between galaxies.

---

## **IV. History in the Rearview Mirror: From Colony to “Cautious and Secretive”**

I reopened the newspaper clippings, trying to understand Taiwan’s past through Mr. Da’s eyes — and to understand what Zheng Liwen meant by “the present.”

A 1932 issue of the *Taiwan New People’s Daily* contained a report that startled me:

> “The Japanese authorities strictly prohibit Taiwanese people from publicly commemorating Sun Yat-sen. During the anniversary of the Xinhai Revolution, merchants in Dadaocheng, Taipei, displayed banners celebrating the restoration of the Han nation. The military police forcibly removed them and arrested several people.”

Mr. Da had written beside it:

> “What colonialists fear most is national memory.”

Another Japanese magazine from 1934 reported on Taiwan’s literary circles:

> “Taiwanese writer Lai He adapted Lu Xun’s ‘Hometown’ into ‘Returning Home,’ which was banned by the colonial authorities on the grounds of ‘disturbing public order.’ Taiwanese New Literature publications often featured special issues on Lu Xun and had to be circulated secretly.”

His annotation read:

> “Literature does not die; neither does a nation.”

These fragments gradually formed a clear picture.

Under Japanese colonial rule, Taiwanese people could face serious consequences for publicly commemorating Sun Yat-sen or reading Lu Xun.

Their national sentiments were suppressed, distorted, and forbidden from being openly expressed.

And Zheng Liwen had said:

> “On the mainland, commemorating Sun Yat-sen is natural and entirely legitimate. But in Taiwan today, commemorating Sun Yat-sen requires extraordinary caution, careful planning, and secrecy.”

Those words were like a key.

They unlocked my understanding.

If “today” referred not to 1936 but to some future era, then perhaps Taiwan had already been restored to China.

Japan had lost the war.

Taiwan had returned to China.

But why would commemorating Sun Yat-sen still require secrecy?

There could be only one explanation.

After restoration, Taiwan must have encountered new problems.

Perhaps internal political conflict.

Perhaps outside interference.

Perhaps historical wounds that never healed.

Perhaps these unresolved wounds had caused confusion, division, or even rejection surrounding the meaning of “China.”

And so commemorating Sun Yat-sen — the Father of the Chinese nation — had once again become politically sensitive.

People had to be careful.

They had to find ways to do it quietly.

They could not always do so openly.

How ironic.

Under Japanese colonial rule, commemorating Sun Yat-sen was prohibited because he represented opposition to colonialism and the revival of the Chinese nation.

But after Taiwan’s restoration, why would people once again be reluctant to commemorate him?

Could it be because Sun Yat-sen represented “China,” and some people no longer wished to identify themselves as Chinese?

I did not dare think further.

But Zheng Liwen’s tears seemed to say everything.

She was crying over the irony of history.

She was crying because the wounds of the nation had not healed.

She was crying because the distance between the two sides of the Taiwan Strait remained so deep.

She mentioned Zhang Wojun’s article, “May the Hero’s Tears Fill His Robe.”

I found the clipping.

Its summary contained a line:

> “Mr. Sun, do you know that somewhere on a lonely island overseas, there is also an unknown young man with tears streaming down his face…”

Only one sentence.

Yet it expressed the entire anguish of a colonial youth.

They longed for their homeland, yet lived under foreign rule.

They admired the Father of the Nation, yet dared not publicly mourn him.

They wanted to contribute to their nation, yet had nowhere to do so.

Zhang Wojun wrote those words in 1925.

Sun Yat-sen had just died.

Taiwan was still a Japanese colony.

A century later, Zheng Liwen quoted those words at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum and once again choked with emotion.

Why?

Because that emotion — the emotion of “tears filling the hero’s robe” — still existed.

The confusion over national identity among Taiwanese youth, the uncertainty surrounding cross-Strait relations, and the sensitivity toward historical wounds remained.

Time had passed.

But the wound had not fully healed.

Looking into history’s rearview mirror, I saw a winding road:

1895, the Treaty of Shimonoseki and the cession of Taiwan;

1936, Japanese colonial rule;

1945, Taiwan’s restoration;

and then the “present” spoken of by Zheng Liwen.

For more than a century, Taiwan’s fate had remained deeply connected to China, yet the path had been full of twists and turns.

If Mr. Da knew all this from beyond the grave, what would he think?

He had spent his life calling for the cultivation of independent human beings, for national strength, and for the destruction of servility.

If he knew that even after Taiwan’s restoration, young people still struggled with questions of identity, and that some people still had to commemorate Sun Yat-sen quietly and cautiously, would he feel sorrow?

Would he feel anger?

I think he would write even more firmly.

He would criticize even more sharply.

He would analyze even more deeply the illness within the national psyche.

And I could only sit in this cold and lonely room, facing a pile of old papers, and silently shed tears.

---

## **V. Two Women Looking at Each Other: Old-Style and Modern**

The night grew deeper.

I blew out the kerosene lamp and lay down, but sleep would not come.

Zheng Liwen’s voice still echoed in my ears, like the distant ringing of a mountain bell — long and heavy.

I began to think about the difference between her and me.

I am Zhu An.

I was born in the fourth year of the Guangxu era, 1878, in Shaoxing, Zhejiang.

My father was a merchant, and our family was reasonably well-off.

My feet were bound.

I attended a private school for three years and learned a few characters, but I was never truly educated.

At twenty-two, I married Zhou Shuren.

It was an old-style wedding: phoenix crown and red bridal robes, three bows and nine prostrations.

For thirty-seven years of marriage, there was neither love nor intimacy.

I lived like a widow while my husband was still alive.

I called him “Mr. Da.”

He called me “Mrs. Da.”

The closest we ever came was when he wrote in his study while I stood outside the door.

The greatest distance between us was the seven years he spent studying in Japan while I waited for him in Shaoxing.

I never traveled far.

I accomplished nothing great.

I never spoke loudly.

I was like a decorative object placed in the Zhou family’s sitting room, slowly covered in dust, slowly growing old.

And Zheng Liwen?

I do not know her exact background.

But from her speech, I imagine she was Taiwanese or had lived in Taiwan for many years.

She was well educated.

She was able to speak at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum, which meant she must have belonged to an influential segment of society.

She cared about politics.

She cared about cross-Strait relations.

She cared about the destiny of the nation.

She dared to express profound grief in public.

She was a modern woman — educated, thoughtful, responsible, influential.

She and I belong to two different eras of womanhood.

I represent the old era:

women confined to the household,

women defined as wives and mothers,

women whose value depended upon men.

My suffering was private.

Silent.

Unheard.

She represents the modern era:

women able to leave the household,

participate in public affairs,

express political opinions,

and influence the course of society.

Her suffering is public.

It has a voice.

People can hear it.

Is this progress?

Of course it is.

But why, when I heard her choking voice, did I feel an even deeper sadness?

Because although she could stand at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum and speak before thousands, what she spoke about was still national pain.

Still the shadow of history.

Still the separation across the Taiwan Strait.

The peace she longed for still seemed so far away.

Women had been liberated.

They could speak.

They could participate in politics.

Yet national questions, cross-Strait questions, and historical questions remained heavy as iron.

This reminded me of something Mr. Da once said:

> “What happens after Nora leaves home? She either falls or returns.”

Modern women had walked out of the household and into society.

But if society itself remained old — full of contradictions, divisions, and wounds — how far could modern women really go?

Zheng Liwen’s tears at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum were for Taiwan’s past, but also for the present relationship across the Taiwan Strait.

Her voice had reached the future — my present — which meant her speech carried weight.

Yet the fact that she still had to choke back tears eight times showed how heavy the problem remained.

This left me with a sense of helplessness.

The progress of an era seemed not to have healed its deepest wounds.

Women had been liberated, yet the nation remained divided.

Individuals had gained freedom, yet the collective still remained uncertain.

Zheng Liwen and I were separated by a century, yet we shared the same sense of powerlessness:

powerlessness before the wheel of history,

powerlessness before the destiny of a nation,

powerlessness before our own smallness.

The difference was that she could express her helplessness through a speech.

Mine could only be buried inside me.

---

## **VI. If Mr. Da Were Still Here: What Would He Say?**

Just before dawn, I had a dream.

I dreamed that Mr. Da was sitting in his study, wearing the long robe that had been washed so many times that its color had faded.

He was writing at his desk.

I opened the door.

He looked up at me.

His eyes were no longer as cold as they once had been.

There was instead a profound weariness in them.

“You heard it?” he asked.

I nodded.

“I heard it. A woman named Zheng Liwen was speaking at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum. She choked with emotion eight times.”

Mr. Da put down his pen and lit a cigarette.

Through the swirling smoke, his face became indistinct.

“Taiwan…” he slowly exhaled. “It remains a problem after all.”

“You were worried about it back then?”

“Worried.”

He nodded.

“The most poisonous aspect of colonial rule is not the exploitation of resources. It is the transformation of the human mind. Fifty years of colonial rule can make one generation forget its mother tongue, two generations forget its history, and three generations forget that they are Chinese.”

“And after restoration?”

“Restoration is easy. Healing the mind is difficult.”

He smiled bitterly.

“The Japanese may leave, but the scars remain. Some people became accustomed to being ‘imperial subjects’ and suddenly found themselves expected to become ‘Chinese.’ They could not adapt. Some had been educated to believe that Japanese civilization was superior and China backward. Others exploited those feelings to pursue division and private interests.”

“So people still have to commemorate Sun Yat-sen secretly?”

“Because Sun Yat-sen represents China.”

Mr. Da’s voice was low.

“Some people do not wish to recognize that China. Naturally, they suppress those who commemorate Sun Yat-sen. It is another form of colonialism — self-colonization. Becoming spiritual slaves willingly, while believing that it is a free choice.”

I was frightened.

“What can be done?”

Mr. Da remained silent for a long time.

Then he said:

“Education. Culture. Time.

“Let young people in Taiwan truly understand Chinese history, Chinese culture, and the Chinese spirit.

“Let them understand that Sun Yat-sen’s revolution was not only for the mainland, but also for Taiwan — for all Chinese people living under colonial rule.

“Let them understand that the two sides of the Taiwan Strait share blood and destiny.”

“Zheng Liwen is trying,” I said.

“Yes.”

Mr. Da nodded.

“That is why she chokes with emotion.

“Because she knows how difficult this is.

“The historical burden is too heavy. The realities of politics are too complicated. There is too much external interference.

“But she continues to speak.

“She continues to call for peace.

“She continues to plant seeds.

“That is hope.”

“Would you support her?”

Mr. Da did not answer directly.

Instead, he said:

“I spent my life writing, criticizing, attacking falsehoods, precisely in order to awaken people’s minds.

“If my words can help young people in Taiwan break through the spirit of self-deception, if they can give them the courage to confront real history, then my life of writing will not have been in vain.”

He paused.

Then he said:

“Tell her not to be afraid of choking with emotion.

“Choking means that there is still pain.

“It means there is still conscience.

“The most frightening thing is when people can no longer choke with emotion — when they become numb and indifferent.

“That is true despair.”

I cried.

In the dream, I cried like a child.

Mr. Da reached out and gently patted my shoulder.

It was the first time in thirty-seven years that he had touched me.

“You have suffered too,” he said.

Then I woke.

Dawn had arrived.

Winter sunlight filtered through the paper windows and cast mottled shadows across the floor.

I sat on the bed.

The traces of tears had not yet dried.

The dream had been so real.

So clear.

Mr. Da’s words still echoed in my ears.

---

## **VII. My Decision: I Will Write All of This Down**

I got up, washed my face, and ate a few mouthfuls of the porridge left from the previous night.

Then I sat at the table, unfolded a sheet of paper, and ground the ink.

I was going to write down everything that had happened the previous night.

Although I could not write very well,

although my words were clumsy,

although this diary might never be read by anyone,

I would still write.

For whom?

For Zheng Liwen in the future, if she should ever see it.

For everyone who cares about Taiwan, cross-Strait relations, and the destiny of the nation.

And for myself —

to prove that my entire life had not consisted only of silence,

only of waiting,

only of meaninglessness.

I began with the voice I had heard on the radio.

I wrote about Zheng Liwen’s speech.

I wrote about her tears.

I wrote about her account of Taiwan’s colonial history.

I wrote about her longing for peace across the Taiwan Strait.

I wrote about Mr. Da’s concern for Taiwan.

I wrote about his relationship with Zhang Wojun.

I wrote about the thoughts behind his annotations.

I wrote about my confusion, shock, and emotional response as an old-style woman.

I wrote about the twists and turns of more than a century of history in the rearview mirror.

I wrote about the encounter between two women.

I wrote about the progress of the age and the wounds that remained unhealed.

I wrote about the dream.

I wrote about Mr. Da’s words.

I wrote and wrote.

From morning until noon.

From noon until evening.

My hand grew sore.

My eyes grew tired.

Yet my heart became increasingly clear.

This was the first time in my life that I had expressed myself so freely.

Perhaps my words were awkward.

Perhaps my logic was imperfect.

Perhaps what I wrote was shallow and laughable.

But it was real.

Real confusion.

Real shock.

Real tears.

Real hope.

When I finished the final character, I put down the brush and breathed a long sigh of relief.

Outside, the sun was setting.

The afterglow turned the sky above Beiping red.

From somewhere in the distance came the melodious sound of a bell.

One stroke.

Two.

Three…

Would that sound reach the future?

Would Zheng Liwen hear it?

I do not know.

But I know this:

Once certain voices are spoken, they do not disappear.

Once certain emotions are expressed, they do not perish.

Once certain hopes are planted, they will take root, sprout, and grow.

Mr. Da spent his life writing for this very reason.

Zheng Liwen spoke at Sun Yat-sen’s Mausoleum for the same reason.

And I, an old-style woman who could barely write, recorded this diary for the same reason today:

**To let voices be heard.
To let history be remembered.
To let wounds heal.
And to let the seeds of peace take root in the hearts of Chinese people on both sides of the Taiwan Strait, sprout, and grow into a towering tree.**

# **Afterword: A Psychological Perspective**

This diary is presented as having been written on December 18, 1936, after Lu Xun’s death and shortly after the death of Sai Jinhua. From a psychological perspective, several aspects deserve attention.

### **1. Intergenerational Transmission and Resonance of Trauma**

Through the surreal experience of “hearing a future voice on the radio,” Zhu An establishes an emotional connection with Zheng Liwen a century later.

This resonance is based on a shared historical trauma: Taiwan’s colonial experience.

Although Zhu An did not personally experience Japanese colonial rule in Taiwan, she internalized this historical trauma through Lu Xun’s perspective.

Zheng Liwen, as a Taiwanese political figure, directly confronts the unresolved questions of historical identity.

Two women separated by time therefore share the same “historical pain,” illustrating the possibility of intergenerational transmission of traumatic memory.

### **2. Cognitive Breakthrough and Reconstruction of Self among an Old-Style Woman**

Zhu An spent most of her life in a state of “voicelessness,” with her self-understanding constrained by traditional ideals of female virtue.

Through her encounter with Lu Xun’s materials concerning Taiwan and her imagined experience of hearing Zheng Liwen’s speech, the boundaries of her cognition are disrupted.

She begins to think about nations, politics, cross-Strait relations, and other large-scale historical questions.

She also begins to express herself through writing.

This represents a reconstruction of the self in later life — a transformation from a passive “relic” into an individual capable of thought and expression.

Although this reconstruction remains limited and private, it is deeply meaningful for her personally.

### **3. Mirror Identification and Gender Reflection**

In Zheng Liwen, Zhu An sees an image of the “modern woman”:

educated,

articulate,

socially engaged,

and capable of participating in public affairs.

This image causes her to reconsider her own position as an “old-style woman.”

Yet she does not simply fall into inferiority or envy.

Instead, she engages in a dialectical reflection:

women may have achieved greater freedom,

but national problems remain unresolved.

This moves her thinking beyond her personal circumstances toward an understanding of the limitations of her historical era.

### **4. Historical Consciousness and Disturbance of Temporal Perception**

The “radio hallucination” can be interpreted as a projection of Zhu An’s historical consciousness.

She transforms Lu Xun’s concern for Taiwan and her own imagination of the future into the concrete experience of “hearing a speech from the future.”

This disturbance of temporal perception reflects her psychological position at a historical turning point:

the old era has passed,

while the new era remains uncertain.

By connecting the past represented by Lu Xun with the future represented by Zheng Liwen, she attempts to locate herself within history.

### **5. Mourning and the Construction of Meaning**

After Lu Xun’s death, Zhu An is presented as being in a profound state of mourning.

Sorting through his belongings and writing a diary become part of her mourning process.

Yet she does not remain trapped in personal grief.

Instead, she transforms mourning into reflection on the destiny of the nation.

By connecting Lu Xun’s concerns with Zheng Liwen’s appeal for peace, she gives Lu Xun’s death a broader meaning:

his spirit continues,

his words continue to influence people,

and his intellectual concerns continue to resonate across the Taiwan Strait.

Zhu An’s diary is therefore presented as the inner monologue of an old-style woman living through a period of dramatic historical transformation.

With limited education and simple language, she touches upon profound questions of national identity, historical trauma, and cross-Strait relations.

Her thinking may not be systematic or academically rigorous.

But precisely because it is intimate and sincere, her concern, emotional resonance, and desire for understanding possess their own power.

Looking into history’s rearview mirror, what we see is not merely Zhu An as an individual.

We also see the psychological landscape of an entire generation of Chinese people:

thinking amid suffering,

longing for unity amid division,

and searching for light beneath the shadow of history.

And perhaps that is where hope begins.

## **Author’s Note**

This work is a historical-psychological narrative.

It is based on historical materials but reconstructs events through literary imagination.

No original manuscript of Zhu An’s diary has survived. This text is therefore an imaginative reconstruction based on her known biography, personality, and historical circumstances.

The historical events, interpersonal relationships, and atmosphere depicted in the work are presented as being grounded in historical materials, while the psychological analysis draws upon relevant theories.

The purpose is to explore questions of collective trauma, historical memory, cross-Strait identity, and the psychological transformation of an old-style woman living through a period of profound historical change.

**This work should not be interpreted as an authentic historical diary written by Zhu An.**
