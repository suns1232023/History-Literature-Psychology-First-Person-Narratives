# **The Twilight of the Heroes' Gathering: The Last Testament of a Dying Eunuch**

**The eleventh day of the eleventh month, the seventh year of the Tianqi reign — A Bitterly Cold Night in Fucheng**

Tonight is colder than usual.

The footsteps outside are growing more frequent. I know they are running out of patience. The men sent by the Chongzhen Emperor have followed me all the way from Beijing. Officially, they are here to escort me. Unofficially, they are here to hurry me toward my death.

I do not blame him.

If I were in his position, I would kill Wei Zhongxian too. A new emperor ascends the throne. If he does not kill a powerful eunuch to establish his authority, how can he expect the empire to submit?

And yet I cannot help laughing.

Ah, Emperor Chongzhen. My poor Emperor Chongzhen.

Do you really think that by killing me, the Ming Dynasty will suddenly be made whole?

I lie here on this broken kang, listening to the wind outside, and suddenly I remember the fifth year of Tianqi.

That was the year Yang Lian died by my hand.

Zuo Guangdou died by my hand.

Wei Dazhong died by my hand.

I had their throats cut, their bones reduced to ash, and I stood outside the imperial prison watching civil and military officials kneel in rows before me, calling me **“Nine Thousand Years.”**

At the time, I believed this was my world.

Only now do I understand:

**The day I won was the day I began to lose.**

It sounds like a paradox. But the principle is simple.

I crawled out of the mud of Suning. I know what it means simply to stay alive.

For thirty years I survived inside the palace—from cleaning chamber pots, to overseeing the imperial kitchens, to entering the Directorate of Ceremonial, and finally to holding its seal. I have seen too many men build towering mansions, feast their guests, and watch the whole structure collapse.

But only during these last two years have I truly understood something:

**The empire is like a balance scale. It needs weight on both sides if it is to remain upright.**

## **I. The Balance**

It is true that we and the Donglin faction were mortal enemies.

Yang Lian accused me of twenty-four crimes. He would gladly have torn me to pieces.

I killed Yang Lian and Zuo Guangdou. I would gladly have ground their bones to dust.

We fought for years.

Countless men died.

The hatred between us was absolute.

Yet there is one thing I kept hidden in my heart and never told another soul:

**Without the Donglin faction, I could never have remained the Nine Thousand Years for so long.**

Even I find the statement absurd.

But it is true.

During the Tianqi Emperor's reign, the court was divided between two forces.

We, the eunuch faction, controlled the Directorate of Ceremonial, the Eastern Depot, and the Embroidered Uniform Guard. We controlled imperial red approvals, arrests, interrogations, and investigations.

The Donglin faction controlled the Censorate, the Six Offices, and the Hanlin Academy. They controlled impeachment, public criticism, and political opinion.

Whenever we wanted to do something, they watched us.

Whenever they wanted to do something, we blocked them.

Today they would accuse one of us of corruption.

Tomorrow we would accuse one of them of factionalism.

The emperor watched from above, sometimes leaning toward one side, sometimes toward the other.

And the people beneath them?

If they wanted promotion, they had to cultivate both sides.

They brought gifts to our eunuch faction, but they also had to send their seasonal offerings to the Donglin men.

No one dared offend either side.

Everyone had to appease both.

And so the balance swayed and trembled—

yet somehow, it held.

**Why?

Because both sides had weight.

Neither could swallow the other.**

When we wanted to abuse our power, the Donglin men shouted.

When the Donglin men wanted to push something too far, we blocked them.

They watched us.

We watched them.

They bit us.

We bit them.

Neither side was allowed to run unchecked.

That, I later learned, was called **“checks and balances.”**

One of the petty officials escorting me once called it a **“dual-pole system.”**

He said that when both poles remain, the system can remain stable.

I did not understand the terminology then.

But I understood the principle:

**As long as the Donglin faction stood opposite me, I could remain the Nine Thousand Years with surprising stability.**

## **II. I Dismantled the Other Side with My Own Hands**

But eventually I forgot that principle.

In the fifth year of Tianqi, Yang Lian died.

In the sixth year, Gao Panlong died.

Zhou Shunchang died as well.

The men who cursed me most fiercely were dragged one after another into the imperial prison.

One after another, they died by my hand.

Donglin academies were dismantled.

Donglin officials were executed, imprisoned, exiled.

The court became wonderfully quiet.

Only our own men remained, kneeling before me and shouting:

**“Nine Thousand Years!”**

Their voices shook the palace.

I stood above them at Meridian Gate, looking down, intoxicated by my own triumph.

At last, I thought.

No one opposes me anymore.

No one curses me anymore.

I can do whatever I please.

But gradually, something began to feel wrong.

First, no one told me the truth anymore.

When the Donglin men were still there, they cursed me.

They cursed me viciously.

But sometimes, buried among their insults, there were things that were actually true.

When I ordered the Ministry of Revenue to increase taxes, they accused me of imposing a tyranny worse than the tiger.

They said ordinary people could no longer survive.

I would become furious.

But somewhere inside, I would wonder:

Perhaps they are right.

Perhaps the taxes really are too heavy.

Now there was no one left to criticize me.

I said, “Increase the taxes.”

The Ministry of Revenue increased them.

No one asked:

How much?

Who will pay?

I said, “Delay the soldiers' pay.”

The Ministry of War delayed it.

No one asked whether the soldiers guarding the frontier had enough to eat.

Then the men beneath me began competing with one another to flatter me.

Before, even when they praised me, they had to be discreet.

They feared being impeached by the Donglin faction for flattering a eunuch.

Now the Donglin faction was gone.

So they stopped pretending.

Shrines to me appeared one after another.

From Jizhou to Miyun.

From Tongzhou to Zhuozhou.

Incense burned day and night.

One man wrote an inscription proclaiming that my virtue rivaled the ancient sage-kings.

I was embarrassed even to hear it.

They did not care.

The more shamelessly they flattered me, the faster they rose.

**This is the weakness of a single-pole system.

There is no corrective force.

No brake.

Only the accelerator, pressed all the way down.**

Those were my years of intoxication.

I believed I was the Mandate of Heaven itself.

I believed the whole empire adored me.

But what was actually happening?

The frontier armies were owed millions of taels.

Soldiers in Yanbian and Shaanxi were starving.

Disaster victims were beginning to rebel.

I barely noticed.

The reports coming from the Eastern Depot spoke only of peace throughout the realm and universal devotion to the throne.

When I wanted the truth, no one dared speak it.

When I did not want the truth, everyone gave me lies.

**This is what they call an “information bubble.”**

The same little official explained that to me.

When a system contains only one voice, everyone inside the system begins living in a dream.

They can no longer see the world outside it.

I, Wei Zhongxian, lived inside that dream.

In the dream, I was the Nine Thousand Years.

The empire adored me.

The people worshipped me.

Outside the dream, the Ming Dynasty was already rotting from within.

## **III. The Wolf Beyond the Frontier**

What I did not understand then was that outside my dream there was not merely a decaying Ming Dynasty.

There was also a wolf waiting beyond the frontier.

Its name was **Later Jin**.

During the Tianqi reign, the Later Jin state was constantly advancing.

Nurhaci died.

Hong Taiji succeeded him.

And the son was even more formidable than the father.

They fought us again and again beyond the passes.

Ningyuan.

Jinzhou.

Battle after battle.

In the sixth year of Tianqi, Yuan Chonghuan defeated them at Ningyuan.

That was while our faction still held power.

And the reason the battle could be fought was simple:

The soldiers received their supplies.

The artillery reached the front.

The Ministries of Revenue and War did their jobs.

Why?

Because I was watching.

I said:

“Ningyuan must not fall.

Anyone who delays this matter will pay with his entire family.”

No one dared delay.

The soldiers at the front had food.

They had ammunition.

They had artillery.

But after the Donglin faction disappeared, something changed.

In the seventh year of Tianqi, Hong Taiji attacked Ningyuan and Jinzhou again.

The battle was brutal.

The commanders at the front reported that the soldiers were short of pay and fighting on empty stomachs.

I flew into a rage.

I had ordered the Ministry of Revenue to release the funds.

Why were the soldiers still unpaid?

Then we investigated.

The silver had been siphoned off at every level.

By the time it reached the soldiers, barely a third remained.

The governor took one share.

The provincial authorities took another.

The commanders took another.

These bastards retreated when it was time to fight and rushed forward when it was time to divide the money.

If the Donglin faction had still existed, they would have turned the matter into a storm.

“Frontier troops unpaid! Eunuch faction embezzlement!”

Their impeachment memorials would have piled up to the roof.

The emperor would have been forced to intervene.

I would have been forced to restrain myself.

And the men beneath me would not have dared to steal quite so openly.

But there was no one left to shout.

So they stole more.

And more.

**That is another weakness of a single-pole system:

Without external oversight, corruption eventually becomes self-reinforcing.**

And the wolf beyond the frontier was watching.

They knew our soldiers were hungry.

They knew they lacked clothing.

They knew resentment was spreading.

So their agents began approaching them quietly.

They whispered:

“Come over.

There is food here.

There is silver here.”

I did not know how many defected.

Later I heard that soldiers defected in large numbers from Liaodong to Xuanda.

One of them was a young soldier named Wang Er.

He came from Shaanxi.

He had been forcibly conscripted and sent to Liaodong.

For six months he received no pay.

Then a letter came from home.

His parents had starved to death.

His younger sister had been sold into a brothel.

That evening, Wang Er stood on the wall and watched the Later Jin cavalry beyond it.

He touched his empty stomach.

That night, he and more than a dozen comrades defected.

There were stories like this everywhere.

One after another.

**But what was happening in the court?

No one reported it.**

The Eastern Depot was still busy arresting people who said inconvenient things.

The Embroidered Uniform Guard was still interrogating men suspected of sympathizing with the Donglin faction.

The wolf was almost through the door.

And we were still busy fighting among ourselves.

No.

That is not quite right.

**We were no longer fighting among ourselves.

There was no “other side” left.**

We were playing against ourselves.

And we were playing with extraordinary enthusiasm.

## **IV. What Emperor Chongzhen Inherited**

After the Tianqi Emperor died, Chongzhen ascended the throne.

He was an intelligent man.

Smarter than me.

The moment he took power, he understood that I had to be removed.

But he did not understand that by removing me, he was not merely eliminating one man.

He was inheriting a balance whose other side I had already destroyed.

He removed my side.

And when he finished—

**there was nothing left on the scale.**

I am sure that when Chongzhen ordered my execution, he thought:

Kill the eunuch.

Restore the Donglin officials.

Then the empire will recover.

But he failed to understand something else.

The Donglin faction was excellent at denouncing people.

It was considerably less impressive at governing.

When I held power, they accused me of tyranny.

They said my taxes were crushing the people.

Once they gained influence, one of the first things they did was abolish commercial and mining taxes.

They said:

“We must not profit from the people.

This is benevolent government.”

What they did not emphasize was that many of them were themselves among the largest landowners and merchants in Jiangnan.

Abolishing commercial taxes was, conveniently, a tax cut for themselves.

Then the treasury ran dry.

So agricultural taxes increased.

Military levies multiplied.

The Liaodong levy.

The military training levy.

The anti-bandit levy.

Layer upon layer was placed on the peasantry.

The harvest was no longer enough to pay the taxes.

People fled.

People rebelled.

Li Zicheng built his armies from precisely this misery.

When I was in power, the Donglin men accused me of destroying loyal ministers.

They claimed I had eliminated every capable general.

But look carefully.

Yuan Chonghuan served under the system I controlled.

Man Gui was promoted under it.

Sun Chengzong.

Yuan Keli.

These were men who defended the frontier while I held power.

I killed people who opposed me.

That is true.

But I did not casually remove commanders who could fight.

Because I understood one thing:

**At the frontier, the empire needed soldiers who could fight—not scholars who could merely write elegant accusations.**

Then the Donglin faction gained power.

Yuan Chonghuan was executed.

Man Gui died in battle.

Sun Chengzong was pushed aside.

And who remained?

Men who knew how to choose factions, but not how to fight wars.

By the seventeenth year of Chongzhen, Li Zicheng reached the gates of Beijing.

The emperor struck the bell and summoned the officials.

The court was almost empty.

Those men who had spent years speaking of benevolence and righteousness were either hiding at home, gathering their gold and silver, or quietly leaving the capital to negotiate with the rebels.

Qian Qianyi, one of the leading Donglin figures, eventually submitted to the Qing and even helped guide Qing forces in their search for Ming loyalists.

These men were fierce when denouncing others.

They were remarkably quick when surrendering.

Why?

Because what they cared about was not the Ming Dynasty.

They cared about themselves.

As long as the Ming survived, they could serve in office.

When the Ming disappeared, they could serve another master.

They could preserve their wealth under any banner.

I was different.

I knew that if the Ming Dynasty disappeared, my own power and fortune would disappear with it.

**This is what happens when a single-pole system faces external pressure.

Once that pole collapses, what remains is hollow.

A collection of people who protect themselves but no longer protect the system.**

When the enemy arrives, they flee.

Or surrender.

Or stand frozen and wait for death.

But almost no one stands forward and says:

**“I will bear this.”**

## **V. What I Finally Understand**

For the last few days, lying in this broken room, I have been thinking about all of this.

I did not understand it before.

I believed power had to be held in one man's hands.

Held tightly.

No one allowed to take even a grain of it away.

When I destroyed the Donglin faction, I thought I had won.

Only now do I understand:

**The day I won was the beginning of my defeat.**

Because I dismantled the other side of the balance.

Once that side was gone, the balance was no longer a balance.

It became a single plank standing alone.

How long can a single plank remain upright?

One strong wind is enough.

And beyond it stood the wolf.

And the starving soldiers.

And the desperate peasants.

And the rebels.

I, Wei Zhongxian, was once nothing more than a street tough from Suning.

I grew up rolling in the mud.

I became accustomed to being trampled.

I hated those who stepped on me.

I hated those who called me a useless eunuch.

So when I finally acquired power, I wanted every one of them kneeling beneath my feet.

What I never considered was this:

**Once they all knelt, I could no longer remain standing.**

As long as they stood opposite me, I knew what was right and what was wrong.

What could be done.

What must not be done.

When they disappeared, I became blind.

Deaf.

And I lived inside my own dream until the day the dream ended.

Chongzhen probably still does not understand.

He believes that once I am dead, and the Donglin officials are restored, the empire will recover.

But I know he will understand eventually.

When he sees Li Zicheng enter Beijing.

When he sees the Qing armies cross the passes.

When he sees the men who once preached righteousness shave their heads and kneel before a new master.

Then he will understand.

But by then it will be too late.

**Systemic collapse is not easily reversible.**

Like water spilled onto the ground, the original shape cannot simply be restored.

The Ming Dynasty is that spilled water.

## **VI. My Final Thought**

The footsteps outside are getting closer.

I know they are coming for me.

The brush hidden beneath my robe presses painfully against my chest.

It was a gift from the Tianqi Emperor.

I have lived many lives within this one.

From a street tough in Suning to the eunuch who held the imperial seal.

From Li Jinchong to Wei Zhongxian.

From being trampled into the mud to making the whole empire kneel and call me the Nine Thousand Years.

And now?

I am alone.

I will die in a ruined house in the middle of nowhere.

I remember one summer long ago.

I was kneeling before the Tianqi Emperor.

He was making a chair.

Wood shavings covered the floor.

Sunlight poured through the window and illuminated his young face.

Without looking up, he said:

“Zhongxian, you handle it.”

And I did.

I dismantled the other side of the balance.

I handled the empire.

I handled it until this was all that remained.

But if there is another life—

and if I am allowed to choose again—

I honestly do not know what I would choose.

Should I have kept the Donglin faction alive?

Let them curse me every day.

Impeach me every day.

Oppose me every day.

Or should I have done what I did?

Kill them all.

Enjoy several peaceful years.

Then watch the empire decay before my eyes.

I do not know.

**I know only this:

An empire needs two sides.**

Even if those two sides fight every day.

Even if they curse one another.

Even if they make each other's lives unbearable.

That is still better than having only one side.

With two sides, the balance can hold.

With two sides, the wolf outside is less likely to pounce.

With two sides, the Ming Dynasty might have endured a few years longer.

The door opens.

I close my eyes.

# **Afterword**

In the seventeenth year of the Chongzhen reign, Li Zicheng captured Beijing, and the Chongzhen Emperor hanged himself on Coal Hill.

Before his death, the emperor ordered Wei Zhongxian's remains to be gathered and buried at Biyun Temple in the Fragrant Hills.

That same year, Qing forces entered the Shanhai Pass.

Qian Qianyi, a leading Donglin figure, surrendered to the Qing.

A contemporary verse mocked him:

> “Qian's reputation for loyalty once reached the heavens;
> When the nation fell and the sovereign died, he cared not.
> Calmly he entered the new dynasty,
> As though nothing had happened.”

During the Southern Ming Hongguang regime, Ma Shiying and Ruan Dacheng continued their factional struggles while Qing forces advanced.

One censor reportedly declared:

> “I would rather die at the hands of the Qing than die at the hands of my own faction.”

At that moment, Qing armies had already breached Yangzhou, where Shi Kefa died defending the city.

During the Yongli reign of the Southern Ming, Donglin figures again supported the imperial court centered around the Prince of Gui, yet factional struggles continued.

When Qing forces eventually entered Yunnan, the Yongli Emperor fled toward Burma.

Some officials surrendered.

Others fled.

Few chose to die for the fallen dynasty.

Later, the Qing court compiled the *History of the Ming*.

The Donglin faction was treated as part of the orthodox political tradition.

Wei Zhongxian was placed in the chapter on eunuchs and remembered as the archetypal leader of the “eunuch faction.”

**The verdict of history had been written.**
