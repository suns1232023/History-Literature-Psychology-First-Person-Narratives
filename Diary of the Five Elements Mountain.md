# **Diary of the Five Elements Mountain**

### *A Monkey’s Five-Hundred-Year Meditation*

**Keywords:** #Culture #Media #Literature

---

## **Volume I · The Year the Mountain Came Down**

**Some day, some month, some year. Weather: No weather. Only earth.**

My name is **Sun Wukong**, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, the Protector of Horses—though I have always hated that second title.

Right now, I am buried beneath a mountain called the **Five Elements Mountain**.

Only my head is sticking out. I cannot move the rest of my body. I am like a grasshopper pinned to the ground.

That old fellow **Tathagata** bet me that I could not escape the palm of his hand.

I took the bet.

I lost.

He turned his palm over, and five fingers became five peaks—**metal, wood, water, fire, and earth**—and together they pinned me here.

Every day, the local mountain spirit brings me iron pellets and molten copper to eat and drink.

They taste a hundred thousand times worse than the wild peaches of Flower Fruit Mountain, yet somehow they have made me clearer-headed than all the celestial wine in Heaven.

For the first few days, I cursed.

I cursed the Jade Emperor and his ancestors. I cursed Tathagata and his lotus throne. I cursed the Queen Mother and her Peach Garden. I cursed Laozi and his furnace.

The old mountain spirit would cover his ears and hide somewhere far away, appearing only at mealtimes to leave the iron and copper before running off again.

After about three years, I stopped cursing.

Not because I had run out of words.

But because the words suddenly felt weightless—like autumn leaves spinning in the air before landing without making a sound.

I began counting ants.

Every now and then, a black ant would crawl through a crack in the mountain. I would watch it from the moment it left its nest, to the moment it found food, to the moment it carried that food home.

Sometimes I watched for an entire day.

Its busy little life made me laugh.

It thought it was moving food.

It never realized that **the entire mountain was a prison**.

By the fiftieth year, I had learned how to sleep.

Not the mortal kind of sleep.

I slept while awake.

My eyes remained open, while my mind became empty. Thoughts passed like clouds and flowing water—never stopping, never staying.

The word **stillness** that Patriarch Bodhi had taught me at Mount Lingtai, I finally understood beneath this mountain.

**Five hundred years.**

I do not know how many dynasties have risen and fallen in the human world, or how many Peach Banquets Heaven has held.

I know only this:

When the first ray of dawn had entered my eyes for the hundredth time in the fiftieth cycle of some forgotten year, I finally understood something.

**Those peaches were not stolen by me.**

**At least, not all of them.**

# **Volume II · The Days in the Peach Garden**

**Some day, some month, some year. Weather: Thinking about it still makes my blood boil.**

Let me tell you how it began.

The Jade Emperor once granted me the title **Great Sage Equal to Heaven**.

It sounded magnificent.

In reality, it was an empty title with no salary.

I had a grand office, no responsibilities, and nothing meaningful to do.

So I wandered around Heaven, befriending immortals of every rank. I treated everyone as an equal.

One day, Xu Jingyang, one of those celestial officials who could never mind his own business, warned the Jade Emperor:

> “If he remains idle, trouble will surely follow. Better give him something to manage.”

So the Jade Emperor appointed me keeper of the **Peach Garden**.

Looking back now, I realize how perfect the trap was.

**Everyone knows a monkey loves peaches.**

And the Great Sage’s residence was deliberately built beside the Peach Garden.

It was like **putting a cat in charge of fish**.

At the time, I thought it was a reward.

Now I understand.

It was bait.

The Peach Garden contained **3,600 peach trees**.

The first 1,200 produced fruit once every 3,000 years. Anyone who ate those peaches could become immortal.

The middle 1,200 produced fruit once every 6,000 years. Whoever ate them would live forever.

The final 1,200 produced fruit once every 9,000 years. Whoever ate those would enjoy the same longevity as Heaven and Earth.

When I first took charge, the mountain spirit showed me around.

“Great Sage,” he said, “the peaches in this garden are all **accounted for**. Every year, when the Queen Mother holds her Peach Banquet, the Seven Fairies come to collect them—two baskets of the best peaches, three of the middle kind, and two of the lesser kind. Not a single one may be missing.”

I laughed.

“Accounted for? Look at those trees. There must be tens of thousands of peaches. Who would notice if ten or twenty disappeared?”

The old spirit's face changed instantly.

He looked left.

Then right.

Then lowered his voice.

“Great Sage, please be careful what you say. You are not the only one watching this garden.”

Only then did I notice them.

At each corner of the garden stood a small pavilion.

Inside each pavilion sat an old immortal with a white beard, eyes closed, apparently meditating.

The mountain spirit told me they were **Peach Inspectors**.

They had been stationed there for **eight hundred years**.

Their sole responsibility was counting.

**Eight hundred years.**

Something inside me shifted.

The first peach I stole was actually a test.

I picked one of the 9,000-year peaches, ate it in three bites, buried the pit in the soil, and walked casually out of the garden.

The Peach Inspector did not even open his eyes.

The next day, I took two.

The third day, five.

By the seventh day, I simply climbed the tallest tree and sat among the branches, eating until I was full.

The inspectors still did nothing.

They remained perfectly still, as though I did not exist.

Then came the day the Seven Fairies arrived.

That was when I discovered the truth.

They entered the garden.

The mountain spirit hurried forward to greet them.

The inspectors finally opened their eyes.

The fairies went directly to the rear garden, where the 9,000-year peaches grew.

I hid behind a tree.

They lifted the leaves.

And then—

**they froze.**

“How can there be so few left?”

The leading fairy cried out.

The mountain spirit dropped to his knees.

“Fairies, please hear me! This year is not like the others. The Jade Emperor appointed the Great Sage Equal to Heaven to supervise the garden. We were instructed to report to him before opening it. But this monkey…”

He pointed toward me.

“**He has eaten all the old peaches!**”

I nearly fell out of the tree.

All the peaches I had eaten together amounted to perhaps a hundred.

Most of them were ordinary peaches.

I had eaten only three of the 9,000-year peaches.

How had I suddenly eaten **all of them**?

But the Seven Fairies had already believed him.

They hurriedly gathered the few remaining baskets and rushed to report to the Queen Mother.

I followed them.

Outside the Jade Pool, I heard everything.

The Jade Emperor said to Guanyin:

“He ate all the old peaches. And when the banquet was arranged, **he was not invited because he was an unpaid official**.”

**An unpaid official.**

Those four words struck me harder than any weapon.

Only then did I understand.

I had not been forgotten.

**I had never been intended to attend.**

My title of Great Sage Equal to Heaven was ceremonial.

My appointment as keeper of the Peach Garden was bait.

My theft of the peaches was the crime they needed.

**The entire trap had been prepared from the moment they gave me the title.**

# **Volume III · Five Hundred Years of Arithmetic**

**Some day, some month, some year. Weather: The more I calculate, the colder I feel.**

For the first hundred years beneath the mountain, all I had was **hatred**.

I hated the Jade Emperor's treachery.

I hated the Queen Mother's cruelty.

I hated Tathagata's interference.

I hated the Seven Fairies for their loose tongues.

I replayed every detail of that day again and again, searching for a weakness.

Perhaps one day I would escape and expose the truth.

By the second century, the hatred began to fade.

Something else took its place.

**Doubt.**

There were 3,600 trees in the Peach Garden.

Each tree produced perhaps a hundred peaches.

That meant the garden should contain **hundreds of thousands of peaches** across its cycles.

Even if I ate one hundred peaches a day, that would amount to only about 36,500 in a year.

And I had not even been in charge for a full year.

Even if I had eaten until I burst every single day, I could not possibly have consumed **tens of thousands** of peaches.

Yet when the Seven Fairies arrived, they said that only a few of the finest peaches remained.

The ordinary and middle-grade peaches were also nearly gone.

The missing amount must have been **tens of thousands**.

So I began asking myself:

**Where did all those peaches go?**

By the third century, I began doing arithmetic.

Suppose each year the Peach Banquet consumed two baskets of the finest peaches, three of the middle kind, and two of the lesser kind.

If each basket held roughly fifty peaches, that meant approximately **350 peaches per year**.

The peach trees had different maturation cycles, but the garden was designed to produce peaches continuously across those cycles.

And the mountain spirit had told me something else:

The Peach Inspectors had been there for **eight hundred years**.

For eight hundred years, every Peach Banquet had required peaches.

Every year, someone had to count them.

Every year, someone had to account for the harvest.

If the harvest came up short, the books would show a deficit.

After centuries of deficits, how could the inspectors continue to explain the numbers?

Unless—

**someone else was made responsible.**

By the fourth century, I thought I understood the entire chain.

The Peach Garden was nominally under the Queen Mother's authority.

In reality, it was the core of Heaven's **longevity economy**.

Every year, immortals attended the Peach Banquet and extended their lives.

The peaches were scarce.

The demand was endless.

Every immortal wanted a few more years.

That meant the Peach Inspectors, the mountain spirits, and perhaps even higher officials within the **Peach Administration** had enormous room to manipulate the accounts.

The finest peaches were precious.

They could be quietly given to powerful figures.

The middle-grade peaches could be used to cultivate relationships.

Even the lesser peaches, accumulated over centuries, represented considerable value.

Eight hundred years of this would create an enormous deficit.

And then I arrived.

A **wild monkey**.

No family.

No political network.

No faction.

And yet somehow carrying the dangerous title of Great Sage Equal to Heaven.

Putting me in charge of the Peach Garden was like putting a **scapegoat inside a pack of wolves**.

Every peach I ate could be magnified tenfold in the books.

Every peach I did not eat could be quietly placed under my name.

When the Seven Fairies arrived and discovered that only a few of the finest peaches remained, **those peaches had not necessarily been eaten by me.**

There may simply have been very few left to begin with.

Eight hundred years of missing inventory needed a spectacular scandal.

And my **Havoc in Heaven** provided the perfect cover.

Why was the Jade Emperor furious?

Of course he was furious.

But **not because I had eaten peaches**.

He was furious because I had disrupted the system.

If I had not caused trouble, the Peach Banquet would have gone ahead as usual.

The immortals would have drunk diluted peach wine and eaten whatever remained.

Nobody would have asked too many questions.

But once I caused chaos, the banquet collapsed.

The immortals lost their expectation of extended life.

And Heaven's **financial black hole** suddenly became visible.

So the Jade Emperor had to become furious.

He had to send the celestial army.

He had to portray me as an irredeemable demon monkey.

Only then could everyone's attention be shifted from:

**“Where did the peaches go?”**

to:

**“The monkey must be punished.”**

The old deficit could disappear into the new scandal.

# **Volume IV · The Details I Had Overlooked**

**Some day, some month, some year. Weather: The more I remember, the colder it gets.**

Five hundred years of silence have brought back details I ignored at the time.

### **Detail One: The mountain spirit's panic**

When the Seven Fairies questioned the missing peaches, the mountain spirit's first reaction was not:

“I failed in my duty.”

It was:

**“The Jade Emperor appointed the Great Sage Equal to Heaven to supervise the garden.”**

He shifted the blame toward me immediately.

Too quickly.

Like a line rehearsed a thousand times.

### **Detail Two: The inspectors' silence**

When I stole peaches, the four Peach Inspectors kept their eyes closed.

When the Seven Fairies arrived, they opened them and began counting.

Their closed eyes and open eyes were not negligence.

**They were coordination.**

They already knew the peaches were missing.

They had been waiting for someone to take the blame.

### **Detail Three: The Jade Emperor's precise anger**

The Jade Emperor did not simply call me a thief.

He called me an **“unpaid official.”**

That distinction matters.

He was not describing what I had done.

He was defining what I was.

He transformed the issue from **theft** into **insubordination**.

From an **accounting problem** into a **political problem**.

An accounting problem can be audited.

A political problem can be suppressed.

### **Detail Four: Tathagata's timely arrival**

I caused chaos throughout Heaven.

The Jade Emperor summoned Tathagata.

Tathagata arrived.

We made a bet.

He turned his palm over.

And the mountain came down.

Everything happened with extraordinary efficiency.

**Too much efficiency.**

Almost as if—

**they had discussed it beforehand.**

Perhaps my fate had been decided the moment I ate the first peach.

### **Detail Five: The mountain came down “gently”**

The original story says Tathagata **“gently”** pressed me beneath the mountain.

Gently?

I could not move.

For five hundred years.

What kind of gentleness was that?

Later I understood.

The word “gently” was not describing physical force.

It was describing **attitude**.

Tathagata did not consider me worth the effort.

I was merely a piece on the board.

**A piece that did not need to be crushed with force.**

# **Volume V · The Verdict**

**Some day, some month, some year. Weather: The monkey's mind is finally still. The verdict has already been written.**

Five hundred years have passed.

Under this mountain, I have changed from an **angry monkey** into a **thinking monkey**.

I have understood three things.

### **First: Heaven's books can never truly be balanced.**

The deficit in the Peach Garden may not have lasted eight hundred years.

It may have lasted a thousand.

Perhaps two thousand.

The inspectors, mountain spirits, and administrators came and went.

Each generation inherited another generation's missing peaches.

My “theft” simply allowed centuries of unclear accounts to be consolidated into one enormous **bad debt**.

I was the sheep brought to the slaughter.

But **the knife had been sharpened for eight hundred years.**

### **Second: The Jade Emperor's anger was always partly a performance.**

His fury was meant for the immortals to see.

He needed them to believe:

The peaches were gone because of the monkey.

Their longevity was threatened because of the monkey.

Heaven's troubles were caused by the monkey.

Their anger moved from:

**“Heaven is corrupt.”**

to:

**“That demon monkey is causing trouble.”**

And the Jade Emperor's throne remained secure.

### **Third: My rebellion was partly designed for me.**

From the moment I received the title of Great Sage Equal to Heaven, to the moment I was put in charge of the Peach Garden, to the moment I stole the peaches and caused havoc—

**every step was an invitation.**

The Jade Emperor knew my nature.

**Proud. Impulsive. Greedy. Angry.**

He did not have to force me.

He only had to give me a stage.

I would step onto it myself.

This was not merely a conspiracy.

It was something more elegant.

**A trap that did not need to hide itself.**

Five hundred years later, if someone asks me:

“Do you regret it?”

I will answer:

**Yes. And no.**

I regret being so foolish.

I regret being used without realizing it.

But I do not regret causing the chaos.

At least once, I disrupted Heaven's books.

At least once, I made the immortals wonder whether the peaches had really been eaten by one monkey.

As for the truth?

**The truth was buried beneath the Five Elements Mountain, together with me.**

# **Volume VI · Epilogue — The Day the Mountain Broke**

**Some day, some month, some year. Weather: I hear a monk is coming.**

The mountain spirit has been visiting more frequently lately.

His eyes keep darting away.

He seems to be carrying a secret.

“Great Sage,” he finally said, rubbing his hands, “I heard that… the five-hundred-year term is nearly over. A monk traveling west will pass this way. If he removes the Buddhist seal from the top of the mountain, you will be free.”

I looked at him.

Five hundred years had passed.

The old fellow had aged considerably.

His white beard now nearly reached the ground.

“Old man,” I said.

“Yes, Great Sage?”

“I want to ask you something.”

“Please.”

“That year in the Peach Garden…”

I paused.

“**Where did the finest peaches really go?**”

The mountain spirit's face became exactly what it had been five hundred years ago when the Seven Fairies discovered the missing peaches.

Pale.

Terrified.

As if someone had suddenly put a hand around his throat.

“Great Sage… Great Sage must be joking. Those peaches… they were not…”

“I know they weren't eaten by me,” I interrupted.

“What I want to know is:

**Where did they go?**”

The mountain spirit fell to his knees.

Exactly as quickly as he had five hundred years ago.

“Great Sage, spare me! I was forced! The Peach Inspectors… they… for eight hundred years…”

I laughed.

Five hundred years.

That was the sentence I had been waiting to hear.

“Get up,” I said.

“I do not blame you.”

He stared at me.

“You were a piece on the board.

So was I.

**There are no winners in a game like this.**”

The old spirit slowly stood up.

Tears ran down his face.

“Great Sage… you no longer hate us?”

“Hate?”

I looked toward the top of the mountain.

The Buddhist seal fluttered in the wind.

“I have forgotten how to hate.”

Five hundred years of iron pellets and molten copper had boiled the hatred away.

**What remained was understanding.**

“What have you understood?”

I remained silent for a long time.

The setting sun stretched the shadow of the mountain across the earth.

Finally, I said:

“I have understood that the greatest prison in Heaven and Earth is not the Five Elements Mountain.”

“It is **greed inside the human heart.**”

“The Jade Emperor coveted power.

The Queen Mother coveted longevity.

The immortals coveted life.

And I coveted fame.”

“Everyone wanted something.

So everyone calculated.

Everyone used everyone else.

And in the end, they put the entire bill on one monkey.”

The mountain spirit swallowed.

“And what will you do after you are free?”

I smiled.

Five hundred years without brushing my teeth had left my fangs sharp.

“Free?”

I paused.

“First, I will accompany that monk on his journey west.”

“And then?”

I smiled again.

“Then, when the time is right…”

I looked toward Heaven.

**“I will read this verdict aloud to the Three Realms.”**

“The verdict?”

“Yes.”

“The verdict against Heaven.

The verdict against the Jade Emperor.

The verdict against every immortal and Buddha who helped write it.”

“They may have been able to bury me for five hundred years.”

“But they cannot bury the truth forever.”

The mountain spirit stared at me.

It was as though he were seeing me for the first time.

“Great Sage…”

“You have changed.”

I shook my head.

“No.”

**“I am still the same monkey.”**

I looked toward the sky.

“Only…”

**“I can finally see clearly.”**

The mountain wind roared.

The Buddhist seal fluttered violently above me.

I closed my eyes.

And waited for the monk.

I waited for the day when the mountain would crack,

when the stone would split,

and when the heavens would finally hear

**the sound of a monkey breaking free.**
