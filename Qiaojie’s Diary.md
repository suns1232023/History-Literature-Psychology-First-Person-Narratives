# Qiaojie’s Diary

### *How I Survived: A Survivor’s Answer to Lin Daiyu, Jiao Da, and Zhen Yinglian*

**Keywords:** [#Culture & Media#](https://xueqiu.com/k?q=%23%E6%96%87%E5%8C%96%E4%BC%A0%E5%AA%92%23)

Ban’er came home from the market with three old notebooks. He said someone had found them among the ruins of an abandoned old estate.

I opened the first one and recognized Aunt Lin’s handwriting at once.

The second was written in crooked, uneven strokes, as though someone had tried to record Jiao Da’s words exactly as he spoke them.

The third was thin, its pages yellow with age. It belonged to Aunt Zhen.

I read all three through the night.

At dawn, Ban’er turned over in his sleep and mumbled, “You’re still not asleep?”

I blew out the lamp and lay down.

But I could not close my eyes.

Three figures kept moving through my mind.

Aunt Lin, coughing blood among the shadows of bamboo at Xiaoxiang Hall.

Jiao Da, pinned to the ground and humiliated.

Aunt Zhen, standing beneath a locust tree, looking up at its blossoms.

All three seemed to be asking me the same question:

**“How did you survive?”**

I had no answer.

I only tightened my grip on the corner of the quilt.

I have thought about dying.

When my uncle sold me to that man, I crawled into the deepest corner of the carriage. All I could think was:

*If only this carriage would overturn.*

It wasn't courage.

I simply could not bear any more.

Grandmother told me later that the first time she saw me, she knew immediately that **“there was no light left in this child’s eyes.”**

She said it while shelling beans, without even looking up, as if she were speaking of something ordinary.

But those words pierced me.

**“No light in the eyes”—those are the eyes of someone who has already wished to die.**

But I did not die.

Grandmother lifted me from the carriage and wrapped me in an old, torn quilt.

She smelled of earth and firewood.

It wasn't a pleasant smell.

But it was warm.

I remembered that smell for more than twenty years.

As the years passed, I gradually stopped thinking about death.

Not because life became better.

Life was never particularly kind.

It was because I came to understand something:

**When Heaven lets you survive, it is not necessarily so that you may enjoy a better life. Sometimes it is so that you can live the blessings that others never had the chance to receive.**

Aunt Lin wrote in her poem:

**“When the red fades and the fragrance dies, who will grieve?”**

She was writing about herself.

But she was also writing about all those women who died and were forgotten the moment they were gone.

Then I thought of Jiao Da.

He had once saved the life of the old master.

Who remembered him?

His service was swallowed up.

His life was ground beneath other people's feet.

When he cursed the Ningguo household, he was not mad.

He was desperate.

I know that kind of desperation.

But I do not want to become like him.

Not because I am afraid of humiliation.

But because **I do not want to spend the last bit of strength I have on hatred.**

Hatred is exhausting.

More exhausting than spinning thread.

More exhausting than working the fields.

When you hate someone, you must keep thinking about them.

Your heart remains tied to that person.

Meanwhile, they go on living perfectly well.

I do not want that.

Then I thought of Aunt Zhen.

She remembered almost nothing.

She could no longer remember her parents.

She could no longer remember her home.

Even her name had been changed three times.

But she remembered a locust tree.

She remembered the ground covered in white blossoms when the locust flowers were in bloom.

That single memory carried her through an entire lifetime.

Sometimes I think Aunt Zhen and I are opposites.

She could not remember.

I could not forget.

Yet her way of surviving saved her, and my way of surviving saved me.

**Everyone has their own way of enduring. None is necessarily wiser than another. If it keeps you alive, then it is a good way.**

Later, I read a book by an old scholar who said that survival is not really an accomplishment.

It is fate.

Those with stronger fates survive.

Those with weaker ones disappear.

I do not believe that.

Was Grandmother's fate stronger than mine?

Her husband died young.

Her daughter died young.

She raised Ban’er by herself.

How much suffering did she endure?

Yet she did not die.

**It wasn't that her fate was stronger. She chose to live.**

Every morning, when she opened her eyes, she chose to live one more day.

So did I.

**Every morning, when I opened my eyes, I chose to live one more day.**

It wasn't courage.

I simply thought:

*Dying is easy. Living is the difficult part.*

If Heaven wanted to make life difficult for me, then I would live long enough to show it just how difficult I could endure.

I closed the window and turned the wick of the oil lamp a little higher.

Some things had been pressing against my heart for more than twenty years.

It was time to write them down.

---

When I was a child in the household, I had seen Aunt Lin.

She rarely spoke.

When she sat there, she seemed like a bamboo stalk—still and upright, unmoved by the wind.

People said she was aloof.

They said she was difficult.

They said she was too sensitive.

Only much later did I understand:

That was not aloofness.

It was **a woman swallowing all her tears until they became poetry.**

In Xiaoxiang Hall, with one lamp, one curtain of bamboo, and one bowl of medicine, she endured countless nights.

No one knew how many.

Her poems were not merely words.

They were herself.

Now, when I sit beside my spinning wheel, the spindle turns and turns, never stopping, just like the days.

And I think:

Aunt Lin wrote as I spin.

Both of us were alone.

Both of us drew the thread inside our hearts out, strand by strand.

Only her thread was red.

Mine is white.

She died that night, burning her manuscripts and coughing blood.

She used the last of her strength to burn her own words.

She said:

**“Born pure, I shall return pure.”**

And she left this world cleanly.

I cannot do that.

I am not ready to let go.

---

I heard about Jiao Da when I was still a child.

Whenever his name was mentioned in the household, people frowned.

They said he was an old man who demanded respect because of his age.

They called him a madman.

But after reading his words, I realized that he may have been the clearest-eyed person in the entire household.

He turned himself into a knife.

The blade pointed outward.

And inward.

He cut others.

And he cut himself.

I learned something from him:

**Some truths, once you see them, cannot be spoken. And if you speak them, you must be prepared to pay the price.**

But I also envied him.

He did not care about the price.

---

And then there were Aunt Zhen's pages.

Calling them a diary is perhaps too generous.

They were really only a few scattered sheets of paper.

She wrote:

**“I can no longer remember my parents’ faces. But I remember that there was a great locust tree outside our home when I was little. When it bloomed, the ground was covered in white.”**

When I reached that passage, I began to cry.

A person could lose almost everything and still remember a single tree.

That tree was her only thread connecting her to the past.

She held onto it desperately, as though by remembering it she could remain that four-year-old girl.

Her name was changed, too.

Xiangling.

Qiuling.

Every time her name changed, it was as though another layer of skin had been peeled away.

Yet she smiled.

She smiled while learning poetry.

She smiled while being bullied.

It wasn't that she had learned to let go.

She simply did not dare to remember.

Some pain is so great that it splits a person in two.

I once read a book that described this as locking the most painful memories inside a drawer that no one can open, then throwing away the key.

I once wanted to do the same.

When I was sold.

When I was locked in a dark room.

When I was dragged into the carriage.

Those images kept returning.

They would wake me in the middle of the night.

I wanted to forget.

But I couldn't.

The harder I tried to forget, the more clearly I remembered.

Then Grandmother told me:

**“If you can't forget, then don't. Remember it the way you remember a scar. As long as the scar remains, you know which road never to take again.”**

So I did.

Those things are still inside me.

But I am no longer afraid of them.

---

Ban’er asked me whether reading the three notebooks made me feel better.

I told him no.

But somehow, yes.

It hurts because I feel for them.

Aunt Lin died so young.

Jiao Da was humiliated.

Aunt Zhen spent her entire life being carried from one place to another.

They suffered far more than I did.

I was sold.

But Grandmother rescued me.

And I had Ban’er beside me.

They had almost nothing.

Yet they endured to the end.

Each in their own way.

It feels better because now I know:

**I am not alone.**

There are countless people in this world who suffer.

Everyone has their own way of enduring.

Aunt Lin endured until she became poetry.

Jiao Da endured until he became a curse.

Aunt Zhen endured until she became a field of white locust blossoms.

And me?

I endured until I became a strand of thread on a spinning wheel.

Whether it is thick or thin does not matter.

If it can be woven into cloth,

if it can mend a garment,

if it can keep someone warm,

then that is enough.

Grandmother says I should stop thinking about the past.

The more I think about it, she says, the harder it will be to escape.

But she does not understand.

I am not trapped in the past.

I have simply understood something:

**The way out is not to throw the past away. It is to carry it on your back and keep walking forward, one step at a time.**

Carry it long enough,

and eventually it no longer feels so heavy.

---

Last night, I dreamed of Aunt Lin.

She was wearing a pale moon-colored jacket, sitting beside the window in Xiaoxiang Hall.

She held a brush in her hand.

There was not a single word on the paper.

I walked over and asked,

“Aunt Lin, what are you writing?”

She looked at me.

“I'm waiting for you.”

“What are you waiting for?”

“For you to tell me how you survived.”

I woke up.

My pillow was wet again.

I wondered:

If Aunt Lin were still alive, what would I tell her?

I would not give her some grand speech.

I would simply tell her about ordinary days.

What time I wake up in the morning.

How to cook the porridge without letting it burn.

What Ban’er sold at the market that day.

Whether Grandmother's cough had improved.

When the child first learned to walk.

These things are trivial.

But you do them one by one.

Then night comes.

And another day is gone.

**Life is not something you think your way through. It is something you live through.**

Aunt Lin wrote:

**“Three hundred and sixty days a year—wind like knives, frost like swords, pressing hard from every side.”**

Whose head is free from wind and frost?

She simply stood in a higher place.

So the wind hurt her more.

I crouch in the fields.

The wind is weaker here.

But it is still cold.

Cold or not, I still have to live.

---

Dawn is almost here.

I closed the three notebooks, wrapped them in a piece of blue cloth, and placed them at the very bottom of the cabinet.

When my child grows old enough to read, I will take them out.

I will let him see them.

Let him know what his mother saw in this lifetime.

Let him know what she thought about.

A rooster crowed outside.

Ban’er mumbled,

“Still awake?”

I said,

“I'm not going back to sleep. It's time to get up.”

I put on my clothes and went to the kitchen to start the fire.

The firelight warmed my face.

I thought once more of the three people whose words I had read:

Aunt Lin.

Jiao Da.

Aunt Zhen.

They are all gone.

But I am living for them.

I spin thread for them.

I cook for them.

I carry on with ordinary life for them.

Their tears, their curses, their laughter—

they all remain here, inside me.

I cannot write poetry.

I cannot curse like Jiao Da.

I cannot pretend not to understand.

I only know how to spin thread.

So I spin it, one strand at a time, strong and even.

One day, these strands will become cloth.

My child will wear it.

And perhaps my child's children will wear it too.

By then, who will remember Qiaojie?

Perhaps no one.

And that is all right.

**As long as they are warm, that is enough.**

— **Qiaojie, written beside the spinning wheel in a remote village**
