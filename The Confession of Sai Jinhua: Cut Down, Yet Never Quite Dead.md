# **The Confession of Sai Jinhua: Cut Down, Yet Never Quite Dead**

### *A Psychological Monologue*

**Time:** Late autumn, 1936
**Place:** Renli, Tianqiao, Beijing
**Speaker:** Sai Jinhua (1872–1936), also known in different periods of her life as Zheng Caiyun, Fu Caiyun, Hong Mengluan, Cao Menglan, and Wei Zhaolingfei

**Keywords:** Sai Jinhua; trauma; identity; resilience; memory reconstruction; social stigma; survival threshold

---

## Prologue: Why I Am Writing This Today

It is the ninth day of the tenth lunar month in the twenty-fifth year of the Republic.

The wind in Beijing has turned hard.

This morning, Gu Ma went to the alley and begged for two bowls of douzhi. When she came back, her face was blue with cold.

“Second Mistress,” she said, “today is your sixty-fourth birthday. You should at least eat something warm.”

I held the bowl in my hands and looked at my own reflection in it.

A shrunken face. Yellow skin. Wrinkles everywhere.

Who would believe that this face was once called that of the “most beautiful woman in the East”?

I am not writing today to make myself into a legend.

Too many people have interviewed me over the years. Mr. Liu Bannong came. Mr. Shang Hongkui came. They wrote down what I told them and turned it into books.

Some people called me the “Nine Heavens Protectress of China.”

Others called me nothing more than an old prostitute who had invented her own legend.

The truth is probably somewhere in between.

I was neither as noble as they say nor as wicked.

I was simply a woman who lived through an extraordinary number of things.

From Yixian to Suzhou.

From Suzhou to Beijing.

From Beijing to Europe.

And from Europe back to a broken alley in Beijing.

Recently I have heard people use a strange expression: **“the killing line.”**

I do not understand its theories.

But when I look back on my life, I feel that I have spent almost seventy years dancing along an invisible line.

At times I was lifted above it.

At times I was cut down beneath it.

Today, I want to understand why.

---

# I. Yixian: The Beginning I Could Never Return To

My family name was Zhao. My childhood name was Caiyun.

I was born in 1872 in Yixian County, Anhui.

My father, Zhao Bage, ran a pawnshop in Suzhou. My mother died when I was young.

When I was eight, I was sent to Suzhou to live with my father.

At first, our family was reasonably well off. My father hired a tutor to teach me reading and writing.

I learned quickly.

The tutor once said:

> “If this girl had been born a boy, she might one day have passed the imperial examinations.”

Unfortunately, I was not a boy.

My mother died when I was still young.

Then my father took to opium.

The pawnshop declined.

Eventually, he had to sell it.

When I was fourteen, my father died.

I became an orphan.

A distant relative took me in.

And then—

sold me to a flower boat.

Looking back now, I think that was the first killing line of my life.

Modern psychology has a name for what happened: childhood trauma.

A girl loses her mother, then her father, and then, before she has even learned how to live on her own, she is sold into prostitution.

There was no time to recover from one loss before another arrived.

It was as though the foundation of a house had been torn away before the house had even been built.

Perhaps that is why I later seemed able to survive so much.

Once a person has already been destroyed at fourteen, perhaps she becomes less frightened of being destroyed again.

But that is not a blessing.

It is a wound.

I do not want to write much about the flower boat.

People called us *qingguanren*, “cultured courtesans,” and *hongguanren*, courtesans who entertained paying customers.

The names sounded elegant.

But underneath them, we were still commodities purchased by men.

I learned to sing.

I learned to play music.

I learned how to entertain.

And I learned how to hide a knife behind a smile.

People in Suzhou began calling me Fu Caiyun.

*Caiyun* means “colored cloud.”

Clouds disperse easily.

Perhaps the name itself was a prophecy.

---

# II. Hong Jun: Three Years in the Clouds

In 1887, when I was fifteen, I met Hong Jun.

He was a former imperial *zhuangyuan*, the highest-ranking candidate in the imperial examinations.

He was forty-eight years old.

He was old enough to have been my father.

He came to the flower boat, saw me, and kept returning.

For seven days.

Eventually he paid a thousand taels of silver to secure my freedom and took me as his third concubine.

I was given another name: Hong Mengluan.

From a flower boat to the household of a *zhuangyuan*—

it was such an enormous leap that I scarcely believed it myself.

The principal wife disliked me.

The other concubines resented me.

But I was young.

I was beautiful.

I knew how to speak.

And Hong Jun favored me.

In that household, as long as the master favored you, you survived.

But what truly transformed me was the journey abroad.

In 1888, Hong Jun was appointed envoy to Germany, Russia, Austria, and the Netherlands.

The principal wife refused to accompany him.

I volunteered.

Hong Jun hesitated for a long time.

Eventually he agreed.

He even allowed me to wear the principal wife's official ceremonial clothing so that I could accompany him in the capacity of an ambassador's wife.

That journey changed everything.

I saw Berlin.

St. Petersburg.

Paris.

London.

I met Kaiser Wilhelm II.

I met Empress Augusta Victoria.

The Empress once held my hand and told me that I was beautiful.

I learned German.

I learned Western table manners.

I learned how to speak with foreigners.

In Berlin, I encountered a German officer named Alfred von Waldersee.

At that time, he was not yet the commander of the expeditionary forces.

We had merely met socially.

A nod here.

A greeting there.

Nothing more.

But those years in Europe changed the way I saw the world.

For the first time, I saw how modern states functioned.

I saw institutions.

Law.

Diplomacy.

Military organization.

And when I looked back toward China, I suddenly understood what it meant for a country to be weak.

The realization did not make me proud.

It made me helpless.

I knew that my country was sick.

But I was a woman.

What could I do?

In 1893, Hong Jun died in Beijing.

I was twenty-one.

On the journey south with his coffin, the Hong family began treating me differently.

The principal wife told me:

> “You are only a concubine. Now that the master is dead, you should remain a widow.”

But I did not want to become a widow at twenty-one.

I had seen Europe.

I had learned German.

I had lived as the wife of a diplomat.

And now they wanted me to spend the rest of my life mourning behind a gate?

I refused.

I left.

---

# III. Shanghai and Tianjin: Dancing on the Killing Line

After leaving the Hong household, I went to Shanghai.

I took two young women with me and rented a house in the International Settlement.

I returned to prostitution.

I hung Hong Jun's portrait in the drawing room and told my customers:

> “I was the wife of a *zhuangyuan*. I was the wife of a diplomat.”

Shanghai was fascinated.

Merchants, officials, foreign residents, and people from every level of society wanted to see the woman who had been both a famous courtesan and a diplomat's companion.

Some people said I had no shame.

But what should I have done?

I was twenty-one.

I had no family home to return to.

No substantial dowry.

No profession that society would accept.

I had only my appearance, my body, my ability to entertain people, and what I had learned in Europe.

What alternative did society offer me?

Factory work?

A few coins a day?

Starvation?

Or the river?

Society had left women like me almost no legitimate road to survival.

So I made my own road.

In 1898, I went to Tianjin.

There I began using the name **Sai Jinhua**.

I formed the Jinhua troupe.

Tianjin was more prosperous than Shanghai and much closer to Beijing.

There were officials, businessmen, foreigners, and wealthy men everywhere.

I continued to display Hong Jun's portrait.

I continued to identify myself as the wife of a former *zhuangyuan*.

Business was good.

From the perspective of what I now call the **killing-line model**, however, those years were extremely precarious.

I appeared prosperous.

But I possessed almost no institutional security.

No stable pension.

No reliable property.

No family safety net.

No social insurance.

My security depended on three fragile things:

my appearance,

my reputation,

and whether people still wanted me.

How long could any of those last?

The first principle of the killing-line model is therefore simple:

> **When a person's survival depends almost entirely on conditions that cannot be sustained, she may already be standing on the killing line—even while she appears to be thriving.**

In 1900, I finally fell.

---

# IV. 1900: Pulled Back from the Edge

In the summer of 1900, the Allied forces entered Beijing.

The Empress Dowager fled.

The emperor fled.

Officials fled.

I did not.

Not because I was brave.

Because I had nowhere to go.

I was living in Shitou Hutong, in the area controlled by German troops.

One night, German soldiers broke into my residence.

They were armed.

They had been drinking.

They tried to take me away.

I shouted in German:

> “I am the wife of a Chinese diplomat! I have met your emperor and empress!”

They hesitated.

I brought out photographs from Berlin.

Photographs with the imperial family.

Photographs from my years in Europe.

They reported the matter to their superiors.

Eventually I was taken to the headquarters of the Allied commander.

It was Waldersee.

I recognized him immediately.

Fifteen years earlier, I had seen him in Berlin.

He recognized me too.

He asked:

> “What are you doing here?”

I answered:

> “This is my home. Your soldiers are destroying my home.”

What happened afterward became legend.

People later said I saved Beijing.

Others said I slept with Waldersee in exchange for peace.

Still others claimed that I had invented the entire story.

I want to say only this:

I did speak with him.

I did use German to plead for civilians and urge restraint.

I did become an intermediary in some matters.

But whether I “saved Beijing” is another question.

I was not a national savior.

I was simply a woman who happened to know the language, happened to know the commander, and happened to survive when many others did not.

Perhaps another Chinese woman with the same language skills and connections could have done something similar.

The rest may have been added by history.

From the perspective of trauma psychology, the events of 1900 can be understood as an extreme stress test.

All the abilities I had accumulated over the years suddenly became survival resources:

my German,

my social experience,

my knowledge of European customs,

my ability to remain calm under pressure,

my understanding of how powerful men behaved.

What had once looked like the skills of a courtesan became, in that moment, survival tools.

The second principle of the killing-line model is therefore:

> **Under extreme conditions, unconventional resources may temporarily substitute for institutional protection and pull a person back from the edge. But such rescue remains fragile because the underlying structure has not changed.**

After 1900, I soon fell again.

---

# V. Three Marriages, Three Widows' Lives

In 1903, I was arrested in connection with the death of a servant named Fenglin.

She had taken poison.

I had beaten her with a whip, but I had not intended to kill her.

In that society, however, a woman like me was easy to condemn.

I was sentenced to be sent back to my ancestral home in Yixian.

Yixian.

The place where I had been born.

Now I was being driven back there as if I were a disgrace.

My relatives looked at me as though I were a monster.

I could not stay.

So I ran.

What followed was a cycle of marriage and loss.

In 1911, I became the concubine of Cao Ruizhong.

He died the following year.

In 1918, I married Wei Shijiong.

He was the second man I truly loved.

He did not care about my past.

He gave me the name **Wei Zhaolingfei**.

He said it meant that my soul had finally learned to fly.

We had a Western-style wedding.

I wore a white wedding dress.

I believed that I could finally cut myself away from the past.

In 1921, Wei Shijiong died.

Three marriages.

Three widows' lives.

Each time I went from having protection to having none.

From having a home to having no home.

Each time I returned to the world of entertainment and prostitution.

Each time I had to climb back upward from the edge.

But every climb became harder.

Because something inside me had been consumed.

The third principle of the killing-line model is:

> **Repeated exposure to loss, rejection, and insecurity can gradually consume psychological and social resources. The fact that a person survives one crisis does not mean she will have the same capacity when the next crisis arrives.**

After Wei died, his family drove me away.

I eventually returned to Beijing.

Gu Ma came with me.

We settled in Renli, near Tianqiao.

It was one of the poorest parts of the city.

The lanes were narrow.

The houses were broken.

Even rickshaw pullers did not like living there.

---

# VI. Old Age: When the Killing Line Finally Arrived

From 1921 to 1936, I lived in Renli.

How did I survive?

By relying on other people.

Zhang Xueliang helped me.

Xu Beihong helped me.

Qi Baishi helped me.

Li Kuchan helped me.

But such help could only rescue me temporarily.

A gift could pay today's rent.

It could not guarantee tomorrow's.

In 1933, I was taken to court over unpaid rent.

Once the newspapers reported the case, Beijing remembered me again.

Journalists came.

Scholars came.

They wanted to hear my stories of 1900.

I told them many things.

Some were true.

Some had become distorted in my memory.

And some—

I admit—

I invented.

Why?

Because the real story of my old age was unbearable.

A sixty-year-old woman living in poverty, unable to pay her rent, dependent on charity—

what kind of story was that?

If I did not create a version of myself as a woman who had once stood beside history, what would I have left to hold on to?

Modern psychology might describe this as **memory reconstruction** or **self-enhancement**.

But I do not think it is always deliberate deception.

Sometimes a person reconstructs her past because she needs a past she can continue to live with.

In that sense, memory is not merely a record.

It can also become a shelter.

By 1936, winter had become unusually cruel.

On December 4, I became seriously ill.

There was little heat.

Little money.

Little strength.

I remember lying under a thin quilt.

My body grew colder.

I wanted to call Gu Ma.

But no sound came.

After that, I remember nothing.

---

# VII. The Killing Line: What My Life Taught Me

I never studied psychology.

But after sixty-four years, I have learned one thing from my own life:

Every person has a threshold.

Once enough losses accumulate and the threshold is crossed, talent alone may no longer be enough.

I call that threshold the **killing line**.

Call it something else if you prefer.

The name does not matter.

What matters is what lies beneath it.

### First: The killing line is real.

Being sold at fourteen was one line.

Becoming a widow at twenty-one was another.

Being expelled from one household after another was another.

Every time I reached the edge of survival, people called me lucky.

Perhaps I was.

But luck is not the same as strength.

I did not survive because I was invincible.

I survived because, each time, I happened to have one more resource left.

### Second: A safety net matters more than individual effort.

I have met women more intelligent than me.

More capable than me.

Why did some of them fail to survive?

Because they did not possess the unusual resources I happened to acquire:

language,

social connections,

knowledge of foreign society,

access to powerful people.

But those resources were never reliable.

The truly reliable protection would have been institutional:

pensions,

healthcare,

social assistance,

legal protection,

and a stable home.

There was little of that available to women like me.

So women like me were forced to live on the edge.

### Third: Do not worship resilience.

People later called me strong.

They said I was indomitable.

But I want to tell them something:

I was not indomitable.

I simply had no alternative.

If someone had once told me,

> “Stay here. You are safe. I will take care of you,”

perhaps I would have stayed.

Resilience is often praised because people do not see the absence of alternatives that produced it.

And resilience has limits.

A person can survive many storms.

But survival itself consumes resources.

### Fourth: Even beneath the killing line, something may remain.

This is the most contradictory lesson of my life.

I was destroyed and rebuilt many times.

I became Zheng Caiyun.

Then Fu Caiyun.

Then Hong Mengluan.

Then Sai Jinhua.

Then Cao Menglan.

Then Wei Zhaolingfei.

Society gave me countless labels:

courtesan,

concubine,

diplomat's wife,

criminal,

widow,

pauper.

But beneath every label there remained someone who could still breathe.

Society could rename me.

It could humiliate me.

It could glorify me.

It could condemn me.

But as long as I was still breathing, I had not completely disappeared.

I do not mean that the soul is immortal.

I mean something much simpler:

> **As long as a person is still alive, the possibility of another beginning has not entirely vanished.**

---

# VIII. To Those Who Stand on the Killing Line

Today is my sixty-fourth birthday.

Gu Ma cooked me a bowl of noodles.

There was no egg.

The broth was almost clear.

And yet I thought it was the best bowl of noodles I had ever eaten.

Not because the noodles were good.

Because I was still alive.

And because someone had cooked them for me.

I have been a courtesan.

A concubine.

A diplomat's wife.

A widow.

A defendant.

A famous woman.

A poor woman.

I have slept on a flower boat.

In the carved bed of a wealthy household.

In Europe.

And on a broken kang bed in Renli.

People have lifted me into the clouds.

People have pushed me into the mud.

If there is one thing I would leave to those who come after me, it is this:

> **Do not despise people who are standing on the killing line.**

You never know how much it has cost them simply to remain standing.

I am leaving now.

I do not know whether anyone will ever read these words.

If someone does, remember this:

I was neither a “Nine Heavens Protectress” nor a “dangerous beauty.”

I was simply a woman who spent her life struggling along the edge of survival.

I struggled.

I failed.

I climbed back up.

I fell again.

And eventually, I died in a poor alley.

But at least—

I struggled.

**—Zhao Lingfei (Sai Jinhua)**

**Beijing, 1936**

---

# Author's Note

This text is a work of **historically informed literary reconstruction**, not an authentic diary or verbatim autobiography by Sai Jinhua.

The narrative draws primarily on surviving accounts of Sai Jinhua's interviews with Liu Bannong, Shang Hongkui, contemporary newspaper reports, and later historical studies. Because Sai Jinhua's own recollections were recorded decades after many of the events they describe, they should be treated as historical testimony that requires comparison with independent sources.

In particular, accounts concerning Sai Jinhua's relationship with Alfred von Waldersee during the Boxer Uprising, her alleged role in restraining violence, and her involvement in negotiations surrounding the Clemens von Ketteler memorial remain subjects of historical debate. In this narrative, such episodes are deliberately presented as **Sai Jinhua's remembered account and the legends subsequently constructed around her**, rather than as uncontested historical facts.

The psychological interpretation is likewise contemporary. Concepts such as **trauma, cumulative stress, identity reconstruction, social stigma, resilience, and the “killing line”** belong to the analytical framework of the modern author; they should not be attributed to Sai Jinhua herself.

The term **“killing line”** is used here as a heuristic metaphor rather than as an established clinical theory. It refers to the point at which accumulated economic insecurity, social exclusion, psychological burden, and repeated loss exceed the resources available to an individual.

The central question of this narrative is therefore not whether Sai Jinhua was truly a heroine or a fraud.

It is a more difficult question:

> **When a person's survival depends for decades on beauty, personal courage, social connections, unusual skills, and sheer luck, should we call her “resilient”—or recognize how dangerously close she has always been to the edge?**

Perhaps Sai Jinhua's life is valuable precisely because it does not provide a simple answer.

She was neither simply a heroine nor simply a victim.

She was a woman repeatedly forced to reconstruct herself in a society that offered her very few stable places in which to exist.

And perhaps that is the deepest meaning of the killing line:

**sometimes survival is not proof that the system works.**

**Sometimes survival is proof of how hard the individual has had to fight against it.**
