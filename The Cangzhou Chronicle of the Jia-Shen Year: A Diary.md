# The Cangzhou Chronicle of the Jia-Shen Year: A Diary

### The Twenty-Fourth Day of the Third Month, Seventeenth Year of Chongzhen — Cangzhou, Night

I arrived in Cangzhou today.

I had left the capital on the sixth day of the third month, after taking my leave of the Emperor. I was traveling south **on imperial orders, bound for Nanjing to settle government affairs**.

It had been more than half a month since I left Tongzhou.

I had expected to stop in Cangzhou for a night's rest.

Instead, what awaited me was a **dead city**.

An eunuch named Gao had been stationed here with troops. The gates were under strict lockdown, and something already felt terribly wrong.

At the fifth watch the following morning, the news exploded:

**The Governor had fled during the night. The soldiers had scattered like startled birds and beasts.**

I changed into plain clothes and entered the city.

The streets were empty.

No markets.

No pedestrians.

The nameplates had been removed from the residences of the local gentry. Shopkeepers had sealed their doors with mud.

When I reached the prefectural office, **the prefect, Luo Huang, was kneeling on the ground, clutching my legs and weeping uncontrollably.**

> **“It is all over,”** he said.

**On the nineteenth, the outer city of the capital fell. The inner city could not be held either. The eunuchs slipped away in the middle of the night.**

This morning, several local scholars came to seize the prefectural seal, intending to present it to the new dynasty.

Luo said **he was simply waiting to die**.

He and I **pressed our hands to our chests and wept bitterly, then took our leave of one another as men who might never meet again.**

There was an old fellow townsman of mine in Cangzhou, Jia Yongdi.

I went looking for him.

He was already gone.

His four sons were still there, all grown men, all respectable, all reduced to helpless lamentations over the state of the world.

I had intended to remain in Cangzhou for a while.

Then I remembered:

**I had been sent south by imperial command. To linger here would be improper.**

So I left that very night.

But the boatmen heading south told me that **everything north of Dezhou was already territory controlled by the rebels**.

Boats were being robbed.

Officials were being captured.

The boatmen refused to go any farther.

One family had more than twenty people in all, including seven children under the age of ten. They were crying together in a terrible confusion. Someone even threatened to throw himself into the water.

I lowered my voice.

**“Crying will do nothing. I have heard that the rebels kill officials but spare merchants. If I stay with you, you will all die.”**

I selected four servants.

We divided the women and children among several boats and disguised ourselves as merchants.

The boats traveled separately, one ahead of another.

We hung torn pieces of cloth from the masts as signals so that we could recognize one another.

**We smashed all the hairpins, jewelry, and ornaments. We tore our brightly colored clothes to pieces. My official robes and ceremonial tablet were handed over to the boatmen.**

We lit no fire that day.

At dusk, I drank a few mouthfuls of thin porridge.

At the fourth watch, I quietly rose and called for my groom, Cheng Gui.

I told him we were going to scout the rebels' movements.

**I carried only two taels of silver and five hundred copper coins.**

**Once I left, I never turned back.**

From then on, we walked thirty or forty *li* a day.

When we reached Dezhou, I learned the truth:

**The new dynasty had already issued orders to hunt down fleeing officials of the Ming everywhere.**

I dared not stay at inns.

I could only sleep outdoors.

My feet became so injured that I walked with a limp.

Even in the villages, no one would give me anything to eat.

One day, I came upon a roadside tea stall.

I was completely exhausted.

A monk gave me two bowls of porridge.

That night, I slept beneath the stall.

The next morning, after walking about a mile, I encountered an old villager carrying a sack of rice.

He was frail with age and illness, barely able to bear the weight.

I told Cheng Gui to carry it for him.

We walked another twenty *li*.

The old man gave me two flatbreads.

**I ate them, and only then did my strength return.**

Late that day, we reached Sidusi.

I sat beneath the shade of a tree.

Suddenly, I heard a Huguang accent coming from a boat.

**It was an old friend, Wang Shiyu, who had just been appointed prefect of Chaozhou.**

He was astonished to see me.

He asked what I intended to do.

I told him:

**“The troops are searching for Ming officials. Take the land route. Travel day and night without stopping.”**

He wanted to give me ten taels of silver.

I accepted only a single string of copper coins.

Then we parted.

I slept beneath the tree again.

Another day brought me to Zhengjiakou.

**Several thousand households lived behind an earthen wall. The only entrance was a hole barely three feet high. I crawled through it.**

An old man wearing a square scholar's cap and plain cloth robes saw my miserable condition.

He leaned on his staff and asked:

> “Are you a beggar?”

I said:

> “I suppose I am.”

He looked at me and said:

> **“You must be a scholar. Only your luck has failed you.”**

He led me to a schoolhouse and invited me to sit.

He was, as it turned out, a teacher.

On the desk lay a printed essay.

Its title was **“The Orphaned Loyalist and the Wayward Son.”**

And it was **a piece I had written in my youth.**

I was overwhelmed by the coincidence.

The old man asked:

> “Do you understand writing?”

I pointed to the paper on the desk.

**“I wrote that when I was young.”**

The old man sprang to his feet.

> “Then you are Master Cheng?”

I said no.

I told him that Cheng was a fellow townsman of mine who had simply taken my essay and passed it off under his own name.

The old man refused to believe me.

He sent a boy to summon the Su brothers, two local men of standing.

Before long, the brothers arrived.

They invited me to their estate and treated me with the utmost courtesy.

**At that point, I stopped hiding my name.**

That night, for the first time in many days,

**I ate meat.**

The Su brothers were men of courage and reputation.

They told me that bandits roamed the region and made the nights dangerous.

Their estate was one of the few places capable of defending itself against them.

I sat beneath the lamp and watched the candle flame tremble.

**The capital had fallen.**

**The Emperor—**

I did not know whether the Emperor was still alive.

When I left the capital, I had taken my leave of him in person.

His voice was still in my ears.

And yet, in little more than half a month,

**the sky had collapsed.**

I had fled Cangzhou carrying two taels of silver and five hundred copper coins.

**I ran.
I hid.
I ate flatbread.
I drank thin porridge.
I slept beneath trees.**

What about tomorrow?

What about the day after?

I do not know.

**I was ordered south to Nanjing to carry out an official assignment.**

But is Nanjing still there?

Is the court still there?

Tonight, at the Su estate, there is meat to eat.

There is a fire to warm myself by.

But I know that **this safety has been stolen from the chaos. It cannot last more than a few days.**

At dawn, I will have to leave again.

**I am an official of the Ming.**

**The Emperor ordered me south.**

**So south I will go.**

**I will walk until I reach wherever I am meant to reach.**

**Cheng Zhengkui,
recorded beneath the lamplight at the Su estate in Zhengjiakou.**

**The third month is nearly over.**

**Dawn is coming.**
