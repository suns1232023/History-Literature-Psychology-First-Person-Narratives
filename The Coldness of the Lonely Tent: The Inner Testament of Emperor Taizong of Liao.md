# The Coldness of the Lonely Tent: The Inner Testament of Emperor Taizong of Liao

### *A Soul’s Monologue Inside the Prison of Power*

**Keywords:** #Culture & Media#

---

## Prologue: The Empty Shell on the Dragon Throne

I am Yelü Deguang, Emperor Taizong of the Liao dynasty.

The histories describe me as a man of “martial strength and strategic talent, skilled in horsemanship and archery.” I destroyed the Later Jin, entered Bianliang, established the Northern and Southern Administrative systems, and fulfilled my father Yelü Abaoji’s dream of ruling the Central Plains.

Yet as I lay dying in a tent at Shahu Lin, my body wracked by dysentery, I finally understood:

What I possessed was only the shell of power.

My soul had long since been torn into pieces.

This diary was written during the final months of my life.

From the trembling uncertainty of my accession in 927, to the exhilaration of acquiring the Sixteen Prefectures of Youyun in 938, and finally to the emptiness I felt after the fall of Bianliang in 947, every page is a testimony written in blood and tears.

I had never truly possessed power.

**I was merely its prisoner.**

---

# Chapter I: Accession — The Thread in My Mother’s Fingers

### Autumn, 927 — Imperial Camp at Shangjing

Today, I received the urn containing my father's ashes and took my place upon the throne of the Khitan Empire.

Outside the tent, the chiefs of the steppe tribes shouted their acclamations. The sound made the felt walls tremble.

Inside, my mother's gaze was like an icicle pressed against my spine.

“Deguang. From this day forward, you are the ruler of the Great Khitan.”

She handed me a jeweled scepter.

But her fingers dug hard into the back of my hand.

“Remember this: a ruler's blade must always be aimed at the throat of his enemies—not at his brothers.”

I knew exactly what she meant.

My elder brother, Yelü Bei, the learned “Prince of Renhuang,” stood quietly in the corner. His fingers were clenched beneath his sleeves.

He was five years older than I.

Father had loved listening to him recite poetry. He used to praise him as a man possessed of “the spirit of Yao and Shun.”

But Mother said:

“A scholar cannot rule the steppe.

Only your horse's hooves can crush everything that refuses to submit.”

---

### A Psychological Reading: Attachment Trauma and Anxiety over the Transfer of Power

My mother's need for control came from her absolute grip on political authority.

She had used the ancient institution of ritual suicide and funerary sacrifice to eliminate rivals, including three of my uncles.

She invoked “steppe tradition” whenever it served her, binding imperial authority to a council of elders whose approval was required for major decisions.

I grew up in her shadow.

When I was seven, I accompanied Father on the campaign against Balhae. Mother sent instructions ordering me never to make decisions alone.

When I was fifteen and temporarily entrusted with state affairs, she appointed her trusted adviser Yelü Wuzhi to “assist” me.

In truth, he was there to watch me.

At my enthronement ceremony, I wore a Khitan fur robe but a Chinese-style jade crown.

Mother said:

“The difference between Khitan and Han lies in the heart, not in clothing.”

But she did not know that my heart had already been divided.

One half was a steppe eagle, longing to fly free.

The other was a trapped animal, pulled by the threads in her hands.

---

### The Collapse of the Illusion of Power

**The gap between title and authority.**

I was emperor, yet I could not declare war without Mother's approval.

I could not freely appoint officials.

Important offices remained under her control.

I had no independent command of the treasury.

The historians wrote that I “personally presided over affairs of state.”

In reality, I was often little more than the voice reading out my mother's decrees.

**The shadow of brother against brother.**

My elder brother Yelü Bei voluntarily asked to surrender his claim to the throne and withdrew toward Bohai territory.

But he continued to maintain connections with Han officials who opposed me.

Mother told me:

“If he lives, he will become the greatest danger to you.”

I sent men to deliver poisoned wine.

He smiled when he received it.

“Little brother,” he said, “you are better suited to be emperor than I am—because you understand better than I do how to become a puppet.”

# Chapter II: The Sixteen Prefectures of Youyun — The Key and the Shackles

### Winter, 938 — Imperial Residence at Nanjing (Youzhou)

The envoy of Shi Jingtang knelt before me, holding a map of the Sixteen Prefectures of Youyun in both hands.

When he called me “Imperial Father,” I almost laughed.

This “son emperor,” more than ten years older than I was, had traded the Sixteen Prefectures for Khitan protection.

He did not understand that what he had given me was not simply territory.

It was a burning burden.

Mother wrote to me:

“Youyun is not the steppe. Its people are unpredictable. Proceed with caution.”

But when I looked at the names marked on the map—Youzhou, Jizhou, Yunzhou—I could almost see the granaries, salt fields, and iron mines of the Central Plains reaching toward me.

Father had once said:

**“Whoever possesses Youyun possesses the world.”**

How could I turn away?

---

### A Psychological Reading: Achievement Motivation and Cognitive Dissonance

My life was driven by a need to prove myself.

To prove that I was more suitable to rule than my elder brother.

To prove that I understood governance better than my mother.

To prove that the Khitan could rule the Central Plains.

That hunger for achievement made me underestimate the risks.

I rejected the advice of the Han official Han Dezhu, who urged me to win the people over before resorting to force.

Instead, I chose coercion through military pressure.

The result was displacement and suffering among the people of Youzhou.

I trusted Shi Jingtang's promise to send thirty thousand bolts of silk each year, without fully understanding how unstable his own position had become.

I established the Nanjing Regional Administration, yet appointed the Khitan noble Yelü Diela as its chief administrator.

He spoke little Chinese.

He understood little of local society.

Within three months, unrest had erupted.

---

### The Embodiment of Cultural Fracture

In Youzhou, I promoted a policy of **“governing according to local customs.”**

The Northern Administration followed Khitan traditions—the mobile imperial camp, the council system, the customs of the steppe.

The Southern Administration adopted institutions associated with Chinese rule—Confucian ritual, bureaucratic administration, and agricultural taxation.

My ministers praised me for “embracing both worlds.”

They did not know that every night I changed identities between **khagan and emperor**.

By day, I wore the robes of a Chinese sovereign.

I listened to Han officials explain the *Zhenguan Zhengyao*, trying to understand how benevolent rule might win the loyalty of the people.

At night, I changed back into my fur robe.

I drank mare's milk wine in the camp.

I listened to Khitan warriors recount their raids and spoils.

Violence became a way to numb my anxiety.

This split reached its peak after the destruction of the Later Jin.

I wore the dragon robe of a Chinese emperor.

Yet inside the palace, I insisted on setting up felt tents.

I allowed Han officials to formulate laws while tolerating Khitan soldiers' raids around western Bianliang.

The palace women whispered:

“The barbarians are pretending to be Chinese. They will destroy themselves eventually.”

I pretended not to hear.

I belonged neither to the steppe nor to the Central Plains.

**I was an orphan of power.**

# Chapter III: Bianliang — The Illusion of Power and the Weight of the People

### Spring, 947 — Imperial Palace at Bianliang

I sat upon the dragon throne of the Later Jin emperor.

Beneath me was brocade.

Before me flew the banners of the Great Liao.

When the officials knelt, I noticed something strangely revealing.

The Han officials pressed their foreheads to the ground.

The Khitan nobles knelt on one knee.

Their bodies were telling me what their mouths would not:

I was still a **“Khitan ruler.”**

Not yet the sovereign of all under Heaven.

---

### A Psychological Reading: The Alienation of Power and Emotional Detachment

Power is a distorting mirror.

It magnified every weakness within me.

**Cruelty as a means of establishing authority.**

To make an example of those who resisted, I ordered the heads of rebellious citizens displayed upon the city gates.

I watched them sway in the wind.

And, to my shame, I felt a momentary sense of control.

The military physician called it an affliction of “inner fire.”

But I saw something else.

I saw hatred in the eyes of the dead.

It looked like a frozen wolf alone upon the steppe.

**Pathological suspicion.**

I ordered the execution of the Han general Yang Guangyuan, a man who had once saved me, because he had drunkenly remarked:

“A barbarian ruler cannot last.”

As he was being taken to his death, he smiled.

“Does Your Majesty know,” he asked, “that you resemble the puppet emperor Shi Jingtang more than anyone?”

His words entered my heart like a snake.

I dreamed of them night after night.

**Emotional detachment.**

My only son, Yelü Jing, accompanied the army on campaign and died of illness at Xiangzhou.

When I received the news, I continued reviewing memorials in the council hall.

I did not stop.

Not until ink spilled across the imperial decree like a dark stain of blood.

---

### The Backlash of Popular Resentment

I tried to pacify the people through the idea of a unified Khitan-Han realm.

Instead, I made three fatal mistakes.

**Economic extraction.**

To support an army of two hundred thousand men, I ordered the collection of copper utensils from private households for coinage.

Panic buying followed.

Prices soared.

The people suffered.

**Cultural conflict.**

I restricted Han adoption of Khitan clothing and language while simultaneously forcing Khitan nobles to study Chinese ritual.

The magistrate Jia Wei openly challenged me in court:

“A Son of Heaven should follow Heaven and govern accordingly. How can the realm be divided into Khitan and Han?”

**Unrestrained violence.**

When Khitan soldiers conducted raids for food and supplies, I allowed them to plunder civilian homes.

One day I saw an old man shielding his grandson and begging:

“Please leave us enough grain to plant.”

I ordered the old man killed.

Not because I was angry.

Because I was afraid.

I was afraid that his eyes would awaken whatever remained of my conscience.

# Chapter IV: Shahu Lin — The Collapse of Power and the Final Awakening

### Summer, 947 — Imperial Camp at Shahu Lin

The diarrhea would not stop.

The physicians said that poisonous miasma had entered my body.

I lay upon the imperial couch and listened to the sounds of fighting outside the tent—Khitan soldiers clashing with Han civilians.

And suddenly I understood.

I had never truly possessed power.

Those who knelt before me feared the sword, not the emperor.

Those Han officials who cried “Long live the Emperor” were thinking of something else entirely:

*Restore the Chinese dynasties.*

---

### A Psychological Reading: Existential Crisis and the Anxiety of Death

**The collapse of dependence on power.**

I had tried to unite Khitan and Han society through a dual political structure.

But cultural prejudice, institutional contradictions, and failures of governance produced disorder.

An Chongrong, military governor of Chengde, gathered troops under his own command and openly declared:

**“I would rather be a ghost in China than a subject of the barbarians.”**

And I found myself unable to suppress him.

**The embodiment of death anxiety.**

I kept a black leopard in the palace.

Whenever an order was obstructed, I entertained myself by feeding the beast.

Last night, it tore off the arm of its trainer.

For one brief moment, the smell of blood brought me peace.

Even I was frightened by that pleasure.

---

### The Final Vision

As death approached, I saw my elder brother Yelü Bei standing among the clouds.

He said:

**“You conquered the world, but lost yourself.”**

I reached out.

All I held was a handful of cold earth.

---

### My Final Reflection on the Nature of Power

**The relativity of power.**

I destroyed the Later Jin.

I occupied Bianliang.

Yet I could not control the price of wheat in Hebei.

I could not command the grain transport from Jiangnan.

I could not control the merchants traveling toward the western regions.

The title of “Sovereign of All Under Heaven” was ultimately little more than a phrase written by historians.

**The corrosive nature of power.**

I had begun as a steppe ruler who won victories from horseback.

I became a lonely sovereign hidden behind palace walls, calculating the thoughts of others.

Power was a mirror.

It reflected every weakness and every ugliness within me.

**The emptiness of power.**

When I took my final breath at Shahu Lin, only one mute guard remained beside me.

The ministers who had once shouted “Long live the Emperor” had already fled north with the Later Jin ruler Shi Chonggui.

Only then did I understand.

I had spent my entire life fighting for an empty palace.

---

# Chapter V: The Imperial Preserved Corpse — Power's Final Sacrifice

### Autumn, 947 — Luancheng

I am dead.

They say that, to prevent decay, my body was preserved with salt and transformed into what later generations called an **“imperial preserved corpse.”**

When my mother, Empress Dowager Shulü, heard of my death, she reportedly said only:

**“He defied my command and sought to seize the Central Plains. This is the price.”**

This body, emptied by power, became an object preserved in salt.

My ambition.

My fear.

My pride.

My confusion.

All of it seeped into the flesh with the salt.

What remained was a specimen without a soul.

What is power?

It is the sword of Damocles hanging above one's head.

It is poison coated in honey.

It is a prison in which people willingly surrender their freedom.

I once believed I was its master.

In the end,

**I became its slave.**

This is the story of Yelü Deguang.

## 2. Psychological Profiles of Key Figures

### Empress Dowager Shulü

She eliminated political rivals through funerary sacrifice and used “steppe tradition” to constrain imperial authority.

Her relationship with Yelü Deguang can be interpreted as one involving intense political and emotional control.

*(Bowen, 1978)*

### Yelü Bei

Displaced from the succession despite being the eldest son, he used apparent withdrawal to conceal continued political ambitions.

His behavior may be interpreted through the lens of passive resistance and unresolved conflict.

*(Horney, 1945)*

### Zhao Yanshou

A political opportunist who repeatedly shifted allegiance in pursuit of wealth and status, serving different regimes as circumstances changed.

His behavior illustrates the psychological dynamics of political opportunism under unstable regimes.

*(Cleckley, 1941)*

---

## 3. Modern Lessons from the Tragedy of Power

### The Trap of Cognitive Bias

Yelü Deguang equated **military victory with political success**.

The conquest of territory was mistaken for the achievement of legitimate and sustainable governance.

This can be discussed in relation to overconfidence and the limits of metacognitive awareness.

*(Kruger & Dunning, 1999)*

### The Paradox of Cultural Integration

The attempt to govern different populations through rigidly differentiated institutions created a structural tension between unity and separation.

Rather than automatically producing integration, institutional separation could reinforce social boundaries.

This provides a historical case through which to consider the contact hypothesis and the conditions required for meaningful intergroup integration.

*(Allport, 1954)*

### The Ethical Failure of Power

When political authority loses its ethical constraints, power can become detached from its original purpose and transformed into an instrument of coercion.

The tragedy of Yelü Deguang lies not simply in military defeat.

It lies in the gradual transformation of **power from a means of governing into an end in itself**.

# Epilogue: The Epitaph of Power

I died of dysentery.

I was only forty-six.

Before the end, I ordered someone to place the urn containing Yelü Abaoji's ashes beside my pillow.

Perhaps I imagined that by doing so I could return to the days when I was a young man on the steppe, chasing antelope beneath the open sky.

The histories say that my achievements and failures were mixed.

But I know what my life truly was:

**a tragedy of power.**

I obtained the crown of the Central Plains,

but lost the wind of the steppe.

I conquered millions of subjects,

but never conquered the demons within myself.

Today, my tomb lies in Huizhou, Liaoning.

My posthumous title reads:

**“Wenwu Yuan Emperor.”**

But I know that this title is merely another set of shackles placed upon me by those who came after.

The real me was the prisoner who woke in the middle of the night in Bianjing.

The empty shell slowly devoured by power.

What is power?

It is the sword of Damocles hanging above one's head.

It is poison coated in honey.

It is a prison in which people willingly surrender their freedom.

I once believed I was its master.

In the end,

**I became its slave.**

This is the story of Yelü Deguang.

*This work adopts the first-person perspective of Yelü Deguang and combines historical events from the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period with a literary reconstruction of inner experience. Through narrative and psychological interpretation, it explores the deeper mechanisms of power, cultural fracture, political legitimacy, and the alienation of the individual under absolute authority.*

*The psychological interpretations are literary and analytical rather than clinical diagnoses of the historical figures.*
