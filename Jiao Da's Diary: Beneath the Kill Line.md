# **Jiao Da's Diary: Beneath the Kill Line**

### *A Servant's View of Lin Daiyu, Class, Resentment, and the Cost of Being Human*

**Keywords:** `#Dream of the Red Chamber` `#Chinese Literature` `#Cultural Media`

---

## Preface to the Diary

My name is **Jiao Da**.

I am an old servant of the Ningguo household.

I am seventy-three years old this year.

When I was young, I followed the old masters into battle. I have drunk horse urine. I have bled on the battlefield. I have carried men who were already dead.

Then I grew old.

And when I was no longer useful for fighting, I was thrown into the stables to feed the horses.

People in the household call me **“the mad old fool.”**

They say that I drink too much, talk nonsense, and curse everyone from Jia Zhen to Lady Wang.

But they do not understand.

I am not cursing one person.

I am cursing **the Kill Line**.

That invisible line that divides the world into those who stand above—

and those who live beneath the horses.

The line that decides who is allowed to be a *person*,

and who is expected simply to endure.

---

Lately, I have been dreaming about a girl.

Her surname is Lin.

Her name is Daiyu.

Everyone calls her **Lin Mei-mei**—Little Sister Lin.

She is a young lady of the Rongguo household, living in the Xiaoxiang Pavilion.

They say she is extraordinarily beautiful.

They say she cries easily.

I have lived for more than seventy years.

I have seen too many people who were born “above the line.”

But I have never felt what I felt when I first saw her.

It was like being pricked by a needle.

Not exactly pain.

Something sour.

Something bitter.

Something tangled that even I could not name.

So tonight, with enough wine in me to loosen my tongue, I am writing it all down.

Perhaps after I am dead and gone, someone will find these rotten pages and understand something about how people live—

**beneath the Kill Line.**

---

# I. The First Sight of Lin Daiyu

### *That White Figure Pierced My Eyes*

**Qianlong Year 43 · Autumn · Ninth Month, Third Day**

It was almost dark.

I was crouching beneath the second gate, chewing on a cold piece of steamed bread, when I heard the faint ringing of jade ornaments.

I looked up.

For a moment, I nearly thought I had seen a ghost.

A girl in a white gauze dress was walking toward me.

A silver hairpin rested beside her temple.

The corners of her eyes drooped slightly, as though they held two pools of autumn water.

She did not walk quickly.

The hem of her dress brushed the stone pavement.

Not a speck of dust touched it.

“Who is that?” I asked the foolish maid sweeping beside me.

She spat out a melon seed shell.

“Old Jiao, have you gone senile? That's Miss Lin! The Old Lady's favorite!”

Miss Lin.

The words rang inside my head.

I remembered the days when the old masters were still alive.

I had seen plenty of young ladies then.

Every one of them dressed in silk and gold.

Every one walked as though the entire world belonged to her.

But this girl was different.

She was too white.

White as snow.

White as the coat of the mare I used to feed.

Yet there was something fragile about that whiteness.

As though the slightest touch might make her disappear.

What hurt most were her eyes.

So bright.

So clean.

Nothing like these hands of mine, which have spent a lifetime handling manure and feed, with dirt permanently lodged beneath the fingernails.

---

When she passed me, a faint fragrance entered my nose.

Not incense.

Something like the smell of leaves and grass.

Like a willow branch putting out its first shoots in spring.

Suddenly I remembered that I too had once owned clean clothes.

When I was young, the old master gave me a blue cotton robe.

I was afraid to wear it.

I saved it for New Year's Day.

And now?

Now I wore a jacket patched over and over again.

Even that had been handed down to me last year.

“Jiao Da! Get out of the way! Don't block Miss Lin!”

A young servant shoved me.

I stumbled backward and struck a pillar.

Miss Lin turned and looked at me.

Her gaze was light.

Almost weightless.

Like a feather.

Yet that weightless glance kept me awake all night.

I remembered being young.

I remembered the old master once putting his hand on my shoulder.

> “Jiao Da. Well done.”

Once, I had been someone.

Now I was merely:

> **something dirty standing in the way.**

---

### Psychological Note

Jiao Da's sudden sense of pain can be interpreted through the framework of **relative deprivation** (Stouffer et al., 1949).

The important point is not absolute poverty alone, but the psychological impact of witnessing someone else possess what one feels one should have been entitled to—or at least what one has been denied.

Lin Daiyu becomes a living contrast:

* beauty versus physical degradation;
* cleanliness versus dirt;
* refinement versus illiteracy;
* social recognition versus invisibility.

Her whiteness and fragrance become mirrors reflecting Jiao Da's own sense of humiliation.

The underlying question is not simply:

> *“Why does she have so much?”*

It is:

> **“Why was I never allowed to become someone like that?”**

# II. What I Heard About Miss Lin

### *Perhaps Even Beauty Is a Crime*

**Qianlong Year 43 · Autumn · Ninth Month, Fifteenth Day**

Today I overheard two maids gossiping in the kitchen.

They were talking about Miss Lin.

“She's so frail. She's always taking medicine. The Old Lady dotes on her terribly.”

“Of course. Didn't Miss Baochai send her bird's nest soup the other day? She complained it was too sweet. Said she preferred the lighter flavors from Yangzhou.”

“They say her poetry is wonderful. At the chrysanthemum poetry gathering, Master Jia praised her as the *Xiaoxiang Concubine*. He said she surpassed even Tanchun.”

I crouched beneath the stove and listened.

My heart felt as though someone had stuffed it with dead grass.

Frail?

Too sweet?

Good at poetry?

All these things they called “fortunate” sounded to me like evidence of a crime.

I had taken knives in battle.

I had bled enough blood to fill a basin.

I never once said I was “too frail.”

When I fed the horses, I ate moldy bean cakes.

They tasted better to me than anything from the imperial kitchen.

I cannot read more than a few characters.

All I can do with poetry is curse at it when I have drunk enough wine.

They say Miss Lin is “pitiful.”

They say she lost both parents and lives under another family's roof.

But I think:

What is so pitiful about that?

At least someone gives her bird's nest soup.

Someone listens when she recites poetry.

Someone hands her a handkerchief when she cries.

Me?

I have given more than fifty years of my life to the Jia household.

The old master died.

My son died.

And now I have to fight for a bowl of hot food.

Last winter I was so hungry that I drank the bean feed from the horse trough.

Zhou Rui's wife caught me.

She had me beaten twenty times.

Then she called me:

> “Old bastard. Trying to waste food meant for the master's horses?”

---

“Jiao Da! What are you doing here?”

Wang Shanbao's wife suddenly appeared.

She kicked me in the back.

I struggled to my feet.

The keys hanging from her waist jingled.

Those keys represented power.

She controlled the storerooms.

She looked at me sideways.

> “Listen again and I'll have you thrown outside the city to feed the wolves.”

I limped away.

Behind me, the maids laughed.

And suddenly I understood.

There were two kinds of pity.

Miss Lin's “pity” belonged to those above the Kill Line.

Mine belonged to those below it.

Her suffering was tragic.

Mine was deserved.

It was like the horses I fed.

When a horse became sick, someone treated it.

When it was hungry, someone fed it.

When I became sick, I could lie in the stable and wait to die.

When I was hungry,

I ate cold bread.

---

### Psychological Note

Jiao Da's interpretation illustrates a form of **zero-sum thinking**.

He experiences another person's advantages—beauty, education, affection, comfort—not simply as independent goods, but as evidence of his own deprivation.

In this cognitive frame:

> **Their gain becomes my loss.**

Such thinking can emerge under prolonged experiences of scarcity and exclusion.

When an individual repeatedly experiences resources, dignity, and recognition as things that are available to others but inaccessible to oneself, inequality may begin to feel like a personal theft—even when no direct exchange exists.

Jiao Da therefore transforms Lin Daiyu's privilege into a kind of accusation.

Her comfort becomes his evidence.

Her beauty becomes his humiliation.

Her suffering becomes difficult for him to recognize as suffering at all.

---

# III. I Dreamed of Lin Daiyu

### *Her Tears Put Out My Fire*

**Qianlong Year 43 · Autumn · Tenth Month, Second Day**

Last night I had a strange dream.

I dreamed I had become a horse.

I was tied inside the stable.

Across from me stood Miss Lin.

She wore the same white gauze dress.

In her hand was a riding crop.

She touched my back with it.

It did not hurt.

It tickled.

Then she began to cry.

Her tears fell onto my nose.

They were warm.

“Jiao Da,” she said.

Her voice sounded like wind moving through bamboo.

> “Why do you always curse at people?”

I opened my mouth.

Nothing came out.

I wanted to tell her:

I am not cursing you.

I am cursing those who have ridden me like a horse.

I wanted to tell her:

I too was once young.

I too was once called a hero.

I wanted to tell her:

I now live worse than a horse.

Even the horses eat better than I do.

But I could not speak.

My throat felt stuffed with cotton.

She reached out and touched my head.

Her fingertips were cool.

Suddenly I remembered my white mare.

When she died, her body had been cold in exactly the same way.

I held her neck and cried for half the night.

No one came.

No one cared.

But Miss Lin's hand was warm.

Warm like sunlight.

“Don't be sad,” she said.

> “I'll ask Zijuan to bring you some pastries.”

Pastries?

I stared at the embroidered handkerchief in her hand.

A peony was stitched across it.

Red as blood.

Can pastries feed a man?

Can they cure my aching legs?

Can they make me young again?

---

I woke up with my pillow soaked.

I could not tell whether it was sweat or tears.

The moon outside was round.

It looked like Miss Lin's face.

For the first time, I thought:

Perhaps she is not happy either.

She is so fragile.

She cries so easily.

Perhaps she lies awake at night too,

thinking about the things that hurt her.

Perhaps she is more like me than I thought.

But the moment the thought appeared,

I strangled it.

“Bullshit!”

I shouted at the wall.

> “She's a young lady. I'm an old servant. How could her suffering possibly compare with mine?”

---

### Psychological Note

The dream contains what might be called a **reconciliation image**.

Lin Daiyu cries.

She touches Jiao Da.

She offers him food.

At the symbolic level, she is no longer an unreachable young lady above him, but a figure capable of recognizing his suffering.

This can be read through the lens of **attachment theory** (Bowlby, 1969): beneath Jiao Da's anger lies a profound desire to be seen, cared for, and treated as someone whose existence matters.

But when he wakes, he immediately rejects this possibility.

This creates a form of **cognitive dissonance** (Festinger, 1957).

Two beliefs collide:

1. *People above me cannot truly understand people like me.*
2. *Perhaps this woman can actually see my pain.*

To preserve his existing worldview, Jiao Da chooses the first.

His anger protects him from vulnerability.

His contempt protects him from disappointment.

And his insistence—

> **“Her suffering cannot compare with mine.”**

—becomes a psychological wall protecting the identity he has built around deprivation.

---

# IV. Seeing Her Again

### *Her Smile Was a Knife*

**Qianlong Year 43 · Winter · Eleventh Month, Eighth Day**

There was heavy snow today.

I was sweeping the entrance when I saw Miss Lin approaching in a sedan chair.

She wore a crimson cloak.

Against the snow, she looked like a flower blooming in winter.

The chair stopped.

She stepped down with Zijuan's help.

Then she looked up.

And smiled.

I froze.

Her smile was faint.

Just the slightest curve of the lips.

Her eyes narrowed into crescents.

The broom slipped from my hands.

It struck the ground.

I remembered the last time she had looked at me.

That gaze had been light.

Almost empty.

This time it was warm.

Could she remember me?

Could she remember the old man crouched at the gate,

eating cold bread?

“Grandfather Jiao,” she said softly.

> “It's cold. You should rest for a while.”

I hurried to pick up the broom.

My fingers were frozen stiff.

I could barely close them around the handle.

Zijuan stepped forward to help.

Miss Lin stopped her.

Then she took a handkerchief from her sleeve and held it out to me.

> “Wipe your hands. Don't let them freeze.”

The handkerchief was plain white.

Several bamboo branches were embroidered across it.

When I took it, my fingers touched hers.

Cold as jade.

Suddenly I remembered my daughter.

When she was little, she smiled like that.

She too liked folding handkerchiefs into perfect squares and using them to wipe the sweat from my face.

But she died.

She died on the road during the famine.

There was not even enough wood for a coffin.

---

“Th… thank you, Miss Lin.”

My voice trembled.

I pushed the handkerchief into my clothes as though I were hiding something dangerous.

She nodded.

Then she walked away.

A line of tiny footprints remained in the snow.

Fresh snow soon covered them.

I stood there watching her disappear beneath the hanging flower gate.

The handkerchief smelled faintly of her.

And suddenly I was afraid.

Afraid that Wang Shanbao's wife would see it.

Afraid she would laugh and call me a toad dreaming of eating swan meat.

Afraid she would take it from me and throw it into the latrine.

That night I hid the handkerchief beneath the horse trough.

I covered it with dry straw.

I did not dare look at it.

Because if I looked at it,

I would remember her smile.

I would remember my daughter's smile.

And I would remember all the happiness I had never been allowed to have.

---

### Psychological Note

Jiao Da interprets Lin Daiyu's kindness through the framework of **status inequality**.

To someone who has spent decades being humiliated, generosity from someone of higher status may feel less like equality and more like condescension.

The handkerchief therefore becomes psychologically ambiguous.

It is simultaneously:

* kindness;
* recognition;
* charity;
* humiliation;
* and proof that the giver occupies a position above the receiver.

Jiao Da's reaction can be understood as a form of **defensive low self-worth**.

When self-worth has been repeatedly damaged, kindness itself can become threatening.

The question becomes:

> *“Why would someone like her be kind to someone like me?”*

Rather than accepting the gift, he hides it.

Not because he does not value it—

but because he values it too much.

---

# V. Listening to Her Poetry

### *Perhaps “Refinement” Is Another Kind of Bullying*

**Qianlong Year 43 · Winter · Eleventh Month, Twentieth Day**

There was a poetry gathering at the Rongguo household today.

I sneaked around to the wall and listened.

Miss Lin sat beneath a flowering crabapple tree.

She held a collection of poems in her hands.

Then she recited:

> *“Upon these pages I inscribe my quiet grievances;
> Who can understand the heart of autumn?”*

Her voice was beautiful.

Like water running over stones.

But I understood none of it.

What was “quiet grievance”?

What was “the heart of autumn”?

The only things I know how to say are:

> “I'm hungry again today.”

> “The stable roof is leaking.”

> “Half my bedding is soaked.”

Old Nurse Li elbowed me.

“Jiao Da, stop listening. This is a game for young ladies. People like us don't understand such things.”

“I was just curious.”

That was what I said aloud.

But inside, something pricked me.

Curiosity?

No.

> **Jealousy.**

How could she produce such beautiful words?

I can barely write my own name without twisting the characters.

How could she sit in a warm room, drinking wine beneath plum blossoms?

While I stood outside in the snow, sweeping until my hands and feet went numb?

---

When the poetry gathering ended, Miss Lin left surrounded by her maids.

I found one of the petals she had dropped.

I picked it up.

Held it beneath my nose.

It smelled wonderful.

Then one of the maids came running back.

She saw the petal in my hand.

She screamed.

> “What filthy thing is that?”

She snatched it away.

Dropped it onto the ground.

And crushed it beneath her shoe.

I looked at the fragments.

And suddenly I laughed.

Loudly.

So loudly that the sparrows in the tree flew away.

Old Nurse Li asked what I was laughing about.

I said nothing.

I knew.

I was laughing at myself.

At the old fool who had just imagined that something beautiful might belong to him.

At the old fool who had thought, even for a moment,

that something *refined*

might be meant for someone like him.

---

### Psychological Note

Jiao Da's rejection of poetry can be understood through Pierre Bourdieu's concept of **cultural capital** (Bourdieu, 1986).

Poetry, etiquette, literary knowledge, taste, and artistic refinement do more than provide pleasure.

They also function as **social markers**.

They tell people:

> *This is who belongs here.*

And, implicitly:

> *This is who does not.*

For someone deprived of such cultural capital, “refinement” may appear deliberately hostile.

Jiao Da does not necessarily hate beauty itself.

He hates what beauty reminds him of:

> **a world from which he has been excluded.**

For him, poetry is not an aesthetic experience.

It is evidence of distance.

A full stomach comes before a beautiful metaphor.

Warm bedding comes before an appreciation of plum blossoms.

And so “refinement” begins to look like another form of power.

Another way of saying:

> **You do not belong.**

# VI. The Final Thought

### *Why Do I Hate Her—and Fear Her?*

**Qianlong Year 43 · Winter · Twelfth Month, First Day**

Today is my seventy-third birthday.

No one remembered.

I cooked myself a bowl of longevity noodles and ate them with cold bread.

Afterward I lay in the stable, staring at the hole in the roof.

And I thought about Miss Lin.

Do I hate her?

Yes.

I hate her white dresses.

I hate her fragrant handkerchief.

I hate her poetry.

I hate the way one smile from her can make me forget who I am.

Do I fear her?

Yes.

I fear her eyes.

I fear her kindness.

I fear the way she makes me remember that I too was once a human being—

and not merely an old servant.

---

I have lived for more than seventy years.

At last I understand what the Kill Line really is.

It is not a line.

> **It is a net.**

A net that binds people like me so tightly that there is almost no way out.

We can see the world above us.

We can see the light.

We can see the people living there.

But we can never climb high enough to join them.

So we envy them.

And then we curse them for enjoying their lives.

Perhaps Miss Lin really is a good person.

She gave me her handkerchief.

She smiled at me.

But the kinder she was,

the more painful it became.

Because she forced me to recognize something I had spent my entire life refusing to see:

> **The distance between the people above the Kill Line and those beneath it is not a step.**

> **It is a chasm.**

---

The stable is quiet tonight.

I take the handkerchief from inside my clothes.

I smell it.

Tomorrow,

I will return it to Miss Lin.

I will tell her:

I do not want your charity.

I do not want your pity.

> **I only want my own life back.**

The life of the Jiao Da who followed the old master into battle.

The Jiao Da who was not afraid of death.

The Jiao Da who once believed he was a man.

---

### Psychological Note

Jiao Da's simultaneous hatred and fear reflects an **ambivalent attachment to status and recognition**.

He resents those above him because their existence reminds him of his own deprivation.

Yet he also longs for their recognition.

This produces a psychological contradiction:

> **“I hate that you are above me.”**

and simultaneously:

> **“Please see that I exist.”**

His anger protects his dignity.

His longing exposes his vulnerability.

His hostility protects the boundary between “us” and “them.”

Yet Lin Daiyu's kindness keeps crossing that boundary.

That is precisely why it frightens him.

The handkerchief becomes a symbolic object through which he attempts to reclaim agency.

Returning it is not merely rejecting a gift.

It is an attempt to say:

> **I do not want to be pitied.**

> **I want to recover the person I was before the world taught me that I was worth less.**

## Author's Note

This work is a **literary reconstruction**, not a historical diary.

Jiao Da is a character from *Dream of the Red Chamber*, while the diary format, his encounters with Lin Daiyu, and the psychological reflections presented here are imaginative extensions inspired by the original novel.

The concept of the **“Kill Line”** is used here as a contemporary metaphor for social stratification, relative deprivation, and the psychological boundary between those who possess security and recognition and those who live with chronic exclusion.

The psychological notes draw on established theories in social psychology, sociology, and attachment research. They are interpretive frameworks applied to the fictional characters, rather than clinical diagnoses.

The purpose is not to decide whether Lin Daiyu is privileged or whether Jiao Da is justified in his resentment.

It is to ask a harder question:

> **What happens to a person who can see dignity above him every day, but can no longer imagine that dignity belonging to him?**

---

> *Perhaps the deepest tragedy of the Kill Line is not that some people live above it.*
>
> *It is that those beneath it eventually begin to believe they were born beneath it.*
>
> **And once a person believes that, even kindness can feel like humiliation.**
