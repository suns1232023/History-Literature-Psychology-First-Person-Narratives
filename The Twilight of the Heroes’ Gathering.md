# The Twilight of the Heroes’ Gathering

### The Last Testament of a Manor Lord

*Every feast held in the name of “righteous brotherhood” eventually reveals its bare bones when the tables are cleared.*

*When I heard of that gathering of forty thousand souls in Jiangnan, I suddenly understood—*

*we had never truly understood what the word “hero” meant.*

**Keywords:** #Culture & Media#

---

## Volume I: Snow Upon the Ruins

**Winter, the Ninth Year of Yuanyou, Northern Song Dynasty
The Ruins of Juxianzhuang, a Snowy Night**

The snow began falling around midnight.

At first it was only sleet, whispering against the scraps of mulberry paper still clinging to the window frames. Then it thickened into flakes, filling the sky and earth, burying beneath three feet of white the flashes of steel from thirty years ago, the blood of more than three hundred men, and the gathering of “heroes” once proclaimed as a righteous crusade.

The brazier was nearly out.

Curled beneath a tiger-skin quilt, I was reading a letter that had traveled a thousand years before reaching me from Jiangnan.

It spoke of a newly built “Tiangong Hall” outside Suzhou, large enough to hold forty thousand people. A wealthy merchant house had spared no expense to host a “Reunion Gathering,” inviting a celebrated speaker from the capital, a popular singer to perform, and—most astonishing of all—sending carts laden with gold to distribute among its workers.

More astonishing still, ten of them were to be sent south to Antarctica, that legendary frozen wilderness at the end of the world.

My hands began to tremble.

Not because of such unimaginable wealth.

Because of something else.

Because of the atmosphere.

It was painfully familiar.

Thirty years ago, when the Heroes’ Invitation of Juxianzhuang was scattered across the martial world, it had carried precisely the same sense of spectacle—something that would “shake the realm.”

Only our invitations had been plain paper, while theirs were gilded.

We gathered three hundred “heroes”; they gathered forty thousand “family members.”

We prepared wine for the breaking of friendships and blades for killing.

They prepared music, laughter, and gold.

The snowlight fell across the letter. The neat little characters seemed to dance before my eyes, becoming the torches that once flickered through the great hall, becoming the eyes of my younger brother You Ju, brightened by the fever of empty glory.

Suddenly I began to cough.

My lungs wheezed like a broken bellows.

Between spasms of coughing, a voice pierced thirty years of silence and struck my ears with merciless clarity:

**You Ji—whom were you really holding that gathering for?**

The brazier gave one final *crack*.

One last spark.

The night was still long.

Long enough for a dying man to take apart his entire life, break it into pieces, and, beneath the cold light of snow, try to stitch it back together.

# Volume II: The Feast

## I. The Invitation: A Summons in the Name of Righteousness

The seed of the Heroes’ Gathering was planted on a bitterly cold afternoon in early spring.

My younger brother, You Ju, burst into my study with a message from the Beggars’ Sect hidden inside his sleeve.

There was a light in his eyes I had never seen before.

It was the look of a starving beast glimpsing prey.

“Qiao Feng is a Khitan,” he whispered, each word burning like a coal. “He killed his adoptive parents, Qiao Sanhuai and his wife. He killed Master Xuanku of Shaolin. Brother, this is heaven’s gift!”

He lingered heavily on those last four words.

*Heaven’s gift.*

I understood exactly what he meant.

The two brothers of the You clan—known throughout the martial world as the “Twin Heroes”—sounded formidable enough. In truth, we were little more than two men tending the hearth at Juxianzhuang.

The martial world was a great pot of broth.

We always floated on its surface.

We never tasted what simmered at the bottom.

Shaolin had stood for a thousand years. The Beggars’ Sect was the greatest brotherhood beneath heaven. Even the Gusu Murong clan was revered because of the legend of *“using the enemy’s own techniques against him.”*

And we?

We were merely the hospitable brothers of the You family.

“We don't carry enough weight on our own,” I said, staring at a magnolia tree outside the window, its buds not yet fully open.

“There is Xue Muhua.”

My brother smiled.

It was the smile of a hunter who has successfully sprung a trap.

“He owes us a debt. With his name on the invitation, the Heroes’ Invitation becomes a summons from the King of Hell. Who would dare refuse?”

Xue Muhua.

Known throughout the martial world as the “Enemy of the King of Hell.”

Three years earlier, he had fallen from a cliff while gathering herbs. My brother and I had searched for him for three days and three nights, eventually dragging him half-dead from the jaws of wolves.

He owed us.

That debt had to be repaid.

My brother personally supervised the invitations.

Gilded paper.

Formal calligraphy.

At the very top were three names:

**Xue Muhua.
You Ji.
You Ju.**

I watched as the craftsman added a tiny line beneath Xue Muhua’s name:

*“Master Xue Muhua, renowned throughout the realm as the Divine Physician.”*

My brother smiled with satisfaction.

“We have to make sure even the illiterate brutes understand whose invitation this is.”

At that moment, a faint unease passed through me.

Like the sudden coldness in one’s fingertips just before revealing the final card at a gambling table.

But I suppressed it.

The magnolia opened.

Its branches were covered in white blossoms.

Like silent snow.

---

## II. The Guests: A Feast of Hidden Motives

Three hundred and twenty-seven men.

I would remember that number until the day I died.

They came from beyond the frontier, from Lingnan, Bashu, Jiangnan—pouring into Juxianzhuang from every direction.

The sweaty smell of horses mingled with the rough scent peculiar to wandering martial artists, until the manor seemed to become a vat of wine fermented far beyond its proper strength.

Dan Zheng arrived with his five sons, like a man leading five unsheathed swords.

Tan Gong and Tan Po came together, the quarrelsome old couple even holding hands in public.

Zhao Qiansun came too, one of the survivors of the tragedy beyond Yanmen Pass thirty years before. He kept muttering:

“Who am I? I am already a walking corpse.”

There was Bao Qianling, “the Penniless.”

Qi Liu, “the Swift Blade.”

Xiang Wanghai of eastern Hunan.

The thirty Eight Immortal Tables in the main hall were packed to overflowing.

I wore a newly tailored brocade robe and moved among the guests with a wine cup in hand.

Every time someone said,

“Lord You, what righteousness!”

it was like another spoonful of honey poured over the dry desert of my vanity.

My brother's face was even redder with delight, as if the ancestral tablets of the entire You clan had suddenly begun to shine.

Only Xue Muhua sat quietly at the head table, sipping tea in small mouthfuls.

His eyes swept over the noisy hall.

Now and then they met mine.

Something flickered in them.

Pity?

Contempt?

I could not tell.

Nor did I want to.

For in that moment, I felt as though I had climbed to the summit of the martial world.

Below me were countless ordinary men.

In my hands lay life and death.

Only later did I begin to wonder:

Of those three hundred and twenty-seven men, how many truly believed Qiao Feng was a murderer of parents and teachers?

How many had genuinely come for “righteousness”?

The elders of the Beggars’ Sect were desperate to distance themselves from their former leader.

The monks of Shaolin needed to preserve a reputation built over centuries.

The various martial heroes came carrying hopes of winning Xue Muhua’s favor.

And many of the nameless men had come for nothing more than a spectacle—something they could later boast about over wine:

“I was there at Juxianzhuang.”

**A feast held in the name of justice.**

**Its chef was ambition.**

**Its ingredients were calculation.**

**And what it served was human kindness gone cold.**

---

## III. The Gambit: The Man Who Entered Alone

He came.

There were no galloping hooves.

No herald announcing his arrival.

Not even a knock at the door.

At noon, when the wine had gone around three times and someone had begun shouting,

“That Khitan dog must be afraid to show his face!”

he walked through the main gate carrying a woman in his arms.

Sunlight split the crowd and fell directly upon him.

His blue robe was stained with blood.

His face was exhausted.

But his back remained straight.

The young woman in pale green whom he carried looked as though her face had been painted with gold leaf. Her breath was barely perceptible.

The entire hall fell silent.

Three hundred people, and not a sound except the occasional *pop* of a candle flame.

“Qiao Feng begs your pardon for intruding.”

His voice was not loud.

Yet it silenced every breath in the room.

“I ask Master Xue to save this woman's life.”

My brother was the first to leap to his feet.

He had drunk too much. His face was red as liver.

“Qiao Feng! You Khitan barbarian! You dare come here to die?”

Qiao Feng did not even look at him.

His eyes remained on Xue Muhua.

“She is A'Zhu. She took a Vajra Palm strike from Shaolin while protecting me. Master Xue is the only person in the world who can save her. I, Qiao Feng, will spend the rest of my life repaying this debt.”

“Repaying it?”

Xue Muhua lowered his teacup.

Porcelain struck the pearwood table with a crisp *click*.

“You have slaughtered my Han countrymen. Our hatred is irreconcilable. Why should I save her? So that she may live and help you kill more Han people?”

“I have never killed an innocent person.”

“Your adoptive parents and your teacher were innocent?”

Qiao Feng's face turned pale.

Not the pallor of fear.

It was the cold whiteness of jade moments before a hammer strikes.

He looked around the hall.

His eyes passed over every face:

Angry faces.

Avoiding faces.

Excited faces.

At last, he looked toward me.

“Lord You,” he said.

“Was this gathering held for me?”

My throat went dry.

No words came.

My brother answered for me.

“So what if it was? The heroes of the realm are gathered here today. We will surely rid the world of a villain like you!”

Qiao Feng smiled.

There was something in that smile that made my heart tremble.

“Very well.”

He paused.

“If that is so, I shall first drink a cup of farewell wine with every one of you. From this day forward, friendship is friendship, hatred is hatred.”

He smashed open a wine jar.

Strong liquor poured into great bowls.

He drank one bowl after another.

With each bowl, he named an old friend and said:

“Brother so-and-so, whatever friendship once existed between us ends in this bowl.”

When he reached Master Zhiguang, the old monk accepted the wine with trembling hands, tears streaming down his face.

When he reached Elder Xu, the old man sighed deeply and drained his bowl.

When he reached my brother, Qiao Feng stared at him for three breaths.

He said nothing.

He simply drank.

The bowl shattered against the ground.

A clear, brittle sound.

Like something that could never be put back together.

---

## IV. Blood: Justice Cooked in the Fire of Emotion

The rest has been told in countless versions throughout the martial world.

Some say Qiao Feng lost control and killed dozens.

Others say the heroes of the Central Plains united as one and nearly destroyed him.

Only I know the truth.

It was a gamble in which everyone lost.

The first to attack was Xiang Wanghai.

He struck Qiao Feng from behind.

Qiao Feng's palm sent him flying. He crashed into two pillars and broke them.

Then came Dan Zheng's fifth son, Dan Xiaoshan.

His blade had barely begun its descent when Qiao Feng's kick struck him in the chest.

The crowd exploded like a disturbed hive.

Blades.

Hidden weapons.

Fists.

Feet.

They surged from every direction toward the lone figure in blue.

But what I remember most clearly are the details.

Qi Liu, the Swift Blade, died.

He died beneath the combined palms of Qiao Feng, Xuannan, and Xuanji.

Yet Xuannan folded his hands and said:

“Amitabha. Alas, Qiao Feng, what a terrible sin you have committed.”

Qiao Feng stood covered in blood and laughed toward the heavens.

“He struck at me. I killed half of him. Your two brothers killed the other half. How is all of it charged to my account?”

Xuannan replied:

“If you had not harmed others first, how could this battle have happened?”

**Look at the arithmetic of “justice.”**

When the majority needs one man to bear all the guilt, even Buddhist doctrine can be made to calculate the proportions.

Tan Po, the smiling old woman, quietly slipped behind a pillar and tried to kill the dying A'Zhu with an iron awl.

Zhao Qiansun shouted,

“I am a walking corpse!”

while scattering poisoned caltrops toward Qiao Feng's back.

And Xue Muhua sat at the head table from beginning to end, stroking his beard as though he were watching a play that had nothing to do with him.

My steel shield shattered at that moment.

Qiao Feng's palm struck with the force of a collapsing mountain.

The ancestral shield of the You family—supposedly impervious to blades and spears—split like a biscuit.

Fragments flew in every direction.

One cut across my cheek.

Warm blood blurred my vision.

“Where the shield stands, the man stands. When the shield falls, the man falls.”

Those had been my brother's words.

My brother looked at me.

It was a look I would relive in countless nights afterward.

Fear.

Relief.

And something almost childlike in its bewilderment.

As though he were asking:

*Brother, were we wrong?*

Then he raised his blade.

And cut his own throat.

Blood burst forth in the sunlight.

A brilliant flower that existed for only a moment.

I did not hesitate.

I raised my own blade.

The steel was cold against my neck.

And in that instant, I suddenly remembered the magnolia.

It should have been in full bloom by now.

White.

Innocent.

Entirely unaware of the bloody feast unfolding beneath its branches.

# Volume III: Thirty Years in the Mirror

## I. Another Gathering

When that letter from Jiangnan reached me, I had already lived among these ruins for thirty years.

Most of the manor had burned down.

I rebuilt only two small rooms in the eastern wing.

The weeds in the courtyard grew as high as a man's waist.

Wild foxes often appeared.

I never drove them away.

Sometimes I even left food for them.

There are few creatures in this world capable of accepting charity without asking why.

The letter had been carried by the son of an old acquaintance.

He wrote that a new merchant house had risen in Jiangnan, specializing in mechanical servants capable of sweeping and cleaning on their own. In only a few years, the company had become a leader in its field.

The year before, they had held a “Reunion Gathering” in Suzhou.

They had rented the vast “Tiangong Hall,” large enough for forty thousand people.

A famous speaker from the capital hosted the event.

A celebrated singer performed.

Most astonishing of all, the young owner had given nearly twenty thousand workers a tael of gold each as a reward.

And he intended to send ten outstanding workers to Antarctica—to see the glaciers and witness the aurora.

Antarctica.

I unfolded an old nautical map.

My finger began at Quanzhou Harbor.

South.

Then farther south.

Across the fever-ridden waters of Nanyang.

Through straits roaring with storms and monstrous waves.

At last my finger stopped upon a pure white continent marked:

**The End of the World.**

It was a place even the bravest merchants feared to approach.

And yet they intended to send people there.

Not as punishment.

As a reward.

I sat awake all night.

Two images kept appearing in my mind.

Thirty years ago, at Juxianzhuang:

More than three hundred men shouting,

**“Kill the Khitan dog!”**

Thirty years later, in Suzhou:

Forty thousand people singing of a shared future.

The same noise.

The same exhilaration.

The same spectacle of a “gathering of the realm.”

Why, then, had one ended in blood while the other began with song?

---

## II. Two Kinds of Gathering

I began to study the letter carefully.

It said the merchant's young master was barely thirty.

He had built everything from nothing.

He often told his workers:

“We are not masters and servants. We are family, traveling in the same boat.”

He said the Reunion Gathering was not meant to display wealth.

It was simply a way for workers scattered across the realm to meet one another.

“To see each other's faces. To speak with one another. To know that the others exist.”

Those seven words struck like a needle into the most rotten wound in my heart.

**To know that the others exist.**

When we gathered at Juxianzhuang, had we ever cared to know that the others existed?

We knew that Dan Zheng wanted to eliminate rivals.

We knew the Beggars’ Sect elders wanted to secure their positions.

We knew Xue Muhua wished to borrow the gathering's strength to resolve his own grievances.

We knew many others had come simply to make connections, so that one day they might ask the famous physician for help.

But we pretended not to know.

We did not need one another.

We needed numbers.

The more people there were, the greater the spectacle.

The greater the spectacle, the more unquestionable our “justice” appeared.

**Their gathering was meant to see one another.**

**Ours was meant to use one another.**

Their rewards were gold and journeys—things genuinely given.

Our reward was the empty prestige of “heroism”—something demanded rather than given.

Their singers sang of a world united as one family.

We raised our cups and swore never to reconcile.

That night, I touched the scar on my face.

Then I laughed.

The sound echoed through the empty ruins like the cry of an owl.

---

## III. Two Ways of Writing a Hero

All these years, people in the martial world have continued arguing about Qiao Feng.

Some say he was a true hero, a man who stood taller than heaven and earth.

At Juxianzhuang, he walked alone into the lion's den to save a young woman who had nothing to do with him.

At Yanmen Pass, he gave his own life to stop a war.

He carried the insult of being called a “Khitan barbarian” throughout his life, yet walked the most difficult road of *ren*—compassion:

to answer hatred with virtue,

and to redeem the world at the cost of his own life.

Others say that many of those who once attacked him at Juxianzhuang later became heroes themselves.

When Qiao Feng was imprisoned by the Liao emperor, Xuannan and Xuanji led warriors from the Central Plains into Nanjing to rescue him, risking their lives.

At last, they understood:

**Heroism is not measured by bloodline.**

**It is measured by the breadth of one's heart.**

Then what of me?

What was You Ji?

I remained here among the ruins, as though guarding an enormous grave.

Buried within it was my brother, You Ju.

Buried within it were the lives of more than three hundred men.

Most had been killed by Qiao Feng, it is true.

But whose account should those deaths belong to?

Buried within it was the century-old foundation of the You family.

And buried deepest of all was a man named You Ji—a man who once dreamed of becoming a hero.

I later heard fragments of what happened to my son, You Tan.

He wandered the martial world.

He suffered humiliation and abuse.

Eventually he fell into darkness and became an enemy of the martial world.

People cursed him as “a traitor who recognized the wrong father” and “a despicable wretch.”

But who remembered that he had been seven years old when, among the ruins of Juxianzhuang, he spent three days and three nights digging through the rubble?

All he found were two mutilated corpses.

His father.

And his uncle.

**The epic of a hero is always magnificent.**

**The tragedy of those left behind does not even deserve a footnote.**

# Volume IV: The Ruins Within

## I. The Swarm: When the Individual Disappears into the Crowd

It was not until I read a Western book called *The Psychology of the Crowd* that I finally found a name for the madness of thirty years ago.

It was called mass hypnosis.

Once an individual dissolves into a crowd, intelligence may decline sharply, emotion may become extreme, and moral restraint may collapse with frightening speed.

Inside the great hall of Juxianzhuang, there was no longer any “You Ji.”

No “Dan Zheng.”

No “Zhao Qiansun.”

There was only a creature called:

**the martial world of the Central Plains.**

A creature does not need to think.

It only needs to shout.

It does not need to distinguish.

It only needs to obey.

Zhao Qiansun was the clearest example.

Thirty years earlier, beyond Yanmen Pass, he had witnessed the Central Plains warriors mistakenly killing Qiao Feng's adoptive parents—a peaceful Khitan couple who had merely happened to pass through.

The horror of that mistake had driven him into becoming a “walking corpse.”

Yet thirty years later, at Juxianzhuang, he shouted “Kill!” louder than almost anyone.

Why?

Because back then he had been an individual.

He alone had to bear the burden of his conscience.

But now he belonged to a crowd.

The guilt of the crowd could be divided among everyone.

And when guilt is divided among hundreds, each individual begins to feel innocent.

I was no different.

When I sent out the Heroes’ Invitations, did I truly have no doubts?

Qiao Feng had always possessed an honorable reputation.

How could he become a monster overnight?

But when I saw the fever in my brother's eyes, when I saw Xue Muhua nod, when replies began arriving from every corner of the realm like snowflakes—

my doubts were swallowed by a larger wave.

*So many people believe it.*

*How could all of us be wrong?*

**The applause of the crowd is the finest lullaby for reason.**

---

## II. Distortion: When the Eyes See Only What They Want

The book also described another human weakness.

It called it a “cognitive prison.”

Once people become convinced of something, they instinctively search for evidence that supports their belief and avert their eyes from everything that contradicts it.

When Qiao Feng entered carrying A'Zhu, everyone saw the blood covering his body.

Everyone saw his exhaustion.

Everyone saw that the young woman's life hung by a thread.

Yet we chose to believe:

It was an act.

A deception.

A Khitan villain's trick.

When Qiao Feng drank the wine of farewell and told each former friend that their bond was over, that was the openness of a true hero.

We chose to see it as provocation.

Arrogance.

Defiance.

When the Swift Blade Qi Liu died clearly beneath the hands of three men, Xuannan still declared that the entire death was Qiao Feng's crime.

Why?

Because if we admitted that we too had struck him, then the banner of the “righteous army” would develop a hole.

The banner could not be allowed to tear.

So truth had to be twisted.

The most absurd part was martial arts itself.

We repeatedly called Qiao Feng a “Khitan barbarian,” yet he wielded the most orthodox form of Taizu Long Fist.

And Master Xuannan?

He used Indian Buddhist finger techniques.

Master Xuanji?

His signature style was Arhat Fist.

Both were imported traditions.

The distinction between Han and Khitan had become, in our hands, the easiest blade to wield—

and the most convenient cloth with which to cover our shame.

I often wonder now:

What if, when the first man drew his sword, I had shouted:

“Wait.”

What if, when Xue Muhua sneered, I had asked:

“Where is the evidence?”

What if, when my brother charged at Qiao Feng, I had seized his arm and said:

“Let us think again.”

But there was no “what if.”

In the boiling cauldron of the crowd, the first person to shout “Stop” becomes a traitor.

And the fate of a traitor is often worse than that of an enemy.

---

## III. Redemption: Among the Ashes of Illusion

If time could turn backward—

I have asked myself that question for thirty years.

On every sleepless night.

At every dawn when I coughed blood.

Every time my feet struck a rusted blade among these ruins.

If time could turn backward, I would write one more line on the Heroes’ Invitation:

**“Those who attend are requested to bring evidence.”**

When Qiao Feng entered the hall, I would give him a chair and a bowl of hot tea.

I would say:

“Chief Qiao, speak.”

Before the battle began, I would stand in front of the first raised sword and shout:

**“Are we here to distinguish right from wrong—or merely to overwhelm one man with numbers?”**

But I also know this:

Even if time truly turned backward, I would probably make the same choice.

Because the You Ji of those days was precisely the fool who could be blinded by glory, intoxicated by applause, and persuaded that:

**the majority must be right.**

Some truths cannot be understood until one has waded through blood.

The young master of that Jiangnan merchant house, they say, often tells his workers:

**“Dare to dream. Dare to act.”**

What a magnificent phrase.

We dreamed too.

We dreamed that Juxianzhuang would be remembered for generations.

We acted too.

We were willing to wager our fortunes, our families, our reputations, even our lives.

But we lacked the three things that mattered most:

**reverence for life,**

**humility before truth,**

**and tolerance for difference.**

Their gathering of forty thousand was meant to let people see one another's faces, hear one another's songs, and share tangible gold and dreams.

Our gathering of three hundred was meant to nail one man to a pillar of shame because of his ancestry, then water our withered vanity with his blood.

The snow stopped.

The eastern sky turned the pale blue-green of a crab shell.

I rose slowly and pushed open the creaking door.

The courtyard was still there.

And so was the magnolia.

Thirty years ago it had been white as snow.

Thirty years later its trunk was black and hard as iron.

Yet on its branches were several swollen buds, covered in fine hairs, glowing faintly in the dawn.

Beneath the tree, I dug a hole.

Into it I placed the two broken halves of the steel shield.

I erected no memorial.

I simply stamped the earth flat.

If someone in a later age happens to uncover the fragments, perhaps they will wonder what story they belong to.

That is enough.

**Some stories were never meant to be remembered.**

**They were meant to be buried.**

The final part of the letter said that the merchant house intended to make its mechanical servants available throughout the world.

What an audacious ambition.

What a distant horizon.

Juxianzhuang is gone.

Yet the words *Juxian*—“gathering the worthy”—remain.

Perhaps centuries from now, people will forget the brothers of the You family.

But they may remember this:

True “gathering of the worthy” is not about collecting talented people merely to decorate a stage.

It is about bringing intelligence together to create something.

Not gathering a crowd to gain courage.

But gathering hearts to build something greater than any individual could build alone.

I spent my entire life organizing one Heroes’ Gathering.

I destroyed a family.

I ruined a child.

And now, at the end of my life, perhaps understanding this one truth—

perhaps admitting before these ruins that **I was wrong**—

may itself be a kind of heroism.

Though it came too late.

Though it is too shabby to deserve the name.

Though it is fit only to be whispered to an old magnolia tree.

At last, the morning light crossed the broken wall and fell upon my face.

I narrowed my eyes.

Dust drifted through the light like the finest gold powder.

Thirty years ago, the morning at Juxianzhuang had looked exactly like this.

Back then, I believed the light had come for me.

Now I know better.

**Light is simply light.**

It shines upon heroes and fools.

Upon feasts and ruins.

It does not judge.

It simply exists.

Like time.

# Appendix: Thoughts from a Broken Manuscript

These pages were written intermittently from my sickbed.

The ink is poor.

The brush is worn.

The paper was torn from what remained of the mulberry sheets used to seal the windows.

I do not seek to be remembered.

I seek only peace of mind.

Recently I heard that the Jiangnan merchant house had made another move.

They intend to establish branches overseas and sell their self-sweeping mechanical servants to foreign lands.

Good.

Very good.

The “heroes” of Juxianzhuang once believed:

**“Those who are not of our people must harbor different intentions.”**

These people think instead:

**“Anyone beneath heaven may become a friend.”**

The age moves forward in the end.

Though its progress often requires the ruins of countless “Juxianzhuangs” to serve as its foundation.

When I die, do not erect a monument.

If anyone truly wishes to know what happened here, simply tell them:

**An old man once lived here who, in the final days of his life, finally learned to bow before the truth.**

The magnolia will bloom again.

This time, I shall watch it properly.

**—You Ji, Last Lord of Juxianzhuang**

**Winter, the Ninth Year of Yuanyou
Northern Song Dynasty
Written among the ruins of the old manor**

# Postscript

This manuscript was first written while I was ill and revised three days before my death.

I was foolish all my life.

Only in old age did I glimpse a fraction of the truth.

By then, regret had become useless.

I leave these words not to defend myself, but to those who, in generations to come, may gather people together in the name of “righteousness.”

In the midst of the noise, ask yourself three questions.

**First: Ask your heart.**
Is this act meant to benefit others—or yourself?

**Second: Ask your eyes.**
What you see—is it truth, or merely the illusion you wish to see?

**Third: Ask your actions.**
Are you planting the seeds of good—or sowing the seeds of harm?

If, after asking these three questions, your palms remain dry and your vision clear, perhaps you may proceed.

# Sources and Literary Inspirations

**Jin Yong. *The Demi-Gods and Semi-Devils* (revised edition). Ming Ho Publications, Hong Kong, 1978.**

The Heroes’ Gathering at Juxianzhuang is primarily derived from Chapters 21, *“A Dreamlike Journey Through a Thousand Miles,”* through Chapter 22, *“Eyes Bright as Stars.”* The events, characters, and selected dialogue in this story are creatively adapted from those episodes.

**Gustave Le Bon. *The Crowd: A Study of the Popular Mind*. 1895.**

Le Bon's analysis of crowd psychology—including emotional intensification, the weakening of rational judgment, and susceptibility to suggestion and manipulation—provides an important theoretical lens for understanding the behavior of the crowd at Juxianzhuang.

**Sigmund Freud. *Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego*. 1921.**

Freud's discussion of identification within groups, the reduction of individual restraint, and the release of aggression provides a deeper psychological framework for understanding collective behavior.

**Leon Festinger. *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*. 1957.**

Festinger's theory helps explain why the participants at Juxianzhuang ignored evidence inconsistent with their beliefs while rationalizing their own actions. In order to preserve the conviction that “we are righteous and Qiao Feng is evil,” they distorted reality rather than reconsidering their assumptions.

**Sima Qian. *Records of the Grand Historian: The Annals of Xiang Yu*. Western Han Dynasty.**

The literary structure of the Hongmen Banquet—where danger hides beneath ceremony and conflicting motives beneath apparent hospitality—provides an important classical reference for the narrative atmosphere of this work.

**Albert Camus. *The Stranger*. 1942.**

Meursault's condemnation and execution partly because he fails to conform to society's expectations resonates structurally with Qiao Feng's treatment as an outsider because of his Khitan identity. The parallel explores the fate of those marked as “different” by collective judgment.

**Historical records of factional struggles and “righteous criticism” in *Zizhi Tongjian*. Northern Song Dynasty.**

Accounts of political factionalism and persecution conducted in the name of righteousness provide a broader historical framework for examining the mechanisms of moral mobilization and collective hostility represented by the Juxianzhuang gathering.

**Zen Buddhist koans and the tradition of “the sudden strike.”**

The protagonist's late-life awakening amid the ruins draws in part upon the Zen tradition of direct insight into the nature of mind, particularly the possibility of sudden realization under extreme circumstances.

### Author's Note

The Jiangnan merchant house, the “Reunion Gathering,” the “Tiangong Hall,” and related events described in this story are fictional literary devices inspired by contemporary commercial phenomena. They are not intended to identify any specific real-world company or event.

The purpose of the story is to create a dialogue between the past and the present—to explore how gatherings, leadership, collective identity, ambition, and the language of “righteousness” can either unite people toward creation or mobilize them toward destruction.

The story is inspired in part by characters and events from Jin Yong's *The Demi-Gods and Semi-Devils*, combined with literary reflection on collective psychology, personal ambition, moral responsibility, and the passage of time.
