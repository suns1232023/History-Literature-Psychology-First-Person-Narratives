# Two Inches from the Red Line: The Tragic Game of Ding Chunqiu

**Keywords:** #Culture #Media

### Prologue: The Bell in the Cell

The bell of Shaolin Temple rang again.

Across three courtyards and seven stone walls, its sound reached me, muffled and heavy, like a fist pounding on worn leather—not painful, yet enough to make the heart uneasy. It was already dusk. Through the narrow iron bars of the stone window, the fading light cast a crooked grid upon the floor. Dust drifted slowly through the beam of light, rising and falling like the calculations of my life—ups and downs, victories and defeats, all destined eventually to settle.

Outside, snow was falling over the Central Plains. Dense, orderly, almost meticulous, it covered the temple roofs, ancient cypresses, and blue-gray flagstones.

This snow was different from the snow of Xingxiu Sea.

The snow there was wild, driven by fierce winds, freezing the moment it touched the ground, sharp enough to cut the throat.

The snow here at Shaolin was orderly.

Just like those bald monks.

Orderly to the bone. Orderly enough to be irritating.

I have been locked in this stone chamber behind the Discipline Hall for I no longer know how many days. Xu Zhu—that little monk, who should now properly be called the Abbot of Shaolin—sealed my qi center and destroyed my Energy-Absorbing Skill. Yet he left me my eyes, and this right hand that can still hold a brush.

He said:

“Mr. Ding, look upon the clear sky and daylight. Think about karma.”

Karma?

I, Ding Chunqiu, have never believed in karma.

I believe only in methods. In calculation. In the greed, fear, and delusion that stir deep within the human heart.

For fifty years I calculated without error. I calculated where martial arts manuals were hidden. I calculated the weaknesses of my enemies. I calculated the petty thoughts concealed beneath my disciples' endless flattery.

Yet in the end, I failed to calculate one thing:

**myself.**

Today, the little monk who brought me food no longer looks at me with the fear he had a month ago. There is only a thin layer of pity in his eyes.

That hurts more than hatred.

They no longer fear me.

The Star-Lord of Xingxiu, the man who once required everyone in the world to tremble and endlessly calculate his moods, is dead.

He died on Shaoshi Mountain, in the grand spectacle I staged myself.

He died on the stage I had built with my own hands—and then pushed over with those same hands.

The ink is yesterday's remainder. The brush is worn to a stump. The paper is nothing more than scraps left over from copying sutras.

So be it.

On this rough paper, I shall record some rougher truths.

I will not write about martial arts. I deceived others about that for an entire lifetime, and in the end, I nearly deceived myself.

I will write only about the game I played for half my life—

**and lost completely.**

---

# Chapter One: Fools and Giants

At first, I truly believed they were fools.

Xuanci of Shaolin wore compassion on his face and opened his mouth only to say things like, “Lay down the butcher's knife and become a Buddha.”

Ridiculous.

If you lay down the knife, someone else simply turns you into meat.

The beggars of the Beggars' Sect carried broken bowls in their hands while shouting the word “chivalry” loud enough to shake the heavens.

Then there were Kunlun, Kongtong, Qingcheng, and all the other orthodox schools.

They dressed themselves in magnificent armor, but inside, their abacuses were clicking furiously.

They were like giants clad in armor—tall, imposing, powerful enough to shake the earth with a single step.

But precisely because they were giants, they were slow to turn.

Precisely because they wore armor, they had too many things to consider.

Before taking a step, they had to look around.

Would they step on Shaolin's fields?

Would they offend the Beggars' Sect?

Would they obstruct someone's path?

My Xingxiu Sect was different.

We were few, but like a disease attached to the bone, like venomous snakes that followed their prey everywhere.

We did not need to obey their rules.

There were no rules in Xingxiu Sea.

We recognized only one principle:

**The strong are supreme, and flattery reaches the heavens.**

I understood that truth the day I left the Xiaoyao Sect.

My master, Wuyazi, was always talking about “the Dao of following nature.”

But what was the Dao?

What was nature?

Nothing more than a disguise for the weak.

The true Dao was survival.

The true nature of the world was the survival of the fittest.

And so I began playing my game.

I never wanted to overturn the board.

That would have been foolish.

What I wanted was to dance along the edge of the board, on that invisible yet universally understood boundary—

**the Red Line.**

What was the Red Line?

It was the boundary beyond which those giants would temporarily stop calculating among themselves and unite to crush me.

If I stayed too far away, my reputation as the Star-Lord would mean nothing. I would gain nothing worth having. I might as well remain in Xingxiu Sea and rule my little kingdom.

But if I crossed the line, I would be crushed.

Game over.

That was the beauty of it.

**Two inches.**

I wanted always to remain two inches outside the Red Line.

Close enough to make them sweat.

Far enough that they would hesitate before actually striking.

Within those two narrow inches, I could maneuver.

I could seize what I wanted.

I could extract everything I desired:

Martial arts.

Treasures.

Fame.

And above all—

**the pleasure of making them fear me, but only to the precise degree that served my purposes.**

---

# Chapter Two: The Value of Emotion

Every move I made was a calculation.

I stole the sword manual of the Dian Cang Sect and killed its leader.

The martial world erupted.

The Dian Cang Sect had friends everywhere. Outrage spread immediately.

That was when I released what I would later learn to call **“emotional value.”**

At the time, I simply called it strategy.

Sometimes it was humility.

I had my disciples spread the word:

“Old Master Ding and the former leader of Dian Cang were old friends who once discussed swordsmanship together. I merely took the manual for purposes of comparison and study. Dian Cang's martial arts are exquisite. I have always admired them.”

Look at that.

I had given them an escape route.

Old friends.

Friendly exchange.

Mutual admiration.

What respectable explanations.

And so, among the voices demanding revenge, hesitation began to appear:

“Ding Chunqiu may be evil, but he is still part of the martial world. If fellow practitioners exchange techniques, life and death are their own affair…”

Sometimes I used intimidation.

If humility was insufficient, I would personally act.

I planted the “Three Laughs Free-and-Easy Powder” in three or five orthodox fighters.

It was not immediately lethal.

It merely made them laugh.

Laugh until their strength disappeared.

Laugh until they begged for death but could not die.

Then I appeared and sighed:

“Only I possess the antidote. If you gentlemen are willing to turn hostility into peace, I shall gladly provide it.”

They became afraid.

Afraid of that inexplicable laughter.

Afraid of the agony of being unable to live or die.

And so anger became calculation.

For the sake of a single sword manual, was it worth gambling with the lives of dozens of disciples?

Sometimes, I even offered what looked like **kindness**.

One year, an elder of the Beggars' Sect was tracking one of my disciples. He was getting dangerously close to Xingxiu Sea.

I personally rode out a hundred li to receive him.

I hosted a banquet.

After three rounds of wine, I raised my cup.

“I too mourn the fate of your former leader, Xiao Feng. When a hero falls, the entire martial world grieves. Today, after meeting you, Elder, I feel as though we have known each other for years. The Xingxiu Sect is willing to establish eternal friendship with the Beggars' Sect.”

The elder froze for a moment.

The murderous intent in his face faded by a third.

Xiao Feng was an old wound in his heart.

My words had struck precisely where I wanted them to.

I called all these things **bait**.

I threw it out.

The angry giants would lower their heads, sniff it, and hesitate.

And that moment of hesitation was enough.

Shaolin would think:

“Though he belongs to an evil sect, perhaps there is some reason in what he says. Must every dispute in the martial world end in extermination?”

The Beggars' Sect would think:

“Xingxiu's poison techniques are strange and deadly. A direct confrontation could cost us dearly. Perhaps we should wait.”

The smaller sects were even more pragmatic:

“The tallest beam attracts the rot first. Let someone else fight.”

And so the tide withdrew again and again.

Every retreat carved another mark into my heart.

See?

They really were fools.

They could be calmed by words.

Intimidated by fear.

Bought with a little counterfeit kindness.

I sat like a fisherman on the shore, watching fish fight over bait in the water, and I thought:

**This martial world is nothing special.**

---

# Chapter Three: The Echo of Self-Validation

I even built a palace around this belief in their stupidity.

Its foundations were made of my repeated successes.

I killed Xuannan and called it “cleansing the Xiaoyao Sect of its internal corruption.”

The martial world erupted.

But arguments broke out within Shaolin first:

Why had Xuannan been connected to the Xingxiu Sect?

Was this really something Shaolin had the right to interfere with?

The argument lasted three months.

Nothing came of it.

When Su Xinghe died, I said:

“It was merely a contest between fellow disciples. Life and death are determined by heaven.”

Others sighed:

“The internal affairs of the Xiaoyao Sect are not ours to judge. Outsiders have no legitimate grounds to interfere.”

When I captured Azi and seized the Divine Wood Cauldron, I was even more righteous:

“She is a traitor to the Xingxiu Sect. The Divine Wood Cauldron is our sect's sacred treasure.”

Every time, the martial world's response confirmed my judgment.

Every time, I drew nourishment from that confirmation.

And the palace in my mind became more magnificent.

Its central doctrine was my theory of **the foolishness of others**:

The orthodox schools valued reputation more than real interests.

They followed rules and therefore lost initiative.

They fought among themselves.

They were strong on the outside but weak within.

My bait—threats, reassurance, counterfeit kindness—was like throwing small fish to a flock of cormorants.

They fought over the bait and forgot who the fisherman really was.

I lived inside that palace for too long.

So long that I began to believe it was the whole world.

The wind and rain outside the palace sounded like applause for my theory.

My disciples' cries—

“Star-Lord Xingxiu! Boundless is your power! Unstoppable are you!”

—were lamps that never went out.

They illuminated my palace.

They also blinded me.

Because I forgot one thing:

**the palace had been built on sand.**

And the sand was shifting sand.

Every “success” removed another piece of its foundation.

Every person I killed.

Every treasure I stole.

Every poison I planted.

Every conflict I provoked.

None of these things were imaginary.

They were thorns driven into the enormous body of the martial world.

At first, each wound was small.

It could be ignored.

Explained away.

Suppressed in the name of the “greater good.”

But the thorns accumulated.

They went deeper.

Until one day, the body would suddenly realize:

The pain was not in one place.

**The pain was everywhere.**

---

# Chapter Four: The Taste of Saturation

But I forgot something.

People get full on bait.

No.

Not full.

**Sick of it.**

Disgusted.

When you repeatedly cook the same lie with the same seasoning, the first thing to rebel is not the tongue.

It is the stomach.

The people of the Central Plains martial world may have been slow.

They may have been fragmented.

But they were not stupid.

Especially once the pain became real.

Blood.

Bodies.

Loss.

When Xuannan died, Shaolin was enraged.

I offered them the excuse of “cleansing the sect.”

They hesitated, then stepped onto the platform I had given them.

When Su Xinghe died, the martial world erupted.

I offered the explanation of “a contest between fellow disciples.”

They debated.

Some believed it.

When Azi was captured and the Divine Wood Cauldron taken, they shook their heads and sighed:

“Internal fighting within an evil sect. Let them settle it themselves.”

Every time, I carefully controlled the dosage.

The dosage of poison.

The dosage of bait.

I believed I had discovered the perfect formula.

I believed I could play this delicate balance forever.

But the pain was real.

The death of Xuannan meant Shaolin had lost a great monk.

One voice was missing from its Buddhist debates.

One more sigh had entered the sound of its morning and evening bells.

The death of Su Xinghe meant the martial world had lost an extraordinary man.

The Zhenlong chess game had become an echo that would never return.

Young people who once traveled great distances to seek his guidance would never have that opportunity again.

And the Xingxiu disciples who roamed the martial world, bullying men and violating women, left behind countless ruined households and countless families soaked in blood and grief.

All of these were debts I had accumulated.

And what had I paid in return as “emotional value”?

A few weightless words.

Masks that could be torn off at any moment.

Toasts at banquets.

A promise to “stand together” when danger came.

When the debt became so enormous that it blocked out the sky, while my payments remained nothing more than painted promises—

even a fool would eventually wake up.

There had already been signs before Shaoshi Mountain.

Their eyes had changed.

The monks of Shaolin no longer looked at me with confusion or hesitation.

Their eyes had become heavy, like clouds filled with rain.

The Beggars' disciples gripped their staffs so tightly that their knuckles turned white and veins stood out on their hands.

Even the smaller sects that had always sat on the fence were exchanging glances.

There was no fear in those glances.

Only cold determination.

But I chose not to see.

I lived inside my magnificent palace.

I listened to my disciples chant my praises.

I believed the “truth” I wanted to believe.

I believed they were still the same fools.

Still the same scattered sand.

Still the same rabble that I could defeat with a few words and a little poison.

---

# Chapter Five: The Final Bet

The Heroes' Assembly at Shaoshi Mountain was the greatest—and final—bet of my life.

I held You Tanzhi hostage, intending to use my poison arts to control the heroes of the world.

The mountain wind howled.

Thousands watched.

I surveyed the people below.

They were still arguing.

Still calculating.

Murong Fu's eyes were full of schemes.

Duan Yu looked bewildered.

The Shaolin monks were solemn.

The Beggars' Sect clenched their teeth.

I thought:

**I've won.**

It was still the same.

All I needed was one more push.

Display a little more of my newly perfected Corpse-Corroding Skill.

Offer a few empty promises to a few sects.

The same old tricks.

The same scattered sand.

They would make way for me.

I was wrong.

Terribly wrong.

When I pushed my poisonous palm toward You Tanzhi's heart and laughed:

“Today, the heroes of the world shall witness the ultimate arts of the Xingxiu Sect!”

—I suddenly saw it.

Their eyes had changed.

The Shaolin monks' solemnity had become stillness.

Not the stillness of hesitation.

The stillness of a thousand tons of stone moments before they collapse.

The eyes of the Beggars' disciples changed from clenched anger into flames.

Not flickering flames.

Lava.

Molten fire surging from the depths of the earth, ready to burn everything away.

Even the leaders of the small sects that had always hidden behind others stepped forward.

Hands rested on sword hilts.

There was no fear on their faces.

No calculation.

Only one thing:

**consensus.**

Everything I had said over the years—

the humility,

the intimidation,

the counterfeit kindness—

disappeared in that instant like snow beneath the sun.

Their ears seemed to close.

My voice became meaningless noise.

Their eyes, however, opened wider.

They saw me.

And they saw the Red Line behind me.

The line had become blurred over the years.

But it had never disappeared.

Xu Zhu's attack was merely the beginning.

He did not represent himself.

He did not even represent Shaolin alone.

He represented the entire martial world of the Central Plains.

For decades, I had provoked that enormous machine.

Stimulated it.

Hurt it.

Forced it to adapt through pain.

And finally—

**click.**

The final gear engaged.

The machine roared to life.

My “success.”

My “calculations.”

My “control.”

All of it was crushed into dust beneath its wheels.

---

# Chapter Six: The Truth About the Red Line

Now, sitting in this stone chamber and listening to the bells, I finally understand.

I did not lose because Xu Zhu's martial arts were superior.

That was merely the ending.

Not the cause.

I lost because the martial world's attitude toward me changed.

From:

“Scattered resistance. Easily divided. Easily reassured.”

Into:

**“Unified, determined, uncompromising destruction.”**

When the Shaolin monks ignored their Buddhist prohibition against killing;

when the Beggars' disciples abandoned their hesitation over harming a hostage held by the enemy;

when even Murong Fu temporarily put aside his ambition to restore his kingdom;

when even Duan Yu set aside his compassion;

when all of them—

**all of them—**

looked at me with the same determination,

the palace of my “theory of stupidity” began collapsing from its foundations.

Their terrifying unity told me:

**We have had enough of your tricks.**

**We are sick of your bait.**

**And we will no longer allow you to dance along our Red Line.**

This was the key variable in my cognitive collapse.

It was not the martial power of any single person.

It was not the severity of any single confrontation.

It was the moment when their collective tolerance finally crossed a threshold.

They formed a consensus that I could neither divide, nor soothe, nor intimidate.

And that threshold had been reached long before.

I was simply blind.

I could not see it.

Their trust in the “emotional value” I offered had already been eroded piece by piece.

Every time I committed another atrocity and paid only with a few empty words, trust became thinner.

Every time the harm I caused became concrete and bloody while my apologies and explanations remained weightless as feathers, another crack appeared.

Shaoshi Mountain was merely the moment when the crack spread across the entire wall and the wall finally collapsed.

They had not suddenly become smarter.

**Their patience had simply reached its limit.**

Like a string pulled too tight, it must break.

Like water filled beyond capacity, it must overflow.

---

# Chapter Seven: The Echo

Looking back at my own Xingxiu Sect, was it not the same?

“Star-Lord Xingxiu! Boundless is your power! You attack without defeat! You fight without losing!”

I had listened to those chants for thirty years.

At first, they made me proud.

Then they became habit.

Finally, I believed them completely.

That too was emotional value.

I fed it to my disciples.

My disciples fed it back to me.

We nourished one another and lived inside a man-made bubble of golden light.

Until the day the bubble burst.

And the first thing to turn against me was precisely those disciples whose voices had once praised me the loudest.

That was the game I played.

I played it with outsiders.

I played it with insiders.

And most of all—

**I played it with myself.**

I thought I was the player.

The one holding the pieces.

The one smiling while watching the winds of the world change.

But I never realized that I too was a piece.

A piece being pushed toward death by a larger game, by the laws buried deep within human nature—laws I believed I controlled, but had never truly understood.

Outside the window, snow was still falling.

The bell rang again.

This time it was the evening bell.

One strike.

Then another.

Slow.

Heavy.

Like the footsteps of a giant upon the spine of time.

I once thought that bell represented restraint.

Stagnation.

Obsolete rules.

Now I hear something else in it.

A quiet strength.

Unhurried.

Unchanged by circumstance.

Unaffected by wind or rain.

It simply remains there.

Day after day.

Year after year.

Marking the hours.

Measuring consequence.

In its presence, my noisy, frantic, calculating life suddenly seems almost absurd.

Like the performance of a fool.

I lost this game.

I lost it completely.

Not to martial arts.

I lost to the very laws I had mocked, yet which had always governed this world:

**Falsehood cannot remain true forever.**

**Debts must eventually be paid.**

**Human hearts are made of flesh.**

**And when endurance reaches its limit, there is no longer any need to endure.**

The Red Line had always been there.

I thought I was dancing above it.

Dancing dangerously.

Dancing brilliantly.

Dancing before an audience of thousands.

In reality, I had been piling firewood beneath it all along.

Every success.

Every theft.

Every person I hurt.

One more piece of wood.

Until the pile towered into the sky.

And I was still admiring the elegance of my dance.

In the end, the one who lit the pile was not my enemy.

It was myself.

Those greedy hands of mine had never stopped moving.

The bell stopped.

Its final echo slowly disappeared into the snowy night.

I put down the brush.

The ink had dried.

The night was deep.

The winds of Xingxiu Sea would never touch my face again.

But the sand carried by those winds still seems to remain in my eyes.

Grinding.

Burning.

Reminding me that I once believed I could calculate everything.

And failed to calculate the one thing that mattered most.

**The game itself.**

**—End—**
