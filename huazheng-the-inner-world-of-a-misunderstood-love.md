# The Inner World of Princess Huazheng

## *A Song Across the Gobi, a Life Misread*

### A Psychological Self-Examination of Love, Attachment, Obsession, and Letting Go

> *If a fictional character could write down the truth of her own life, what would she say?*

**A first-person psychological reconstruction inspired by Jin Yong's *The Legend of the Condor Heroes***

---

## Author's Note

I have encountered many people who became trapped in love.

Some were young professionals. Others were senior executives. Their circumstances were completely different, yet their emotional patterns were surprisingly similar:

They knew that the person they loved belonged to someone else, yet they continued to pursue them.

They gave everything they had, only to be left wounded.

Whenever I encounter such a pattern, I think of **Princess Huazheng**, a character created by Jin Yong in *The Legend of the Condor Heroes*.

Huazheng is often underestimated.

She is usually remembered simply as **Guo Jing's childhood companion** or **Huang Rong's romantic rival**. In many interpretations, she becomes little more than a narrative obstacle between the two protagonists.

But if we look more carefully at her inner world, a much more complicated person emerges.

She is the beloved daughter of Genghis Khan.

She is a princess of the Mongolian steppe.

She is proud, strong-willed, and accustomed to being protected.

And yet, beneath all of that, she is also an ordinary young woman who gradually loses herself in love.

Her story can be read as a psychological journey through:

* attachment and dependency;
* projection and idealization;
* self-worth and identity;
* fear of abandonment;
* obsessive attachment;
* catastrophic attempts to preserve a relationship;
* guilt and moral injury;
* and, ultimately, psychological growth through letting go.

This essay reconstructs Huazheng's life in the form of a first-person diary.

The narrative begins with the childhood moment when a young Guo Jing saves her from a leopard. It follows her growing attachment to him, her belief that he secretly loves her, her discovery of Huang Rong, the collapse of that belief, and finally her decision to leave the homeland forever.

The purpose is not to claim that Huazheng literally thought these thoughts.

Rather, this is a **literary thought experiment**:

> What might Huazheng's inner world have looked like if she had possessed the language of modern psychology?

The historical and literary events are drawn from Jin Yong's fictional universe and related interpretations. The psychological analysis is a modern interpretive framework rather than a clinical diagnosis of a fictional character.

---

# Chapter I

## The Childhood Imprint — The Leopard and the Boy

**Time:** The Southern Song period, a quiet evening on the Mongolian steppe
**Voice:** Huazheng

My mother told me that I was almost eaten by a leopard when I was four.

I don't remember everything.

But somehow, the image has remained inside me.

Sometimes it returns in dreams.

Tolui's older cousin Du Shi was always cruel to me and my brother Tolui. That day, relying on his family's power, he released a hunting leopard.

The animal came straight at me.

I was so frightened that I could not even cry.

Everything disappeared except the smell of the animal and the sound of its breathing.

Then someone picked me up.

It was Guo Jing.

He was only a little older than me—a Han Chinese boy whom the other children often called stupid.

He grabbed me and rolled across the ground.

The leopard's claws struck his shoulder.

For a moment, I thought we were both going to die.

But somehow he managed to wound the animal with his short knife and carry me away.

I remember very little afterward.

Only that he was bleeding.

And he was smiling at me.

> "Don't be afraid. It's all right now."

What can a child understand at that age?

Not much.

But from that day onward, my eyes seemed to follow him wherever he went.

Today, I might describe it differently.

Perhaps it was an **attachment imprint**.

In psychology, early experiences of danger and rescue can become deeply associated with particular people. The person who provides safety may gradually become psychologically linked with the feeling of safety itself.

I was rescued at a moment when I believed I was going to die.

And somehow, Guo Jing's name became connected with the word **safe**.

The tragedy is that I did not yet understand something important:

> Gratitude and dependency can sometimes be mistaken for love.

---

# Chapter II

## The Boy Who Wasn't Stupid — He Was Simply Uncomplicated

**Time:** The Southern Song period, spring
**Voice:** Huazheng

The children of the steppe grew up.

Tolui became more and more like a warrior.

Jebe's archery became legendary.

And Guo Jing remained Guo Jing.

Slow.

Quiet.

Awkward.

Always practicing.

I often went to see him.

Not really to play.

I simply wanted to be near him.

He never seemed particularly grateful.

> "I'm practicing. Don't bother me."

> "I'm training with my teacher. Go away."

> "Stop distracting me. I don't have time."

If anyone else had spoken to the princess like that, I would have been furious.

But somehow, when Guo Jing did it, I would become angry—and then return a few days later.

Sometimes I wondered:

Why should a princess have to keep chasing after someone?

But I also loved watching him practice.

At sunset, the light would fall across his face. Sweat would run down his forehead. His eyes became completely focused.

It was as if the entire world disappeared.

I heard what other people said about him.

They called him slow.

They called him foolish.

They doubted whether he was worthy of Jebe's teaching.

But I never thought he was stupid.

He was simply **clean**.

There was very little calculation in him.

He said what he thought.

He did what he believed was right.

And once he committed himself to something, he pursued it with extraordinary persistence.

People on the steppe admired clever warriors.

They admired people who could manipulate situations, speak persuasively, and outsmart their opponents.

Yet I found myself drawn to Guo Jing's simplicity.

His foolishness was real.

And so was his goodness.

Perhaps psychology would call this **projection**.

I was seeing in him something I wanted but did not possess myself:

The ability to remain certain of who you are without constantly worrying about how others see you.

---

# Chapter III

## The Archery Contest — The Moment I Thought He Loved Me

**Time:** The Southern Song period, autumn
**Voice:** Huazheng

Something extraordinary happened today.

My father held an archery contest.

Guo Jing shot down two eagles with a single arrow.

Everyone was astonished.

My father was delighted and offered him a reward.

"Ask for anything."

Do you know what that foolish boy said?

He refused.

He said my father had already been kind enough to him and that his mother had everything she needed.

My father laughed.

He told Guo Jing not to be afraid and to ask for whatever he wanted.

Then Guo Jing knelt down.

He asked my father for something.

My heart began beating faster.

What would he ask for?

Then I heard my name.

He asked my father not to marry me to Du Shi.

He said Du Shi was cruel and that I would suffer if I were forced to marry him.

At that moment, I thought the entire steppe had suddenly burst into flowers.

So he cared about me.

He had been thinking about me.

Even that seemingly foolish boy had been quietly watching over me.

My father did not agree.

Of course he didn't.

Political alliances mattered.

But from that day forward, I made a decision:

**I would marry Guo Jing.**

Looking back, I think that was perhaps the happiest moment of my life.

Not because someone had rescued me.

Not because someone had flattered me.

But because I believed that I had finally been chosen.

I was wrong.

He had asked my father not to marry me to Du Shi because he thought it was cruel.

Not necessarily because he loved me.

Had another woman been in the same danger, he might have done exactly the same thing.

But I did not understand that.

More importantly, I did not want to understand it.

Human beings have a remarkable tendency to believe the interpretation that best satisfies their emotional needs.

We do not merely perceive reality.

Sometimes, we construct the reality we desperately want to be true.

---

# Chapter IV

## The Golden Saber — Happiness Falling From the Sky

**Time:** The Southern Song period, summer
**Voice:** Huazheng

Du Shi's marriage arrangement disappeared after Wang Khan's defeat.

Then my father announced that I would marry Guo Jing.

I thought I was dreaming.

I watched Guo Jing kneel before my father and receive the golden saber that symbolized his status as the future son-in-law.

I almost cried.

All those years of waiting had finally produced an answer.

But then I noticed something.

Why did Guo Jing look so confused?

The novel tells us explicitly that Guo Jing had always regarded Huazheng almost like a younger sister. He had devoted himself to martial arts and had never developed romantic feelings toward her.

I did not know that.

I only saw him accept the arrangement.

So I assumed he wanted it.

Afterward, I began preparing myself to become his wife.

He told me that he had to go south.

He wanted to find Yang Kang.

He said he would return.

I didn't care how long it took.

I had waited for him for years.

I could wait a little longer.

When he left, however, he was stiff and distant.

There was no romantic tenderness in his farewell.

I became angry.

I raised my riding crop and struck my horse repeatedly until blood appeared.

I was not really angry with the horse.

I was angry with him.

Why couldn't he say something kind?

Why couldn't he show me that he was happy?

I reassured myself:

> He is simply not good at expressing his feelings.

For years, that explanation protected me.

Much later, I learned a painful truth:

Sometimes a person does not say what you want them to say because they are emotionally inarticulate.

But sometimes—

> **they simply do not feel it.**

---

# Chapter V

## The Woman From Jiangnan — The First Time I Became Afraid

**Time:** The Southern Song period, winter
**Voice:** Huazheng

Tolui's army moved south.

I came with them.

At last, I would see Guo Jing again.

All along the journey, I wondered whether he would be happy to see me.

He was there.

And I was happy.

But then I noticed someone standing beside him.

A woman from Jiangnan.

Her name was Huang Rong.

She was beautiful.

Her eyes seemed to speak.

When she smiled, she reminded me of spring.

And when she spoke, Guo Jing looked at her.

His eyes frightened me.

I had never seen him look at anyone like that.

When he looked at me, his eyes were always clear.

Protective.

Familiar.

Almost like those of an older brother looking at his younger sister.

I had never questioned it.

Until I saw him look at her.

When Huang Rong spoke, he listened.

When she laughed, he smiled.

When she was in danger, he became anxious.

It was different.

I could not explain how.

But I knew it was different.

She looked at me and described me as strong, broad-shouldered, proud, and heroic.

It sounded like praise.

Yet I somehow felt compared.

She was a swallow beneath the willows of Jiangnan.

I was a white eagle of the steppe.

Suddenly, I wished I were a swallow.

This was perhaps the first time I experienced something psychologists might describe as **threatened self-concept**.

My identity had always been stable.

I was a princess.

I was strong.

I was beautiful in the way the steppe valued beauty.

I was important.

But none of that guaranteed that I would be loved by the particular person I wanted.

And that was terrifying.

---

# Chapter VI

## A Thousand Miles to Find Him — Only to Hear That He Was Dead

**Time:** The Southern Song period, late autumn
**Voice:** Huazheng

I could not find him.

Then they told me he was dead.

Yang Kang said it.

He said Guo Jing had died.

My world collapsed.

I don't remember how I returned to the steppe.

I remember only one thought:

**He is dead.**

The boy who saved me from the leopard.

The boy who stood before my father.

The man who had become my promised husband.

Gone.

I wore mourning clothes.

For a long time.

At night, I remembered everything.

The leopard.

His arms around me.

The archery contest.

His request to my father.

The day he became the Golden Saber Prince.

Those memories had once made me happy.

Now they became unbearable.

At least, I thought, he was gone.

Then I learned the truth.

He wasn't dead.

He was alive.

And he was with Huang Rong.

I didn't know whether to feel relieved or devastated.

He was alive.

But he was not mine.

---

# Chapter VII

## The Last Attempt to Hold Him — When Love Became Harm

**Time:** The Southern Song period, spring
**Voice:** Huazheng

My father was preparing for another campaign.

One evening, I saw Guo Jing and his mother preparing to leave.

They were going away.

Without telling me.

My mind went blank.

Where was he going?

Was he going to find Huang Rong?

What about our marriage?

Wasn't I his fiancée?

I did not know what to do.

I only knew one thing:

**I could not let him leave.**

So I went to my father.

I told him that Guo Jing and his mother had discovered the secret message and were planning to escape.

I did not intend for them to die.

I only wanted them to stay.

I thought my father would simply stop them.

I was wrong.

I had forgotten the nature of power.

I had forgotten that revealing a military secret could be treated as a grave offense.

I had forgotten that, in the logic of an authoritarian political system, individual lives could become expendable.

Guo Jing's mother died.

Because of my actions, she died in front of her son.

I will never forget his expression.

There was no hatred in his eyes.

Somehow, that was worse.

Hatred would have meant that I still mattered to him.

But there was only grief.

And distance.

I had destroyed the last possibility between us.

Later, I wrote:

> *"I caused your mother to die. I am ashamed to face you again."*

I could write those words a thousand times.

It would not bring her back.

It would not restore the boy who once rescued me.

Modern psychology has a useful concept here:

**maladaptive attachment behavior**.

When a person experiences an overwhelming fear of abandonment, the attempt to preserve the relationship can become increasingly irrational.

The person tries harder.

Controls more.

Demands reassurance.

Attempts to prevent separation.

And eventually, the very behavior intended to preserve the relationship destroys it.

That is what happened to me.

I believed I was fighting for love.

But I was also trying to possess another human being.

I wanted him to stay.

I never stopped to ask whether he wanted to stay.

That was my mistake.

---

# Chapter VIII

## Farewell — I Finally Understood, Too Late

**Time:** The Southern Song period, summer
**Voice:** Huazheng

The army was moving south.

They were heading toward Xiangyang.

Toward the place Guo Jing would eventually devote himself to defending.

I knew what I had to do.

I sent him a message by white eagle.

I warned him that the Mongolian army was preparing to attack.

It was the last thing I could do for him.

At the end of the message, I wrote:

> *"I shall go west to seek refuge with my elder brother, and I shall never set foot in my homeland again."*

I was leaving.

I would go west, toward the lands of my brother Jochi.

It was far away.

Far enough, perhaps, to begin again.

Some people might ask:

Why not remain with Tolui?

He was my brother.

I loved him.

I would not have been alone.

But I could not stay.

The steppe contained too many memories.

Every horizon reminded me of Guo Jing.

The boy.

The leopard.

The archery contest.

The golden saber.

The life I had imagined.

Those memories would have destroyed me.

So I chose distance.

> Some places are too painful to return to.

> Some people must never be seen again.

> Some stories must finally be allowed to end.

---

# Chapter IX

## The Song of the Steppe — What Psychology Taught Me

**Time:** Autumn, somewhere on the road west
**Voice:** Huazheng

The road west is long.

There is too much time to think.

And I have been thinking about Guo Jing.

I finally understand several things.

### 1. I loved the person I imagined, not entirely the person he was.

I loved the boy who rescued me.

The young man who defended me.

The pure and honest soul who seemed untouched by calculation.

That person was real.

But my interpretation of his feelings was not.

He cared about me.

He respected me.

He wanted to protect me.

But he did not love me as a romantic partner.

That was not his fault.

Love cannot be negotiated.

It cannot be commanded.

It cannot be exchanged for loyalty.

And it cannot be manufactured by waiting long enough.

---

### 2. My identity as a princess became part of the problem.

I grew up surrounded by privilege.

My father loved me.

My brothers protected me.

People on the steppe obeyed me.

I rarely encountered an absolute "no."

Eventually, I developed a dangerous assumption:

> If I want something badly enough, perhaps I can obtain it.

But Guo Jing was not a possession.

He was not a political prize.

He was not something that could be secured through status.

A person accustomed to having their needs satisfied may find rejection extraordinarily difficult to tolerate.

I learned the meaning of the word **no** only after losing the person I wanted most.

---

### 3. Real love means allowing another person to remain themselves.

I once believed love meant keeping someone beside you.

I believed that sacrifice deserved repayment.

That waiting deserved an answer.

That devotion should never be rejected.

I was wrong.

Guo Jing wanted to protect the Song people.

He wanted to follow his moral principles.

He wanted to be with Huang Rong.

He wanted to become the person he believed he ought to be.

I could not love him and simultaneously demand that he become someone else.

Perhaps the purest form of love I could offer him was not possession.

It was freedom.

> I could not keep him.

> I could only let him go.

---

### 4. My value cannot depend on another person's love.

Leaving home was painful.

But after the pain began to subside, I started asking a question I had never seriously asked before:

**Who am I?**

I am not merely Guo Jing's fiancée.

I am not merely Genghis Khan's daughter.

I am Huazheng.

I am a woman who can ride.

A woman who can shoot.

A woman who knows the wind of the steppe.

A woman who has made terrible mistakes.

And a woman who can still choose what to do next.

My path may be lonely.

But at least it will be mine.

---

# Epilogue

## A Letter to Guo Jing

*Sent by white eagle toward Xiangyang.*

> My father is preparing to attack the Song. Although he knows that you have returned south, he will not abandon his intention to invade. Knowing how devoted you are to the defense of your country, I risked my life to warn you.
>
> I caused your mother's death. I am ashamed to face you again.
>
> I shall go west to seek refuge with my elder brother. I will never return to my homeland.
>
> There is a saying: however strong a camel may be, it cannot carry the burdens of a thousand men. To bear an impossible burden alone, even at the cost of one's life, accomplishes nothing.
>
> Take good care of yourself.
>
> May you live a long and peaceful life.
>
> **Huazheng**

---

## The Last Page

I cried while writing this letter.

But when I finished it, I smiled.

I cried for the little girl who fell in love with you when she was barely old enough to understand what love meant.

She waited for so many years.

She gave so much.

And in the end, she received nothing she had imagined.

But I smiled because I finally understood something.

I can let you go.

Your mother's death is the greatest guilt I will carry.

I do not ask you to forgive me.

But I hope that one day you will understand that I never intended to destroy your family.

I only wanted you to stay.

I simply did not understand that loving someone so desperately can make you lose your judgment—and hurt everyone around you.

I am leaving now.

I am going west.

Far away.

I will not return.

Not because I hate you.

Perhaps precisely because I loved you too much.

I do not want to see you struggling between me and Huang Rong.

I do not want you to remember your mother's death whenever you see my face.

You have already made your choice.

I do not blame you.

She understands you.

She can travel the world with you.

She can make you laugh.

She belongs beside you.

And I—

I belong to the steppe.

For years, I waited for someone who would never return.

So I will stop waiting.

The wind of the steppe is strong.

It has made my eyes hurt for years.

Perhaps somewhere else the wind will be gentler.

Perhaps then my eyes will stop filling with tears.

Take care, Guo Jing.

**Huazheng**

*End.*

# Psychological Themes

This literary reconstruction explores several psychological concepts:

| Theme                               | Interpretation                                                                                       |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Attachment**                      | Early rescue becomes associated with safety and emotional security.                                  |
| **Projection**                      | Huazheng attributes her own desired qualities and expectations to Guo Jing.                          |
| **Idealization**                    | The childhood hero becomes an increasingly idealized object of attachment.                           |
| **Fear of abandonment**             | The possibility of losing Guo Jing becomes psychologically intolerable.                              |
| **Threatened identity**             | Huang Rong's appearance and personality challenge Huazheng's assumptions about her own desirability. |
| **Maladaptive attachment behavior** | Attempts to prevent separation ultimately contribute to catastrophic consequences.                   |
| **Moral injury**                    | Huazheng must confront her role in the death of Guo Jing's mother.                                   |
| **Post-traumatic growth**           | Pain eventually leads her toward self-reflection and a reconstructed identity.                       |
| **Differentiation of self**         | She gradually separates her identity from her relationship with Guo Jing.                            |
| **Letting go**                      | Emotional maturity emerges when she accepts another person's autonomy.                               |

# Methodological Note

This work should be read as **literary-psychological interpretation**, not as historical documentation or clinical assessment.

Three layers are deliberately distinguished:

1. **Literary canon** — events and characters originating in Jin Yong's *The Legend of the Condor Heroes*.
2. **Narrative reconstruction** — imagined first-person thoughts, diary entries, and emotional dialogue.
3. **Psychological interpretation** — modern concepts used to examine attachment, identity, projection, fear of abandonment, guilt, and personal growth.

The psychological terminology is therefore **interpretive rather than diagnostic**.

The purpose is not to diagnose Huazheng, nor to claim that Jin Yong consciously constructed her according to modern psychological theories.

Instead, psychology is used as a lens through which a literary character can be examined from another angle.

---

# Why Huazheng Matters

Huazheng is interesting precisely because she is neither simply a villain nor simply a victim.

She loves.

She makes mistakes.

She becomes possessive.

She causes harm.

She experiences guilt.

And eventually, she learns that loving someone does not give her ownership of that person's life.

Her tragedy is therefore not simply:

> *"She loved the wrong man."*

It is something more universal:

> **She confused being needed with being loved, attachment with intimacy, persistence with devotion, and possession with love.**

That distinction is not limited to fictional characters.

It appears in ordinary human relationships every day.

Perhaps that is why Huazheng's story continues to feel painfully familiar.

---

## A Final Question

If Huazheng had understood psychology when she was sixteen, would her life have been different?

Perhaps.

But perhaps not.

Knowledge does not automatically defeat attachment.

Understanding does not automatically defeat fear.

And knowing that a relationship is unhealthy does not necessarily make it easier to leave.

Human beings are not purely rational decision-makers.

Sometimes we understand exactly what we are doing—

and still cannot stop.

That may be the most human part of Huazheng's story.
