# **Empress Dowager Longyu: The Final Volume**

### *A Diary from the Last Days of the Qing Dynasty*

**Fifteenth day of the first month, Xuantong Year Four.
Changchun Palace. The moon is full. The snow has cleared.**

The moon is beautiful tonight.

The snow has stopped, and a thin layer of silver lies over the glazed roofs of the Forbidden City.

I am sitting by the window with an old book resting on my knees. I have turned its pages so many times that the paper has yellowed and the corners have curled. The edges bear the faint marks of my fingers, worn into the paper by years of touching the same passages again and again.

**The book is not mine.

It belonged to Guangxu.**

He left very little behind. Most of what remained were ordinary things. But this book had been wrapped in an old piece of silk and kept beside his pillow.

When I was sorting through his belongings after his death, the silk came undone. The book slipped to the floor and fell open to a page covered with red-ink markings.

Beside the lines were tiny notes in his handwriting, so faint that they seemed almost ashamed to be seen.

I knelt to pick it up.

The passage he had marked was:

**“The people are the foundation of the state; when the foundation is secure, the state is at peace.”**

Eight characters.

He had circled them again and again until the paper was nearly worn through.

**That was the first time—after Guangxu had died—that I truly saw him.**

When he was alive, we were separated by an entire Forbidden City.

After death, separated by the boundary between life and death, I somehow felt closer to him than ever.

Tonight, as I turn to that same page, the moonlight falls across the old paper. The red ink looks almost like dried blood.

And suddenly I wonder:

**Perhaps what I did was not entirely my own idea.**

During the years Guangxu was confined on Yingtai, I visited him every few days. The eunuchs and palace women were afraid to come too close. I was almost the only one who entered.

He rarely spoke when I was there.

He would lie on his couch with books in his hands—Western books, books about reform, copies of memorials submitted by Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao.

Once, while pouring his tea, I saw a scrap of paper beside his pillow.

He had written:

**“China cannot survive without reform. The essence of reform lies in removing the barriers that block information and opening channels to the people.”**

His handwriting was crooked. He had written it while ill.

I said nothing.

I put down the tea and left.

**But I remembered every word.**

A man imprisoned on a small island, surrounded by water, forbidden to see almost anyone—and still writing about opening channels to the people.

For ten years, no one had truly opened a channel to him.

I do not know what he felt while writing those words.

Perhaps he laughed at himself as he wrote, thinking, *What use is any of this?*

Perhaps he cried, because there was no one in the world left who would understand.

**I have often wondered whether, during those ten years, he was waiting for someone.**

Not someone to rescue him.

Only someone willing to walk into that room, sit beside his bed, and say:

**“I understand.”**

But I never said it.

I was too afraid.

Afraid of the Empress Dowager.

Afraid of the eunuchs.

Afraid that even the walls had ears.

Every time I left Yingtai, I carried his words with me. I turned them over in my mind, one character after another, then swallowed them whole.

I thought that once swallowed, they would disappear.

**But thirty years later, those same words—“remove the barriers,” “open channels to the people,” “the people are the foundation”—came out of my mouth as naturally as if they had always been mine.**

Only now do I understand.

**Guangxu was not my husband.

We were two prisoners in two cages, separated by a lake.**

We could see each other.

We simply could not reach each other.

For ten years he wrote on that island.

For ten years I remained on the shore, guarding the words he could not speak.

When he died, those words rose from somewhere inside me.

They rose into my throat.

They waited on my tongue.

They were simply waiting for their moment.

---

The day the abdication edict was read, everyone in the Hall of Mental Cultivation was crying.

But as I heard my own voice reading those final sentences aloud, I suddenly felt strangely detached.

**The voice did not sound like mine.**

There was another voice inside it.

The voice of a man who had spent ten years imprisoned on an island and died with a book still in his hands.

When I finished reading, I looked up.

For an instant, I could almost see Guangxu sitting in the corner of the hall, holding that old book.

He looked at me.

And nodded.

---

Puyi went to sleep early tonight.

I went in to look at him.

He was still clutching half of an unfinished sugar figure. A little smear of sugar had dried at the corner of his mouth.

I wiped it away gently.

He turned over and murmured something in his sleep.

I stood beside his bed for a long time.

He does not look like Guangxu.

And yet, when he sleeps, the slight furrow between his brows—

that uneasy expression, as if even in dreams he is carrying some burden—

**it is so familiar.**

Too familiar.

I remembered Guangxu's afternoons on Yingtai.

He slept like that too.

His brows drawn together, as though even his dreams were arguments he could not win.

I reached out and touched Puyi's head.

**The child does not yet know that, for three years, he has been the son of a man he never knew.**

---

There is something else I must record.

Three days after the abdication edict was signed, Yuan Shikai came to the Forbidden City.

He said he wanted to see “the Emperor's daily life.”

We sat together in the Hall of Mental Cultivation.

Puyi hid behind a screen, peeking out at him.

Yuan smiled and said,

“His Majesty has grown taller.”

I answered politely.

After Yuan left, Puyi ran out from behind the screen and tugged at my sleeve.

“Mother,” he asked, “was that man the one who took our home away?”

I froze.

“No,” I said.

Puyi tilted his head.

“Then why does he get to decide everything, while you don't?”

I picked him up and held his face against mine.

“Because Mother does not want a war.”

“If there is a war, many people will die.”

“Who are those people?”

“People we don't know.”

I looked at him.

“But they have mothers too.”

“Imagine that one day someone wanted to kill Mother. Would you be sad?”

Puyi nodded.

His eyes were already red.

I said,

“If those people die, their mothers will grieve. Their children will grieve too.”

“Mother doesn't want so many children to be sad.”

“So Mother gave up our home.”

He seemed to understand.

He whispered,

“Mm.”

Then he rested his head on my shoulder and fell asleep.

I sat by the window with him in my arms.

The afternoon sun slanted through the room, illuminating the dust floating slowly through the air.

And suddenly I remembered something from long ago.

So long ago that I had almost forgotten it.

I was sixteen.

I once stood behind the Empress Dowager while she argued with Guangxu.

She said:

**“You are Emperor because I made you Emperor.”**

Guangxu stood there with his fists clenched, his entire body trembling.

But he did not answer her.

His teeth were grinding so hard I could hear them.

At last, he said through clenched teeth:

**“I never wanted to be Emperor.”**

The Empress Dowager laughed coldly.

“If you did not want to be Emperor, what did you want to be?”

Guangxu raised his head.

He looked at me.

Just once.

Then he said:

**“I wanted to be a scholar.”**

The Empress Dowager burst into laughter.

Her jewels shook with the force of it.

Everyone in the hall laughed.

**Everyone except me.**

I looked at Guangxu's face.

At his eyes.

**He was serious.**

He truly wanted to be a scholar.

To hold books.

To write.

To sit beneath the sun.

To play chess and talk about the past like the old men outside the ancestral temple.

But he could not.

He was Emperor.

I was sixteen.

I felt sorry for him, but I dared not show it.

I stood with my head lowered, twisting my fingers inside my sleeves until my knuckles turned white.

Thirty years later, sitting here in Changchun Palace with Puyi in my arms, I suddenly remembered that day.

The day Guangxu said:

**“I wanted to be a scholar.”**

And I remembered the way he looked at me.

**He was asking me for something.**

Not rescue.

Not rebellion.

Only this:

*Please do not laugh at me.*

*Please understand.*

**I did understand.**

**But I was too afraid to say so.**

Now I can.

Guangxu, can you hear me?

**I was Emperor for thirty years in your place.**

You could not walk away from the throne.

So I walked the road for you.

You could not let go.

So I let go for you.

The words you wrote beside your books—

“remove the barriers,”

“open channels to the people,”

“the people are the foundation”—

**I spoke them for you.**

I spoke them in the great hall, before everyone.

They believed those were the words of Empress Dowager Longyu.

But I knew.

**They were yours.**

You have been dead for ten years.

Yet your words remain.

You taught me for thirty years.

Not through speeches.

Not through lessons.

**Through silence.**

---

There is one more thing I must write down.

There was an old eunuch in the palace who served me for more than twenty years.

When he was young, he worked in the palace while Guangxu was still alive. Several times, he carried things between me and Yingtai.

Once, I asked him to take a plate of sweets to Guangxu.

He accepted them.

Then he sent back a note.

Only six characters:

**“Empress, do you read?”**

I wrote back:

**“Yes. Not enough.”**

After that, he would occasionally bring books to me.

*Haiguo Tuzhi.*

*Yinghuan Zhilüe.*

Newspapers and writings associated with Liang Qichao.

I dared not read them openly.

I hid beneath the quilts in Changchun Palace, lighting a small lamp.

Night after night, I read until dawn.

The old eunuch died recently.

Before he died, he held my hand.

“Your Majesty,” he said, “I know these years have not been easy.”

I asked him:

“What do you know?”

He answered:

**“I know how many books you read for His Majesty. I know how many of his words you remembered for him.”**

That day, I cried.

For the first time in thirty years, I cried in front of a eunuch.

Because I suddenly realized:

**Someone knew.**

Someone knew about those nights beneath the little lamp.

Someone knew how I memorized the words marked in those books.

Someone knew that I remembered words I had no reason to believe I would ever use.

Guangxu spent ten years writing on Yingtai.

I spent ten years reading on the shore.

**We were like two people standing on opposite banks of a lake, each throwing stones into the water.**

The stones disappeared without a sound.

But beneath the surface—

**they were gathering.**

Stone by stone.

Until, somewhere beneath the water, they formed a bridge.

---

After the abdication, I sometimes sit beside the window and stare into the distance.

And I remember Guangxu's afternoons on Yingtai.

Sunlight passed through the lattice windows, dividing his face into strips of light and shadow.

He always held a book.

But sometimes he did not read it.

He simply held it.

**As though he were holding on to hope itself.**

I did not understand him then.

I do now.

He was thinking:

*Someday, someone will see these words.*

*Even if I die.*

*Even if the Qing falls.*

**The words will remain.**

**I was that someone.**

---

The imperial physician says my strength is failing.

He came today to take my pulse.

When he left, I heard him whispering to the palace women.

His voice was low, but I heard him.

“Prepare for the end.”

I was not frightened.

**Perhaps I have been waiting for those words since the day of the abdication.**

Puyi was exhausted from playing and went to sleep early.

I am sitting here sewing a small padded coat for him.

My stitches are crooked.

My eyes have grown too weak to guide the needle properly.

But I cannot put the sewing down.

**Perhaps this is the last garment I will ever make for him.**

He will grow taller.

Stronger.

He will outgrow this coat within a few years.

But he will have grown up wearing something I made.

And perhaps that is enough.

Perhaps one day, even if he remembers only vaguely, he will remember that once, on a winter night, someone sat beneath a lamp and made him a coat.

That is enough.

---

All my life, people thought me slow.

Simple.

A woman who could be pushed from one place to another because I never knew how to resist.

Almost nothing in my life was chosen by me.

**But the abdication was my choice.**

I walked the road Guangxu had spent his life unable to walk.

And I walked it to the end.

**The road is finished.

It is time for me to rest.**

The moon is very bright tonight.

Snow still covers the roofs of the Forbidden City.

The moonlight on it is almost painful to look at.

I suddenly think:

When my time comes, they will place me in the imperial tomb.

It will be dark there.

There will be no moonlight.

But that is all right.

**I will carry with me ten years of Yingtai, an Emperor's unfinished words, and the last fragment of dignity belonging to a dying dynasty.**

Those things are worth more than any treasure they could place beside me.

Guangxu, I spoke the words you left behind.

You said:

**“The people are the foundation.”**

I abdicated so that this country would not have to fight another war, so that those people might live.

You said:

**“Open channels to the people.”**

I opened the palace gates.

I tried to stop the walls between the court and the world from becoming even higher.

You said:

**“Remove the barriers.”**

I pushed aside what I could—the concealment, the deception, the silence that had accumulated over thirty years.

**I did not do it perfectly.

But I did it.**

Puyi is asleep.

I should sleep too.

It is late.

Tomorrow I will still have to rise early.

No matter how short life has become, one must still live it one day at a time.

I do not know how many days remain.

No matter.

One day at a time.

I ask only one thing.

When the final day comes, when I leave this world, let my spirit drift toward Yingtai.

Let me walk once more beside that lake.

I want to see what Guangxu was reading when he stood there all those years ago.

Perhaps the line he was reading said:

**“When the world has order, one may devote oneself to the Way; when the world has lost the Way, one may devote one's life to the Way.”**

Neither of us became martyrs.

Neither of us changed the world in the way we once imagined.

**We simply lived.

For each other.**

**That is enough.**

**Yehenara Jingfen**

**Fifteenth day of the first lunar month, Xuantong Year Four
Changchun Palace, beneath the evening lamp**

---

# **Postscript II**

After finishing this diary, I added several lines at the end.

I scratched them into the paper with my fingernail.

No ink.

I do not know whether anyone will ever be able to read them.

But I want them to remain.

**“If one day someone finds this book, tell the people of the future this: The last abdication edict of the Great Qing was not written by one person. It was written by two. One spent ten years imprisoned on Yingtai. The other spent thirty years waiting in Changchun Palace. We spoke very few words to each other, but we said the same thing: Do not let the common people die anymore. That is all. It is enough.”**

I placed the diary in the hidden compartment beside my bed.

I put Guangxu's old book beside it.

**Two books.

Separated by ten years.

At last, lying side by side.**

**Jingfen.

Final entry.**
