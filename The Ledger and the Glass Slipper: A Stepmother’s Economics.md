Prologue: Pressed Roses on the Margin of the Ledger
It was the end of the month—time to balance the accounts.

The scraping of the quill across the parchment rustled through the midnight study like rats gnawing at wooden beams. The fire in the hearth had nearly died down. I pulled my shawl tighter—bought three years ago, its wool now worn threadbare and shiny, patched at the elbows with stitches so fine they could not be seen unless looked at up close. Much like this household: immaculate on the surface, but riddled with rot within.

"Mother, still awake?"

My eldest daughter, Anastasia, pushed open the door, carrying a cup of warm milk. She was twenty-four now, with fine lines forming at the corners of her eyes. Her match-making attempt last year had ended in failure—the suitor, a widowed baron, deemed her dowry too meager. I took the milk. My fingertips brushed the ceramic wall; it was warm, just right. She was always so thoughtful—thoughtful to a fault that made one's heart ache.

"Nearly done." I turned a page in the ledger. Written in fresh ink was the line:

March 27th: Cinderella's music lesson tuition, 30 silver coins.

Thirty silver coins. Enough to buy half a bolt of fine linen, enough to pay the coachman's wages for half a month, enough to make Anastasia a respectable gown for a ball. I stared at those words for a long time, until the ink smudged slightly, like a drop of aged tears.

The sound of horse hooves echoed outside. It was the carriage of our neighbor, Baron Duval, just returned from Paris, laden with newly purchased silk and perfume. The rumble of wheels over cobblestones cut sharp and harsh through the quiet night. I rose to close the window. As my hand touched the wooden frame, it brushed against a deep scratch—carved by Charlotte, my younger daughter, when she was ten. Her father was still alive then; she had sat on his shoulders, reaching up with a penknife to etch her name into the wood.

C. T. — Charlotte Tremaine.

Beneath the initials was a smaller line added by her father: "My little princess, born in the spring of 1712."

Sixteen years ago. The war had just ended then, and the economy was booming. This house was filled with banquets, the garden bloomed with roses, and I wore gowns custom-made from Lyon, strolling arm in arm with him while guests praised us as "the most elegant couple in town."

Now the roses had withered, the gowns were pawned, and the guests were long gone. He lay in the church cemetery, the inscriptions on his tombstone half-eaten by moss.

And I sat in the study he left behind, calculating how much money we were short this month to keep from going bankrupt.

The milk had turned cold. I raised the cup and drank it all down. The faint gamey taste of milk mixed with the parchment scent of the ledger, rolling in my throat before I swallowed it whole—like swallowing a block of ice-cold iron.

Anastasia was still waiting. "Mother, Cinderella's tutor said... she's very talented. Next month, court musicians are coming to select pupils. If..."

"I know," I cut her off, my voice colder than intended. "Go to sleep."

She pursed her lips, turned, and left. Her skirts brushed the doorframe, bringing a faint breeze. I watched her slightly slouched back—a habit formed from hours of embroidery over the years, uncorrectable, just like our steady decline in social status.

Silence reclaimed the study. I opened the drawer and drew out an older ledger. Bound in cowhide with worn edges, it contained not numbers, but memories. Or rather, accounts of a different kind.

Flipping to a certain page, I found a dried rose pressed between the leaves. Its petals were scorched yellow, ready to crumble at a touch. Beside it was my handwriting from when I was seventeen—childish, spirited, and free:

"Met him today at the Versailles ball. He wore a dark blue dress coat pinned with a pearl brooch. He asked me to dance and said my eyes were like midnight velvet. Mother said he is Viscount Tremaine, heir to vast vineyards. Though not the wealthiest, his future is promising. I pretended not to care, but my heart beat so fast. His hands were so warm."

I stared at that line for a long time, then picked up the quill and added a sentence beside it. In my current hand—dry, sharp, like a blade carving into stone:

"Thirty years later, the vineyards produced nothing due to pestilence, leaving us drowning in debt. He died of consumption, holding my hand and whispering 'I'm sorry.' It rained on the day of his funeral; water pooled in the grave. When his coffin was lowered, it made a heavy splash, like a stone dropped into a well. I have remembered that sound for ten years."

I closed the ledger. Crumbs of the dried rose drifted from between the pages, landing on the fresh line of the new ledger—Thirty silver coins—like placing a shabby mourning flower over those cold digits.

Outside the window, the moon emerged. Its pale light fell upon the ledger, where columns of numbers stood like headstones, burying my youth, my love, and every gentle imagination I had ever held about this world.

Yet tomorrow, I would still have to rise early to haggle with the dressmaker, deal with the collection lawyers, and tell Cinderella: the music lessons are canceled.

Because thirty silver coins could buy two lives—the lives of my two biological daughters.

Chapter 1: Marriage Is a Limited Liability Company
I was twenty-one when I married Edward Tremaine. Neither too young nor too old. My father was a tax officer, a modest post, and our family had six daughters—I was the third. On the eve of my wedding, my mother took my hands and told me three things:

"First, do not love too deeply. Men die, love cools, but title deeds and certificates of deposit are real."

"Second, bear children as soon as possible, preferably sons. Daughters are a losing proposition; marrying them off strips a layer of skin from the bride's family."

"Third, keep the accounts well. Only when you control the money can you control the house and hold your husband's heart—though a heart is worth the least."

I carved those three sentences into my mind like an epitaph. On my wedding day, I wore a rented wedding dress and carried artificial flowers as I walked down the church aisle. Edward waited for me at the altar. He was indeed handsome, with dark brown hair, gray eyes, and a dimple on his left cheek when he smiled. When he took my hand, his palm was damp with sweat.

The guests applauded and whispered. I caught fragments: "...I heard the dowry was only five thousand francs..." "...The Tremaine family has fallen on hard times too..." "...Make the best of it; these are tough years..."

I smiled, my chin tilted at a calculated angle—practiced a hundred times before the mirror, neither arrogant nor humble. I knew what they were saying. Edward's vineyards suffered consecutive bad harvests, and my father's position was on shaky ground. This marriage was two ruined families huddling together for warmth, two leaking boats lashed together with ropes, praying not to sink too fast.

On our wedding night, he kissed me gently, as if afraid to break something fragile. I responded while calculating in my head: how much for house maintenance, how much for servant wages, how much for next quarter's taxes. When he entered my body, I gasped in pain, my fingers clutching the bedsheets, wrinkling the expensive silk. He whispered "I'm sorry" in my ear, and I replied, "It's fine."

Yet my eyes remained open, gazing at the intricate embroidery on the bed curtains—roses entwined with thorns, one after another. I thought: So this is marriage. Two people lying in one bed, separated by debt, calculations, and the inevitable decline of family honor.

Still, I wanted to manage it well. Like managing a shop: though the capital was small, with hard work and sharpness, we might break even, or even turn a modest profit.

I learned to brew wine—the Tremaine family business. I walked through the vineyards with old workers, inspecting the soil and sunlight, learning how to prune vines and prevent mold. My hands blistered, tore, calloused, and tore again. Edward felt pained for me, saying, "Leave these matters to the servants." I shook my head: "If I don't understand it, how can I manage it?"

I truly applied myself. I lit lamps late into the night to inspect the ledgers, auditing line by line. Catching the steward inflating purchase prices, I dismissed him and took over management myself. When suppliers tried to overcharge me because I was a woman, I compared prices shop by shop and negotiated until their faces turned red and they called me "shrewder than a money-lender."

Edward was gratified at first, but later grew uneasy. Once, after drinking too much, he held me and said, "Mary, you don't need to exhaust yourself like this. I can..."

"You can what?" I refilled his wine glass, my voice gentle. "You invested in shipping last year and lost three thousand francs. The year before, you bought mining stocks and lost everything. Edward, it isn't that I don't trust you—it's that this household cannot afford another gamble."

He fell silent, burying his face in the crook of my neck. I smelled the alcohol on him, mixed with the faint, desolate scent of a defeated man. I stroked his hair, my heart devoid of ripples, holding only a cold clarity: This man is my partner, not my savior. This marriage is a limited liability company; if it prospers, we share the gains, but if it fails, I must ensure my share is not dragged down by his foolishness.

When Anastasia was born, I suffered a difficult labor lasting two days and one night. When the child finally emerged, I suffered a severe hemorrhage and fell into a coma for three days. When I woke, Edward was sitting by the bedside, his eyes sunken and stubble covering his jaw. He held my hand and wept: "We won't have any more. One is enough, enough."

I looked at the daughter in the cradle, tiny and red, like a hairless rodent. I felt no joy, only panic: A daughter. A losing proposition. We would have to prepare a dowry, find her a respectable family, and ensure she wasn't looked down upon—all of which required money.

And I had just walked to the gates of death, nearly paying with my life, only to receive a "losing proposition."

I closed my eyes and said, "Fine. No more."

Two years later, Charlotte was conceived by accident. I took medicines, jumped down stairs, and laced my corset to the point of suffocation, yet she still insisted on arriving into the world late one night. It was worse this time—a breech birth that nearly took both our lives. When the midwife brought the child for me to see, I turned my head away and said, "Take her away."

Edward named them: Anastasia and Charlotte. Both were lovely names, like princesses in fairy tales. But we were no fairy tale; we were declining figures in a ledger, maturing promissory notes, and dwindling harvests in the vineyard year after year.

When Charlotte was three years old, Edward brought home a girl.

Around six years old, thin as a reed, with golden hair, blue eyes, and a tattered rag doll in her arms. She hid behind him, peeking out half her face at me, her gaze timid like a deer in the woods.

"This is Cinderella," Edward crouched down, pushing the girl forward gently. "Her father... was an old friend of mine who passed away from illness recently. Her mother passed long ago. From today on, she stays with us."

I looked at the girl. She wore a worn dress, but the material was fine cotton, and the collar had delicate lace—though frayed. Her little leather shoes were covered in mud, but the style was the latest from Paris. Most importantly, her eyes: clear, innocent, bearing the untarnished naivety of someone well-protected.

That kind of naivety required a great deal of money to cultivate.

"What did her father do?" I asked, my voice calm.

"...A merchant. In the spice trade." Edward avoided my eyes.

"How much inheritance did he leave?"

"Mary!" He frowned, but quickly softened his tone. "The child is still small; we can discuss that later. Let her stay first, all right?"

I did not press further. Because I saw the necklace around the girl's neck—a gold chain holding a small, roughly cut, but high-grade sapphire pendant. It was something I had gazed at countless times in jewelry shop windows, yet could never afford.

I crouched down and smiled at her. Reaching out, my fingertips brushed her slightly curled hair. "Hello, Cinderella. I am Mary. You may call me... Auntie."

She blinked and said softly, "Hello, Auntie."

Her voice was soft, with a southern accent. Pleasant to hear. And very expensive.

Edward sighed in relief, picked up the girl, and said, "Let me show you your room." As he turned, I saw it—a fresh red mark on the back of his neck, looking like a fingernail scratch. Not deep, but noticeable enough.

I stood in place, watching their figures disappear around the turn of the staircase. Then I turned, walked into the study, and opened the ledger. On a new page, I wrote:

"June 15th: Household addition — Cinderella (claims to be six, suspect seven). Estimated annual expenses: Clothing 30 francs, food 50 francs, education 80 francs, miscellaneous 20 francs. Total: 180 francs/year. Note: Need to verify father's estate status ASAP. Also: Edward returned today with scratch mark on neck, scent of spice (musk, expensive variety), suspected affair. Maintain observation."

After writing, I closed the ledger. My fingers stroked the cowhide cover, its rough texture rubbing against my fingertips like sharpening a blunt knife.

Outside the window, the roses bloomed. Vasty patches of red, intense as blood. Edward had said his mother loved roses most, which was why he planted the whole garden with them. But roses were delicate; they needed fertilizer, weeding, and meticulous care, or they would wither before your eyes.

Just like this household. Just like marriage. Just like this strange girl who suddenly appeared, costing one hundred and eighty francs a year to raise.

I stood up and walked to the window. In the garden, Edward was holding Cinderella's hand, pointing out the roses to her. The golden light of the setting sun spilled over them like a heartwarming oil painting.

How beautiful.

Beautifully false, beautiful as those red figures in the ledger that could never be balanced.

I drew the curtains, shutting out both the light and the roses.

Chapter 2: Emotion Is a Liability, Reason Is an Asset
Three months after Cinderella's arrival, the matter of the inheritance was settled.

Her father had indeed been in the spice trade, and he had indeed gone bankrupt—not dying of illness, but jumping into a river to commit suicide because debt crushed him. What he left behind was not an inheritance, but IOUs. A thick stack, stuffed at the bottom of Cinderella's small trunk, bound with a ribbon like a cruel coming-of-age gift.

Edward burned the IOUs in front of the hearth, sheet by sheet. Flames licked the paper, curling it, turning it black, reducing it to ashes. He stood with his back to me, his shoulders slumped like a hollowed-out sack.

"She knows nothing," he said, his voice raspy. "She thinks her father went away on business."

I said nothing. I was calculating: over these three months, Cinderella's expenses had exceeded fifty francs. She was a picky eater, consuming only tender chicken and fresh berries; she was delicate, requiring daily clothes changes with scented laundry; she was sensitive, having nightmares at night that required company, which had already scared away two maids.

Fifty francs. Enough for three months of allowance for Anastasia and Charlotte. Enough to pay half a year's property tax. Enough to buy a draft horse.

And now, those fifty francs had yielded a pile of ashes and someone else's daughter who would need to be raised indefinitely.

"She cannot stay," I finally spoke, my voice as calm as if discussing the weather. "Send her to an orphanage, or find a distant relative. We cannot afford to keep her."

Edward turned around, his eyes red—whether from the smoke or from crying. "She is so small! Mary, how can you be so heartless?"

"I am heartless," I walked toward him, step by step, my heels clicking against the marble floor—click, click, click—like beads on an abacus. "Because I must first be heartless for the sake of my own daughters. Anastasia is sixteen; it is time to prepare her dowry. Charlotte is frail; her medicine cannot be stopped. The manor roof is leaking; if not repaired, we will freeze to death in winter. Edward, you have seen our ledgers."

He opened his mouth, but made no sound. I took another step closer, looking up at him. The gray eyes that once thrilled me were now covered in a haze, murky and powerless.

"Or," I softened my tone, reaching out to adjust his collar—a gentle gesture, like that of a devoted wife, "tell me where our extra one hundred and eighty francs will come from. Will you win it at the casino? Take out a high-interest loan? Or..."

I paused, my fingertips brushing the back of his neck—the red mark had faded, but a faint trace remained if one looked closely.

"Or borrow it from the widow of your... 'spice merchant friend'?"

He froze. His pupils contracted sharply, as if pricked by a needle. Then he grabbed my hands tightly, his nails digging into my flesh. "You investigated me?"

"I am your wife." I let him hold me, not struggling, even offering a faint smile. "Investigating your whereabouts to ensure you aren't duped out of our family estate by some temptress is both my duty and my right."

He released his grip, staggering back until he bumped against the mantelpiece. The brass candlestick wobbled, nearly falling. He looked at me as if looking at a stranger.

"She is not a temptress," his voice trembled. "She is Cinderella's mother. We... were in love. But her husband refused a divorce, so she could only give birth to Cinderella in secret and foster her in the countryside. Now her husband is dead, and she wanted to claim her daughter, but..."

"But she is bankrupt too and cannot afford her, so she dumped the child on you?" I took over the sentence, walking to the ledger, opening it, my finger pointing to the newest deficit. "Edward, your love story is moving. But being moving doesn't pay bills, repair roofs, or prepare dowries for my daughters."

I snapped the ledger shut with a loud clap that echoed through the quiet study.

"Cinderella may stay," I said, each word dropping like an ice pellet onto the floor. "But from now on, I run this household. Your allowance is halved, and you are forbidden from making further investments. Anastasia and Charlotte's expenses come first; Cinderella gets what remains. Agree, and nod. Disagree..."

I walked to the window and drew open the curtain. The sun had set; in the garden, Cinderella was crouching by the rose bushes, trying to catch a butterfly. She smiled brightly, her golden hair catching fragments of light in the twilight.

"Disagree, and I will go tell her right now: how her father died, and why her mother abandoned her. Then I'll have the coachman take her to the cheapest orphanage in the city—you know what it's like there. Rats, lice, a piece of moldy bread a day, and toes frozen off in winter."

I turned back to look at Edward. His face was deathly pale, as if drained of blood.

"Choose," I said.

He lowered his head, his shoulders trembling. At first it was suppressed, but soon it broke out into loud weeping. A forty-year-old man, kneeling before the hearth, crying like an abandoned dog.

I walked over, crouched down, and held him. Like holding a child. My chin rested on his shoulder while my hand gently patted his back, humming a lullaby—the one I used to sing Anastasia and Charlotte to sleep.

He wept even harder.

As I gazed at the ashes in the hearth—the remains of those IOUs now cold and black, like a rotted heart—I knew I had won. A complete victory. I had won the authority of the head of the house, but I had also gained a husband who hated me and an extra burden that would consume my daughters' dowries.

Yet what of it? In this world, you either eat or get eaten. You are either ruthless, or you starve.

I chose the former.

That night, I added a new page to the ledger. Title: "Cinderella Tremaine (Provisional Name) Cost-Benefit Analysis."

The left column listed "Costs": Board and lodging, education, medical care, dowry (long-term).

The right column listed "Potential Returns": If cultivated properly, may marry into a middle-class family for a bride price of roughly 500–1000 francs (depending on suitor's means); if exceptionally beautiful, may attach to nobility, bringing connections to the family (probability under 5%).

In the "Notes", I wrote: "Subject exhibits delicate disposition; requires strict discipline to remove ostentatious habits. Education shall focus on practical skills (housekeeping, needlework); luxury items such as music and dance to be reduced as appropriate. Target: Married before eighteen; bride price must cover cumulative maintenance costs with maximum surplus. This is an investment, not charity. Emotional factors excluded from calculation."

Finishing the entry, I put down the quill. Outside, an owl screeched—piercing and long. I rose and walked to my daughters' room.

Anastasia and Charlotte slept together like two kittens, curled up to keep each other warm. I tucked in their blanket, my fingertips brushing Anastasia's face—she resembled me, especially her eyes, though her gaze was always timid, lacking the brightness of my youth.

I visited the adjacent room. Cinderella was asleep too, holding that tattered rag doll, tears still clinging to her eyelashes—she had tripped while chasing butterflies earlier, scraping her knee, and cried for a long time. I stood by the bed, watching her for a long while.

Moonlight filtered through the sheer curtain, casting light on her face. So small, so soft, so innocent.

I raised my hand, suspending it above her cheek. I wanted to touch her, to wipe away the tear trails. But in the end, I pulled my hand back.

Turning to leave, I heard a voice inside my heart—faint and cold:

"I am sorry, child. But this world has never apologized to the poor."

So I would not apologize either.

Chapter 3: Class Is a Cage, Marriage Is a Jailbreak
Edward died late that autumn.

Consumption. Ill for over half a year—coughing, spitting blood, until reduced to a rack of bones. Before he passed, he grasped my hand; his skin was icy, like a fish frozen for three days. He murmured: "Mary... I'm sorry... also, Cinderella... I beg you..."

Before he could finish, he drew his last breath. His eyes remained open, staring at the ceiling as if questioning God.

I closed his eyes for him, my palm catching the last bit of his body heat, which quickly turned cold. I sat by the bed for a long time until a servant stepped in, whispering: "Madam, the lawyer is here to read the will."

The will was simple: all property belonged to me until my remarriage or death, after which it would be split equally among Anastasia, Charlotte, and Cinderella. Fair, yet foolish—he did not know that those "assets" had long turned into negative numbers.

It rained on the day of the funeral. Not heavy, but fine and sticky, clinging like spiderwebs. The priest read the eulogy in a flat monotone, sounding like he was reading a ledger. Guests were sparse, standing in a few straggling rows, most bearing solemn faces—not for Edward, but out of concern over whether the money they lent us could be recovered.

Dressed in black with a veil covering my face, I stood straight. Anastasia and Charlotte held my arms on either side, crying quietly and suppressively. Cinderella stood at the very front; she did not cry, but kept her eyes wide open, staring at the headstone as if pondering a difficult question.

When the funeral ended and the crowd dispersed, Baroness Duval—my former close friend with whom I rarely interacted now—walked over, took my hand, and sighed: "My condolences, Mary. Things... will be harder from now on."

She was not wrong.

During the first month, three creditors showed up simultaneously. I had the servant serve tea, sat in the primary seat, spread open the ledger, and calculated line by line for them: current assets, liquidatable assets, and monthly repayment capacity. I spoke slowly and clearly, as if teaching children arithmetic. Finally, I said: "Drive me to death, and you won't get a single coin. Give me three years, and I will repay principal and interest in full. Otherwise, take me to court and see if I have money to cover legal fees."

They looked at each other, cursed under their breath, and left. But they did not return.

During the second month, the tailor's shop sent a bill for a dress coat Edward had ordered before his death that was never picked up. I returned it and paid the breach penalty, which was cheaper than full price. I cleared out all of Edward's clothing—selling what could be sold and altering what could be altered to make new dresses for Anastasia and Charlotte. They needed presentable clothes to go out, socialize, and... marry.

During the third month, the hardest times arrived: the manor steward resigned, citing wages too low to support his family. Next went the coachman, the gardener, and the cook. Eventually, only old Betty and her lame husband remained—he guarded the gate, while she cooked and cleaned.

"Madam, I'm not leaving," Betty said, rubbing her apron, her eyes red. "I grew up in the Tremaine household; the master was kind to me. Besides..." She glanced at Cinderella playing in the mud outside and lowered her voice: "That poor child has no mother, and now no father. I can't bear to leave her."

I embraced her thin frame, her bones pressing against me. Then I gave her her wages for the month, along with a bonus. "Go buy some meat," I said. "The children are growing."

That night, I added another page to the ledger: "Staff Reduction and Cost-Cutting Plan." On the left were previous personnel expenses; on the right were current ones. The difference was significant, but not enough. Far from enough.

I stared at those numbers until my eyes grew blurry. Then I stood up and walked to the window. The garden had long fallen into ruin; most of the roses were dead, and the remainder were sickly, overgrown with weeds. Under the moonlight, the entire garden looked like a giant, festering piece of worn cloth.

Footsteps sounded behind me. It was Anastasia, carrying a cup of hot tea. "Mother, go to sleep."

I took the tea without drinking, looking at her. In the candlelight, her face was soft, her features gentle, resembling Edward closely. Yet she possessed a resilience Edward lacked—polished by poverty, like a pebble in a riverbed, its sharp edges smoothed by the current, yet harder and heavier.

"Anastasia," I spoke suddenly, "have you thought about marriage?"

She paused, her face flushing. "Why do you ask so suddenly, Mother..."

"Answer me."

She lowered her head, twirling her fingers. "I have. But... with our family like this, who would marry me? Unless..." She paused, her voice dropping lower, "unless I require no dowry, or... marry someone... older, or..."

"Or a widower whose wife died, leaving him with children?" I finished the sentence for her.

She nodded, her eyes rimmed red.

My heart twitched as if pricked sharply by a needle. I pulled her close and embraced her. Her shoulders were delicate, trembling slightly. "No," I said, my voice steady enough that even I believed it. "My Anastasia will marry the best person and live the best life. Mother promises."

Yet how could I promise? With what could I promise?

That night, I sat in the study until dawn. As daylight faintly broke, I pushed open the window, letting the cold air rush in with the frost of late autumn. I saw Cinderella in the garden—she always rose early, crouching by the withered rose bushes, digging in the soil with a small trowel, searching for something.

Then I saw it.

She dug up a small iron box, rusted all over. Opening it, she took out a package wrapped in oil paper. She unwrapped it carefully to reveal seeds—various flower seeds labeled with names on paper strips: night-blooming jasmine, violet, daisy...

There was also a small card written in a delicate feminine hand: "To my Cinderella: When spring comes, plant these flowers with Papa and wait for Mama to return. — With endless love, Mama."

Cinderella looked at the card for a long time. Then she raised her head toward the pale sky as tears fell in large drops. But she made no sound, merely biting her lip tightly until it bled.

I closed the window. Returning to the desk, I opened the ledger and wrote on the newest page:

"Adjustment Plan for Investment Directions."

First item: Anastasia. Advantages: Gentle disposition, exquisite needlework, modestly literate. Target demographic: Upper-middle class or above, respectable family background, suitable age (can be relaxed to thirty-five). Investment required: Procurement of two banquet gowns (estimated 80 francs), etiquette tutor (30 francs), expanding social circle (tea parties, concerts, etc., monthly average 20 francs). Expected return: Dowry/bride price not lower than 1,000 francs, with post-marital support for maiden family.

Second item: Charlotte. Advantages: Good looks (resembling father), skilled in song and dance, lively personality. Disadvantages: Frail health, delicate. Target demographic: Wealthy merchants or lower nobility (who do not mind ill health). Investment required: Health conditioning (medical fees monthly average 15 francs), refining talents (music tutor, 30 francs monthly), crafting a 'fragile artistic maiden' persona. Expected return: Bride price above 1,500 francs (merchants willing to pay premium for 'noble aura').

Third item: Cinderella.

I stopped there. The quill tip hovered over the paper, ink dripping down to form a black smudge.

This extra burden, this obstacle consuming my daughters' dowry—how was she to be handled?

Send her to an orphanage? Possible. But she was ten years old and held memories; she would hate me, and should her circumstances change in the future, she might seek revenge. Keep her at home? She would continue to cost money and potentially crowd out resources for Anastasia and Charlotte.

I recalled the gaze in her eyes when she dug up the flower seeds, and how she bit her lip to keep from crying out loud. I recalled Edward's dying plea: "Cinderella... I beg you..."

I also recalled the glaring deficits in the ledger.

Finally, I wrote:

"Third item: Cinderella. Positioning: Household maid (apprentice). Duties: Assist Betty with cleaning, laundry, and kitchen chores. Education: Retain basic literacy only (taught by Anastasia in free time); all talent lessons suspended. Diet: Same standard as servants. Clothing: Altered old clothes from Anastasia and Charlotte. Target: Train as a qualified maid before eighteen to save employment costs, or marry off to a tenant farmer/craftsman requiring no dowry. Note: Strict discipline required to strip away 'young lady' pretensions and adapt to reality."

Finishing the entry, I put down the pen. My hand was shaking uncontrollably. I clenched my fist, digging my nails into my palm to suppress the cold disgust rising from the depths of my heart.

I told myself: Mary, you have no choice. You are a mother; you must ensure your own daughters survive and live decently. As for someone else's daughter...

I looked out the window. Cinderella was still there, crouching by the withered rose bushes, carefully rewrapping the seeds, placing them back into the iron box, and burying it in the soil. She patted down the earth, pressed her hands together, and closed her eyes as if praying.

Praying for what? That spring would arrive sooner? That the flowers would bloom? That her mother would return?

Silly child.

In this world, there is no spring—only a long, biting winter. And flowers bloom only in the gardens of the rich. As for your mother... she died long ago, or worse than death: she abandoned you.

Yet I would not tell her these things. Let her keep that faint hope, like holding onto that packet of seeds that would never sprout.

At least, before freezing to death, she could still dream.

Chapter 4: The Glass Slipper Is an Entry Ticket, the Ball Is a Battlefield
Three years passed like thirty.

The manor was sold to pay off most of the debts. We moved into a small townhouse in the city—three stories with a tiny courtyard. Anastasia's gowns were altered three times because she kept losing weight. Charlotte's medicine was never discontinued, but her complexion improved slightly—because she ate less and worked more. Cinderella... Cinderella had grown taller, but thinner; her golden hair had lost its luster, her hands had turned rough, and her palms bore thin calluses.

She no longer played the piano, drew pictures, or mentioned "Mama." She woke at dawn to light fires, fetch water, sweep, wash clothes, and cook. She learned quickly, like a small plant in a rock crevice that could survive on a bit of soil and water. But she did not speak unless necessary. When looking at people, her gaze was hollow, like a dried-up well.

I told myself: This is good. Only by recognizing reality can one survive.

Yet sometimes late at night, passing the kitchen, I would see her sitting by the hearth, reading an old, tattered music sheet by the dim firelight—retrieved from somewhere unknown, missing pages, turned yellow. Her fingers tapped gently on her knee, silently keeping time. The firelight reflected on her face, and those blue eyes would occasionally flash bright, like unextinguished embers amidst ashes.

Then she would notice, swiftly tuck away the sheet music, and lower her head to continue shelling beans—as if nothing had happened.

I pretended not to see either.

In spring, the royal palace announced a ball to choose a bride for the prince. When the news spread, the whole city boiled. Fabric prices soared, queues formed at jewelry shops, and dance and etiquette tutors were fully booked for the coming month. Anastasia and Charlotte were too excited to sleep, squeezing into my bed at midnight, chattering away:

"Mother, I heard the prince is very handsome!"

"What dress should we wear to the ball? Can last year's be altered to wear again?"

"Invitations... can we get them?"

I patted them, saying, "Yes, we can, for everything." Yet in my heart, I was calculating: two new gowns, at least one hundred francs. Jewelry could be borrowed (Baroness Duval might be willing to help). A carriage rental would cost twenty francs a time. Then there were shoes, gloves, fans...

Another massive expense.

Yet it had to be spent. This was an investment, a gamble—stacking all chips onto the table to bet on an opportunity for social mobility. Win, and Anastasia or Charlotte becomes the princess, elevating the entire family. Lose, and it would merely add another debt; having owed so much already, a bit more made little difference.

I began gathering funds. Pawning the last piece of jewelry—my mother's heirloom ruby brooch. Borrowing from old acquaintances while enduring cold stares and ridicule, I finally scraped enough together. The fabric bought was the most fashionable silk, but I cut and embroidered it myself—my skills remained, and after staying up for seven nights straight until my eyes bled with broken capillaries, I produced two gowns that passed as presentable.

On the eve of the ball, I helped my daughters try on their dresses in their room. Anastasia wore light blue, complementing her gentle nature. Charlotte wore pale gold, appearing lively. They twirled before the mirror, their skirts fanning out like two delicate blossoms.

"Does it look good, Mother?"

"It looks lovely." I adjusted their hair and inserted borrowed pearl hairpins. "My daughters will be the most beautiful at the ball."

Their faces flushed with smiles, their eyes sparkling with light—a youthful brilliance I had not seen in a long time.

I stepped back to look at them, feeling something surging in my chest: pride, heartache, and the tragic resolve of burning one's bridges. I thought, perhaps—perhaps this time, fate might be a bit merciful to us.

Then I saw Cinderella.

She stood in the shadows outside the door holding an iron, having just ironed our gloves. She looked at Anastasia and Charlotte, at those brilliant gowns, and at the sparkling jewelry. Her face showed neither envy nor jealousy, only a deep, near-deadly tranquility.

Yet her eyes—those blue eyes so like her mother's—shone terrifyingly bright in the candlelight.

I walked over and took the gloves. "Go to sleep."

She did not move, merely gazing at me for a long time. Then she spoke, her voice light as a sigh: "Madam, I want to go to the ball too."

I froze.

"What did you say?"

"I want to go to the ball," she repeated, her voice rising slightly, bearing a strange, uncharacteristic firmness. "To see the palace, to see the prince, to dance one dance... just one."

I laughed. Not out of amusement, but because it felt absurd. "Cinderella, do you have any idea what you are saying? That is a royal matchmaking ball; only noble maidens may attend. What is your status?"

"I am a daughter of the Tremaine family," she said, her spine straight. "Father adopted me; legally, I am."

"Legally?" I stepped closer, dropping my voice for fear Anastasia and others might hear. "The law doesn't feed you or clothe you. The law is a game played by the rich. Cinderella, recognize your place—you are a maid, a dependent orphan, a poor wretch surviving on our charity. The ball? Do you think you are worthy?"

The words were venomous, I knew. But they had to be venomous. Her ridiculous illusions had to be shattered so she would stay dutifully in the kitchen and cause no trouble.

Cinderella's face turned pale—pale as paper. But she did not cry; she merely looked at me, and within those blue eyes, something cooled down step by step, freezing into ice.

"I understand," she said, bowing with a posture so standard it resembled that of a true noble maiden—her birth mother had taught her that, as I learned later. "Goodnight, Madam."

She turned to leave, her back straight and her steps steady. Yet as she reached the top of the stairs, I saw it—her hanging hand was tightly clenched, nails digging deep into her palm, blood seeping through her fingers, dropping one by one onto the aged floorboards.

Like tiny red flowers blooming.

I opened my mouth, wanting to call her back, wanting to say something. In the end, no sound came out.

That night, I recorded the ball expenses in the ledger. After writing, I flipped to Cinderella's page and stared at it for a long time. Picking up the quill, I added a line after "Notes":

"Subject exhibits increasingly stubborn tendencies; vigilance required. Lock doors during the ball; prevent contact with the outside world to avoid incidents."

After writing, I put down the pen. The night was quiet; far off, church bells chimed twelve times—ding, ding, ding.

A new day had begun. It was Anastasia and Charlotte's opportunity, and my grand gamble.

As for Cinderella... she would have her own destiny. In the kitchen, in the laundry room, or in the bed of some tenant farmer or craftsman. Perhaps mundane, perhaps grueling, but at least safe.

Safety. For a woman, that was the most luxurious, yet most humble prayer.

I blew out the candle and lay down. Before closing my eyes, the image of Cinderella's bleeding hand flashed across my mind once more.

Then I told myself: Mary, you are not wrong. You are merely fulfilling a mother's duty.

Even if that duty was as cold as tonight's starless sky.

Epilogue: The Other Glass Slipper
You all know the outcome of the ball.

Anastasia and Charlotte were not chosen by the prince. Returning home, they said the prince was cold and distant, dancing only one dance absentmindedly. Instead, a mysterious girl dressed impeccably and dancing exquisitely captured everyone's attention. But she fled at midnight, leaving behind a single glass slipper.

"That slipper was so beautiful," Charlotte lay on the bed, saying gloomily. "Like it was made of stars. If I could wear it just once, I'd die happy."

Anastasia said nothing, merely staring out the window with hollow eyes.

The next day, the entire city searched for the girl who could fit the glass slipper. When the delegation reached our door, my heart nearly leaped out of my chest. Not out of anticipation, but out of terror—I recalled Cinderella's bleeding hand and the gaze in her eyes when she said, "I want to go to the ball too."

Yet how could it be possible? She was locked in the attic, wearing rags, covered in soot. She had no gown, no carriage, no invitation. She had nothing.

Except those feet. Feet that, despite long labor, remained slender and delicate in structure.

I had Anastasia and Charlotte try on the slipper. They squeezed with all their might until their feet swelled and bled, yet they could not fit. I knelt before the royal envoy, weeping: "My lord, give us one more chance, my daughters can do it..."

The envoy waved his hand impatiently: "Next!"

Then, singing echoed from the attic.

Faint and soft—an ancient lullaby. The song Cinderella's mother sang to soothe her to sleep when she was small. I had not heard it for ten years.

Amidst the singing, the attic door opened. Cinderella walked down wearing a dress altered from old curtains, her hair combed neatly, her face smudged with soot, yet her eyes shone bright as burning ice. Barefoot, she walked step by step until she stood before the envoy.

"My lord," she curtsied, her grace putting Anastasia and Charlotte to shame, "may I try?"

The envoy frowned: "Who are you?"

"A daughter of the Tremaine family, Cinderella," she smiled, a smile carrying a fractured beauty. "My father was Viscount Edward Tremaine, and my mother..." She paused, looking at me with a deep, lingering gaze, "my mother was a lady who loved me dearly."

The envoy hesitated, but with the glass slipper before him, he had to let all eligible maidens try. He nodded.

Cinderella sat down and raised her foot. Her foot was dirty, stained with coal dust, bearing scars and calluses. Yet with a gentle slide, her foot slipped into the shoe. Neither big nor small, neither loose nor tight—as if custom-tailored.

Silence fell over the room.

Then, she drew out the other slipper. From her bosom, she slowly retrieved the lost glass slipper. Matching as a pair, they refracted dazzling light under the sunshine.

"Apologies, my lord," she said softly. "I left in a hurry that night and dropped one."

What followed felt like an absurd dream. The prince arrived in person; seeing Cinderella, he embraced her with joy, announcing her as his bride. Carriages, fine gowns, jewelry, and glory flooded into this dilapidated cottage like a tide. Anastasia and Charlotte wept, screamed, and demanded to know, "Why her?"

Meanwhile, I stood in the corner, looking at Cinderella. Wearing silks sent from the palace and adorned with diamonds, her eyes remained fixed on me—looking at me the entire time. Within those blue eyes, there was no triumphant pride nor pleasure of revenge, but a deep emotion I could not comprehend.

She walked over and stopped before me. Then, she did something no one expected.

She knelt. Kneeling before me, she looked up at me.

"Mother," she said, her voice steady and clear, "thank you for these years of... raising me."

She used the word "raising," not "sheltering." She called me "Mother," not "Madam."

Then she reached into her bosom, pulled out an object, and placed it in my palm. It was the packet of flower seeds, wrapped in oil paper, well-preserved though aged.

"Before Father died, he asked me to give this to you," she spoke in a lowered voice. "He said you loved roses most. When spring comes, plant them in the yard; when they bloom, he will smell them in heaven."

I looked down at the packet of seeds. Written on the paper in Edward's handwriting—shaky, yet deliberate line by line:

"To Mary: I'm sorry, and I love you. — Forever your Edward."

I gripped the seeds tightly, the paper packet pressing painfully against my palm. I raised my head, wanting to say something, but my throat felt blocked; not a single word could come out.

Cinderella stood up, turned, and walked toward the prince. The prince took her hand, assisting her into the carriage. In the final moment before the carriage door closed, she turned back and glanced at me.

That glance was brief—brief as a sigh.

That glance was long—long enough that I would see it in my dreams for the rest of my life.

The carriage departed, and the crowd dispersed. Anastasia and Charlotte were still crying, while Betty comforted them. I stood in place, holding the packet of flower seeds and Edward's final words.

Walking back into the study, I sat down and opened the ledger. Turning to the final page, it recorded the ball's expenses, future plans, and all the precise, cold calculations.

Then I picked up the quill and slowly wrote in the blank space:

"Today, Cinderella Tremaine married the prince and became the princess. Return on investment: Incalculable. Note: All my life, I believed in reason, calculation, and survival above all else. I treated marriage as a company, daughters as assets, and emotions as liabilities. I miscalculated the vineyard harvest, the debt interest, and human resilience. Yet my greatest error was failing to calculate this: the girl I viewed as an extra burden carried flower seeds buried in her heart. While I, guarding my ledger, believed it to be the entire world."

Writing to this point, I stopped. Outside the window, the weather cleared. Sunlight streamed in, landing on the ledger; those dense columns of numbers gradually blurred and melted in the light, like snow vanishing in spring.

I closed the ledger and locked it in the drawer. Throwing the key into the hearth, I watched it burn into a twisted piece of metal.

Then I stood up and walked into the courtyard. The soil remained damp, carrying the chill of winter. I crouched down, dug a small hole with my hands, placed the seeds inside, covered them with earth, and patted it down firm.

Betty walked over, asking softly: "Madam, what are you planting?"

"Roses," I said, my voice somewhat raspy. "When spring comes, they will bloom."

She nodded without asking further, turning to resume her chores. I remained crouching, my hand resting on the soil—icy cold, yet underneath, something seemed to be slowly awakening.

Distance brought the sound of bells ringing from the direction of the royal palace. They were celebrating the new princess, fairy-tale love, and the ending of "happily ever after."

And I crouched in this dilapidated courtyard, guarding seeds just planted that might or might not sprout, guarding two daughters unable to marry off, and guarding a half-lifetime of calculations that yielded nothing.

Yet strangely, my heart felt quiet. Quiet as a lake in late autumn, without a single ripple.

Perhaps because I no longer had to calculate.

Perhaps because I finally admitted: some things in this world cannot be calculated. Like Edward's dying words "I love you"—how much was truth, and how much was guilt? Like Cinderella kneeling to call me "Mother"—was there hate in her eyes or not? Like that packet of seeds—did Edward really leave it for me, or was it a lie forged by Cinderella to grant me a final shred of dignity?

Or perhaps, I was simply tired. Too tired to calculate, compete, or prove that I was right, strong, and unyielding.

I stood up and patted the dirt off my hands. Stained black with mud, my hands looked like this half of my life—impossible to wash clean.

Yet I washed them anyway. By the water barrel, I scrubbed my hands again and again. The water was icy, piercing to the bone. By the end, my hands were red and raw, yet dirt remained embedded in the crevices of my nails, stubborn as if grown into the flesh.

Forget it; let it stay.

Consider it a memorial. A memorial to the girl who crawled out of the gutter, believing beauty and calculation could win the entire world. A memorial to the woman who buried love, conscience, and gentleness inside a ledger. A memorial to this beginning of spring, where calculation is finally no longer required.

The breeze blew over, carrying the fragrance of distant gardens. Whose roses they were I did not know, blooming early with a rich, almost cloying sweetness.

I raised my head, closed my eyes, and took a deep breath.

How sweet it smelled.

Sweet as a dream from which one never wakes.

Postscript: Beyond the Ledger
Cinderella's stepmother is framed as a flat antagonist in fairy tales—the antithesis of maidenly fantasy. Yet when placed back into a realistic historical context—18th-century Europe, a widowed noblewoman with two marriageable daughters facing social decline and bankruptcy—her cruelty gains a tragic rationality.

She was not born malicious; she was forced by the era to become an accountant. In an age where women possessed no inheritance or employment rights and could only secure survival resources through marriage, she ran her life like a ledger: calculating returns on every expenditure and measuring value in every emotion. Her mistreatment of Cinderella was fundamentally a survival competition under resource scarcity: a single dowry could only go to her biological child; otherwise, the whole family would plunge into the abyss.

Tragically, she internalized the logic of patriarchal society, becoming an accomplice in oppressing her own gender. What she punished Cinderella with was precisely what society punished her with: beauty had to be exchanged for marriage, and female value had to be defined by men. She viewed the glass slipper as an entry ticket for social mobility, unaware that the slipper locked down the feet of all women.

Cinderella's victory represents both the mercy of fairy tales and the irony of history—she still required a prince to rescue her and marriage to validate her worth. That fitting glass slipper was good fortune, yet also a new cage.

True redemption perhaps began with that packet of flower seeds. It began with the subtle kindness that could still be passed between women beneath calculation and harm. It began with admitting that life is not a ledger; some things cannot be calculated—such as guilt, forgiveness, and the courage to plant roses amidst ruins.

Whether spring will arrive and whether flowers will bloom, I do not know.

At least someone planted the seeds.

At least, beyond the fairy-tale ending and beneath the bubble of "happily ever after," another woman crouches by the dirt, waiting.

Waiting for a tomorrow where she can stand firm without needing a glass slipper.
