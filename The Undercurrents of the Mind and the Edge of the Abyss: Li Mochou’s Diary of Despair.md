# **The Undercurrents of the Mind and the Edge of the Abyss: Li Mochou’s Diary of Despair**

### *A Case Reconstruction through Trauma Psychology and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy*

## **Prologue: Moonlight over Broken-Heart Cliff and a Wound That Never Healed**

The fifteenth night of the sixth month, in the Year of Gui-Mao.
Night.

I am sitting on the cold stone ledge of Broken-Heart Cliff on Mount Zhongnan. The mountain wind has torn a hole in my left sleeve, exposing the scar on my arm—the character *qing* (情), meaning “love,” carved into my flesh with an embroidery needle when I was sixteen.

The ink has long since faded to gray, like a river that has run dry.

Far below lies an abyss so deep that I cannot see its bottom. Clouds churn within it, rising and falling like the thoughts inside my own mind.

Yang Guo calls me a madwoman. Guo Xiang calls me Li Mochou.

Neither of them knows that “Mochou” was my given name, chosen by my master. *Do not worry.* That was what the name was meant to mean.

Now it feels like a cruel joke.

I became the “Red Serpent Immortal,” a woman said to have bloodied her hands with more than three hundred lives. And yet, for all my power, I fear the dark more than I ever did.

My martial arts?

I know more techniques than the seven disciples of the Quanzhen Sect combined. The Ancient Tomb School’s *Whisk Technique* can snap a tree as thick as a bowl. My *Five-Poison Palm* once shattered the protective armor of the Golden Wheel Monk.

Yet none of these skills can buy me a single night of peaceful sleep.

Everyone says I am “consumed by love.”

But love is not what imprisons me.

It is the invisible blade inside my heart—the blade that has been cutting me for twenty years and still has not stopped drawing blood.

Tonight the moon is pale as frost.

I have taken out the blunt writing brush I keep with me, one I salvaged from a burned Taoist bundle. I dip it into the dew and write these words upon the stone.

Perhaps someday someone will read them.

Perhaps then they will understand why a woman who became nearly invincible in martial arts turned her life into a battlefield.

---

# **I. An Old Wound Reopened: The Fire of Lu Manor and the Thorn in My Heart**

> “When the fire began to rise, I heard Lu Zhanyuan laughing.”

Tonight I burned another residence bearing the Lu family lantern.

The fire began in the stable behind the house. I stood upon the roof ridge, an Ice-Poison Needle between my fingers, watching the flames climb toward the main building with the wind.

People screamed.

They cursed me.

“Red Serpent Immortal! Madwoman! Monster!”

I laughed coldly.

I flicked my whisk, and drops of blood scattered across the roof tiles. They looked strangely like the red silk decorations at Lu Manor’s wedding hall all those years ago.

As the flames rose into the sky, I suddenly remembered the spring when I was twenty.

There had been another fire.

The wedding banners at Lu Manor had burned red. He Yuanjun was running through the flames in her red dress.

I chased her.

My silver needle entered her back.

She turned and looked at me.

There was no hatred in her eyes.

Only relief.

“Why?” I screamed.

My voice disappeared beneath the roar of the fire.

She never answered.

Blood flowed from the corner of her mouth and stained her red dress even darker.

Later, outside the Ancient Tomb, I knelt beside the stream and washed my hands.

The blood dissolved into the water like a dying peony.

My master once told me:

> “Mochou, your temper is too fierce. One day it will wound you more deeply than anyone else can.”

I did not understand her then.

I understand now.

But now it is too late to turn back.

I opened the old handkerchief I always carry.

Embroidered upon it are half of a line:

> *“Tell me, what is love in this world…”*

Lu Zhanyuan gave it to me.

He said he would tell me the second half when he returned.

He never returned.

The corner of the handkerchief has worn thin. A loose thread pierced my fingertip.

The pain was sharp.

Perhaps some wounds simply never heal.

---

# **II. Martial Arts as Armor and Prison**

> “I learned martial arts not because I wanted to kill, but because I never wanted to be killed again.”

My master used to say that I possessed extraordinary talent, that I was born to master martial arts.

She did not know why I trained so desperately.

At first, I trained simply because I wanted to protect myself.

When I was seven, bandits raided our home.

I hid inside a rice jar.

Through a narrow crack, I watched my father’s head being cut from his body. Blood splashed across the stove like some hideous flower.

My mother pushed me deeper into the jar.

“Do not make a sound. Wait here until your father comes back.”

But Father never came back.

For the next three years I wandered at the bottom of the martial world—begging, starving, running, struggling simply to survive.

I learned one lesson:

No one will protect you.

You can only protect yourself.

It was not until I was ten that my master took me into the Ancient Tomb.

There, I trained day and night.

I shivered upon the Cold Jade Bed until my limbs went numb, yet refused to climb down.

When I practiced the Ice-Poison Needles, my hands were covered with puncture wounds. Blood stained the needles red.

I never cried.

I wanted to become strong.

Strong enough that no one could ever hurt me again.

But when I finally became powerful, I discovered something was wrong.

Martial arts could protect me from swords.

They could not protect me from people's eyes.

Fear.

Disgust.

Contempt.

They looked at me as though I were a poisonous snake.

Psychology calls one related phenomenon **projective identification** (Klein, 1946).

The more people saw me as a monster, the more I began to believe that I was supposed to become one.

If you insist that I am a demon, then I will become the demon you fear.

I used cruelty to disguise vulnerability.

I used killing to conceal fear.

Every time I struck with the Five-Poison Palm, part of me seemed to be shouting at those bandits from my childhood:

> “Look at me now. This time, you are the ones who are afraid.”

But the cycle has no end.

The more people I kill, the more people hate me.

The more people hate me, the more convinced I become that I must kill them to remain safe.

It is a knot I cannot untie.

---

# **III. Attachment Trauma: When Trust Becomes Poison**

> “He tore the silk handkerchief I had given him in two and said, ‘We are not meant for each other.’”

Last night I dreamed of that spring when I was twenty.

I had found Lu Zhanyuan wounded at the foot of Mount Zhongnan.

When he opened his eyes, he said:

> “Are you an immortal descended from heaven?”

For three months, I broke the rules of the Ancient Tomb School and cared for him near the Tomb of the Living Dead.

He promised to marry me.

He promised to take me to see the camellias of Dali and the misty rain of Jiangnan.

I gave him half of the most precious silk handkerchief in our school—the half embroidered with the character *Mochou*.

Then he left.

And disappeared.

A year later I descended from the mountain to find him.

That was when I learned that he had married He Yuanjun.

Three months after we parted.

I stood outside Lu Manor listening to the wedding music.

The entire world seemed to be laughing at my foolishness.

I killed two of He Yuanjun’s maids with my needles.

But when I faced him, I could not kill him.

He knelt before me.

“Mochou,” he said, “I have wronged you.”

I did not kill him.

I turned and walked away.

Behind me, something inside my heart broke.

Attachment theory (Bowlby, 1969) proposes that early attachment experiences can shape emotional patterns throughout life.

My parents disappeared when I needed them.

Then the man I trusted betrayed me after promising to love me.

Two wounds created a core belief:

> **No one can be trusted. Trust only leads to pain.**

So I began keeping everyone at a distance.

Hong Lingbo followed me for ten years.

I never once smiled at her.

Anyone in the martial world who tried to approach me was assumed to have an ulterior motive.

I built walls out of indifference.

I did not realize that walls keep other people out—and imprison the person who built them.

---

# **IV. Cognitive Distortions: A Martial World Divided into Black and White**

> “In my eyes, there are only two kinds of people: enemies and potential enemies.”

The martial world says I kill the innocent indiscriminately.

They are right.

And they are not.

I kill three kinds of people:

Those named Lu.

Those named He.

And anyone who stands in my way.

Psychology calls this **all-or-nothing thinking**, or dichotomous thinking (Beck, 1976).

It makes life simple.

No judgment.

No hesitation.

No uncertainty.

See an enemy.

Kill the enemy.

Cheng Ying and Lu Wushuang—I hunted those two girls for years.

Sometimes I asked myself:

What did they actually do wrong?

Were they guilty simply because they were Lu Zhanyuan's nieces?

But I could not stop.

Because stopping would mean admitting that I had been wrong.

And I could not be wrong.

If I was wrong, then what had these twenty years of hatred been for?

This is related to **cognitive dissonance reduction** (Festinger, 1957).

When behavior conflicts with self-image, the mind modifies its interpretation of reality to restore consistency.

I had to believe that they deserved to die.

I had to believe that I was administering justice.

But late at night, when I am alone, a small voice still asks:

> “Are you sure?”

Once, in Jiaxing, I killed a scholar merely because his name contained the character *Yuan*, which reminded me of He Yuanjun.

The villagers surrounded me and called me a demon.

I felt strangely wronged.

“I am merely enforcing justice!”

That self-deception became a kind of armor.

It allowed me to sleep at night.

Even when my dreams were filled with blood.

---

# **V. Emotional Dysregulation: Anger as My Painkiller**

> “Anger hurts less than grief. At least anger makes me feel powerful.”

Today I encountered Guo Jing and Huang Rong outside Xiangyang.

Huang Rong looked at me and asked:

> “Daoist Li, are you not tired of living like this?”

I did not answer.

I turned and walked away.

But her question lodged inside me like a thorn.

Of course I am tired.

Anger is one of the most exhausting emotions there is.

And yet I live inside it every day.

I need anger the way a wounded person needs morphine.

Anger makes me feel strong.

For a moment, it silences the little girl crying inside me.

Psychological research has long suggested that anger can function as a secondary emotion, masking more vulnerable primary emotions such as sadness, fear, and shame (Greenberg, 2002).

Beneath my anger is shame.

Shame at being abandoned.

Shame at being betrayed.

Shame at being vulnerable.

Whenever I think of Lu Zhanyuan, anger comes before grief.

Anger is easier.

Anger gives me the strength to kill and burn.

Grief would simply leave me lying helpless upon the ground.

So I cultivated my anger.

I fed it.

I sharpened it.

I made it my weapon.

But every weapon has two edges.

Anger protects me.

It also isolates me.

No one dares approach someone who is angry all the time.

Not even I dare approach the person I really am beneath that anger.

---

# **VI. Identity Crisis: Who Am I?**

> “Red Serpent Immortal. Demoness. Murderous witch. None of these names did I choose for myself.”

The martial world has given me countless names.

None of them belongs to me.

Sometimes I wonder:

If Lu Zhanyuan had never betrayed me, what would I be now?

Perhaps I would be living in some quiet town in Jiangnan, married, raising children.

Perhaps I would still be inside the Ancient Tomb, obeying its rules.

Either way, I would not be the woman I am now.

Erikson (1968) argued that early adulthood involves a fundamental struggle between intimacy and isolation.

I failed that struggle.

I never established a healthy intimate relationship.

I never developed a stable sense of self.

My identity became defined entirely by trauma:

> **I am the woman who was abandoned. Therefore, the people who abandoned me must pay.**

But what is the price?

Lu Zhanyuan has been dead for years.

Yet I still hunt his relatives.

Am I really seeking revenge?

Or do I simply need a reason to remain alive?

Recently I have dreamed the same dream again and again.

I stand in a vast white field of snow.

Mirrors surround me in every direction.

Every mirror contains a different version of myself.

There is the little girl I once was.

There is the young woman who met Lu Zhanyuan.

There is the woman I have become.

They accuse one another.

They scream.

They fight.

When I wake, my clothes are soaked in sweat.

Because I do not know which one of them is the real me.

---

# **VII. The Vicious Cycle of Social Exclusion**

> “They gave me the label of ‘demoness,’ and then turned me into the person the label described.”

The martial world is a strange place.

It worships violence while preaching righteousness.

My martial arts are strong enough to place me among the great masters.

But my methods have forever excluded me from respectable society.

Those so-called righteous sects speak endlessly of compassion.

Their hands are not necessarily cleaner than mine.

They kill with acceptable reasons:

“Defending justice.”

“Destroying evil.”

“Avenging the dead.”

“Purging the sect.”

I kill for reasons too.

The difference is that the martial world refuses to recognize my reasons.

Social psychology describes a related process through **labeling theory** (Becker, 1963).

Once a person is labeled in a particular way, subsequent behavior tends to be interpreted through that label.

Once I became the “demoness,” everything I did was interpreted as evil.

I once saved Cheng Ying.

I regretted it almost immediately.

The rumor afterward was that I intended to raise her only so that I could torture her later.

When I spared someone, people said I was merely “playing with my prey.”

Their hostility eventually convinced me that the entire world truly was my enemy.

So I became harsher.

More violent.

More cruel.

And that only confirmed their judgment.

It is a cycle from which I cannot escape.

Sometimes I even wonder whether some part of me secretly protects the reputation of “demoness.”

Because the demoness may be hated—

but she is powerful.

She is feared.

That is better than being the abandoned, pathetic Li Mochou.

---

# **VIII. Emotional Projection and Displaced Aggression**

> “Their happiness is a mirror that reflects everything missing from me.”

Today I passed a village.

I saw a young husband and wife working in the fields.

The man wiped the sweat from the woman's forehead.

She smiled.

Suddenly I became furious.

I struck my whisk against three willow trees and broke them.

I knew there was no reason for it.

Yet I could not stop myself.

Every time I see a loving couple, I remember Lu Zhanyuan and He Yuanjun.

Every time I see a devoted master and disciple, I think of Xiaolongnü and Yang Guo.

Their happiness is a mirror.

And the mirror reflects my emptiness.

Psychology describes related processes as **emotional projection** (Freud, 1915) and **displaced aggression** (Dollard et al., 1939).

I cannot attack Lu Zhanyuan anymore.

He is dead.

I cannot attack the past.

So I redirect my anger toward people and situations that remind me of what I lost.

But aggression never truly resolves pain.

It is like drinking seawater when you are thirsty.

The more you drink, the thirstier you become.

The more people I kill, the more villages I burn, the larger the emptiness inside me becomes.

That emptiness was left behind when Lu Zhanyuan walked away.

For twenty years I have tried to fill it with hatred.

But hatred is like loose cotton.

It looks like a great deal.

In truth, it fills nothing.

Once I caught Lu Wushuang.

She cried:

> “Kill me. There is no point in my living anyway.”

For a moment I saw something familiar in her eyes.

That same despair I had once carried.

I could not kill her.

Because I saw my younger self in her.

But I still wounded her.

Then I walked away.

I could not afford compassion.

Compassion would mean admitting that we were alike.

And I refused to admit that.

---

# **IX. The Compulsion to Repeat Trauma**

> “I kept repeating the pattern of trust and betrayal without realizing that I was designing tests that could never be passed.”

Recently I have noticed something disturbing.

My attitude toward young men is deeply contradictory.

On the one hand, I distrust them completely.

I assume they will all betray me as Lu Zhanyuan did.

On the other hand, whenever I encounter a young man who resembles him in manner or appearance, I find myself looking twice.

The contradiction torments me.

My reason says that no one can be trusted.

My emotions still long for connection.

That longing embarrasses me.

So I cover it with even greater hatred.

Freud (1920) described a phenomenon known as the **compulsion to repeat**: people may unconsciously recreate patterns associated with early trauma, perhaps in an attempt to gain mastery over an experience they were previously unable to control.

I have spent years repeating the same pattern:

Trust.

Betrayal.

Anger.

Revenge.

I test people.

I set impossible conditions.

When they inevitably fail, I say:

> “See? No one is worthy of trust.”

But what if I am the one designing the impossible test?

Consider Hong Lingbo.

I tested her loyalty by forcing her to choose between her life and obedience.

Whatever she chose, I could interpret it as insufficient loyalty.

That was not a test.

It was a trap designed to prove what I already believed.

A self-fulfilling prophecy.

Sometimes I envy Xiaolongnü.

She too suffered betrayal.

But she had Yang Guo.

She had Guo Xiang.

She had people willing to sacrifice themselves for her.

I pushed everyone away.

Did I choose loneliness?

Or did loneliness choose me?

---

# **X. The Final Reflection: What Is Love in This World?**

> “Did I really love Lu Zhanyuan—or did I love the version of myself who was loved for the first time?”

The moon is full tonight.

It looks exactly like the moon on the night Lu Zhanyuan left.

I take out the old handkerchief and run my fingers over the faded embroidery:

> *“Tell me, what is love in this world,*
> *that it should bind life and death together?”*

The lines come from Yuan Haowen.

I have always known the first line.

The second line I never truly understood.

It is not that I do not understand the words.

I do not understand the kind of love that can make a person willing to live—or die—for another.

Did I really love Lu Zhanyuan?

Or did I love the woman I became when someone finally made me feel cherished?

If he had never betrayed me, would we truly have been happy?

I do not know.

I never will.

For twenty years I used hatred to memorialize love.

I used killing to mourn my youth.

I turned my entire life into a protest against his betrayal.

But I never asked the simplest question:

> **Was it worth it?**

Martial arts gave me strength.

They did not give me wisdom.

They gave me security.

They did not give me peace.

I can defeat the greatest fighters in the world.

I cannot defeat the demon inside my own heart.

I can make hundreds of people fear me.

I cannot make myself happy.

Perhaps Huang Rong was right.

I am tired.

So tired that sometimes I wonder:

If I had died beneath the bandits' blades when I was seven, would that have been better?

At least that little girl was still whole.

She still believed that goodness existed.

She still believed that people could love one another.

What do I believe now?

Nothing.

I believe only in the whisk in my hand and the needles hidden in my sleeve.

They are my choice.

They are also my prison.

Under the moonlight, I place the handkerchief into the stream.

It floats away like an autumn leaf.

I do not follow it.

I simply watch until it disappears into the darkness.

Perhaps one day I will understand the second half of the poem.

Perhaps one day I will discover a reason to live that is not hatred.

But tonight I am still the Red Serpent Immortal.

Still the demoness feared throughout the martial world.

This is my role.

My mask.

My armor.

My prison.

When the sun rises tomorrow, I will continue hunting those who bear the surname Lu.

I will continue fighting the entire martial world.

Because I do not know what else to do.

What is love in this world?

I do not know.

I truly do not know.

# **Afterword: A Psychological Interpretation**

The case of Li Mochou can be read as a fictionalized intersection of **post-traumatic stress symptoms, borderline personality traits, attachment trauma, and prolonged grief**. It is important, however, to distinguish literary reconstruction from clinical diagnosis: Li Mochou is a fictional character, and the psychological formulation here is interpretive rather than diagnostic.

Her tragedy lies partly in the mismatch between the problems she is trying to solve and the tools she uses to solve them.

She uses **problem-solving strategies to address emotional wounds**.

She trains in martial arts to solve the problem of vulnerability.

She pursues revenge to solve the problem of abandonment.

She uses anger to solve the problem of grief.

She uses isolation to solve the problem of betrayal.

None of these strategies can address the underlying wound.

Martial arts can defeat an external enemy, but they cannot heal an internal one.

Hatred can provide a temporary sense of power, but it gradually consumes the very life it was meant to protect.

From a psychological perspective, meaningful recovery would require at least three major processes:

### **1. Mourning what was lost**

Li Mochou would need to grieve not only Lu Zhanyuan, but also the future she once imagined with him—and the younger version of herself who believed she could be loved.

### **2. Revising maladaptive cognitive schemas**

Beliefs such as:

> “Everyone will betray me.”

> “Trust inevitably leads to pain.”

> “If I stop fighting, I will be destroyed.”

would need to be examined and gradually replaced by more flexible interpretations.

### **3. Rebuilding healthy attachment**

Perhaps the most difficult step would be learning that vulnerability does not necessarily equal weakness, and that relationships need not follow the pattern of abandonment and betrayal.

Unfortunately, within Jin Yong's martial world, Li Mochou never encounters the conditions necessary for such reconstruction.

Her story therefore becomes a psychological tragedy:

> **She acquired extraordinary power without acquiring emotional safety.**

The stronger she became externally, the more isolated she became internally.

The prison was not merely the martial world.

It was the psychological structure she constructed from her wounds.

And perhaps that is the deepest meaning of her tragedy:

**The strongest prisons are sometimes built from the very defenses that once saved us.**

The key to opening them, however, is not hidden in the past.

It lies in what can still be chosen in the present.
