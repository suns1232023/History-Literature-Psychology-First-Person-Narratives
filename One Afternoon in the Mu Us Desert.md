# One Afternoon in the Mu Us Desert

### — Ronald Sakolsky

**May 19, 2026**
**Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania — My Home**

When Bai Fan sent me the photographs, it was still dark outside.

It was May in Pennsylvania. A thin mist hung beyond the windows. My wife was still asleep.

I was sitting alone in my study, the blue glow of an old computer washing across the white wall.

On the screen was a landscape I had never truly seen before.

It was not merely a photograph.

It was the guilt I had buried in the sand twenty-six years ago.

A forest.

An endless forest.

The Mu Us Desert had turned green.

Not green in the way television had once shown it—one or two lonely trees standing on a dune, looking as though someone had forgotten to take them away.

This was a real forest.

A vast canopy stretching toward the horizon.

When the wind passed through it, I could almost hear the sound.

Not the dry, abrasive scream of grain upon grain of sand.

But leaves brushing against leaves.

A soft rustling.

I held my pen for a long time.

I discovered that I had nothing to say.

---

## I

**Luoyang, Henan — Autumn, 1999**

I was a teacher.

An ordinary one.

For nearly thirty years, I taught social studies and history at a high school outside Pittsburgh. I took students again and again through the Industrial Revolution, the Cold War, the fall of the Berlin Wall.

I had asked myself countless times:

*How many of the things you teach have you actually experienced?*

Most of the time, the answer was:

Very few.

So I came to China.

Choosing Luoyang was not an accident.

I did not want to stay in one of the glamorous coastal cities.

I wanted to see houses made of mud.

I wanted to see what the backs of people working in the fields looked like beneath the sun.

I wanted to eat what actually appeared on Chinese family dinner tables—not the theatrical version of Chinese food carried out by waiters in five-star hotels.

I explained this decision over and over to my students.

They stared at me with wide eyes.

For the first few months after arriving in Luoyang, everything went according to plan.

Teach.

Go home.

Walk around on weekends.

Look.

Listen.

Luoyang had been the capital of thirteen dynasties. At the Longmen Grottoes, the stone Buddhas had silently watched fifteen centuries of wind and rain.

When I stood beneath the great Vairocana Buddha and looked upward, I suddenly felt that my forty-three years of life were very small.

My worries—

the mortgage,

retirement savings,

professional evaluations,

complaints from parents—

all seemed faintly ridiculous beneath the Buddha's tranquil gaze.

But even that was not enough.

I was still eating respectable Chinese food.

I was still living in a comfortable apartment.

The dollars in my pocket, once exchanged for yuan, allowed me to live considerably better than most Chinese people around me.

I wore a clean white shirt into the classroom.

My students stood in neat uniforms and said:

> “Good morning, Mr. S.”

It was not a bad life.

But it was not what I had crossed half the world to find.

What exactly was I looking for?

I did not know.

Only much later did I understand:

Sometimes a person does something enormous not because he knows where he is going,

but because he can no longer bear to remain where he is.

---

## II

The appearance of that woman on television came without warning.

One day toward the end of 1999, I was sitting in my apartment watching CCTV's English-language programming.

Watching the news on CCTV had become one of my habits in China.

A documentary segment appeared on the screen.

It was about a woman farmer in Inner Mongolia named **Yin Yuzhen**.

Then came the images of the Mu Us Desert.

I had read stories about deserts before.

The Sahara.

The Gobi.

The Taklamakan.

Books described deserts as magnificent, mysterious places that stirred the imagination—

star-filled skies,

campfires,

the distant bells of camel caravans.

But that was the kind of romantic nonsense white intellectuals could write while sitting in air-conditioned rooms drinking iced coffee.

The desert on that screen was different.

It was something that could swallow everything.

From one edge of the frame to the other, sky and earth dissolved into a vast, ochre-colored haze.

The wind lifted dust into rolling walls of brown.

The camera slowly moved closer.

A tiny black speck appeared at the bottom of the frame.

Smaller.

Smaller.

Smaller.

Then the image settled.

A woman.

A thin woman in a faded dark-blue cotton jacket.

She bent over the sand and began digging.

The sand was so loose that every shallow trench she opened immediately collapsed.

She shoveled the sand aside, laid damp sand into the bottom of the trench, and worked again.

One shovel.

Then another.

Each movement awkward and stubborn, like someone physically wrestling with despair.

The saplings were willow cuttings.

They were barely thicker than the woman's fingers.

Tender.

Shaking.

Placed against that roaring sea of sand, they looked almost absurd.

She set one into the damp soil.

Pressed the sand firmly around it.

Then stepped back.

The camera moved between the woman and the tiny tree.

The contrast was almost unbearable.

Human being against desert.

Life against the earth.

A single will against an entire landscape.

The narrator began telling her story.

She had married into Inner Mongolia at nineteen.

Her bridal home was a dugout half-buried in sand.

On the second night of her marriage, a sandstorm buried the entrance.

She and her husband could not push the door open.

Only later did they realize that all four walls of their home were made of sand.

She did not cry.

At least, not in front of the camera.

She simply lowered her head and kept digging.

One shovel.

Then another.

As though she were doing the most ordinary thing in the world.

> **“I'd rather work myself to death planting trees than let the sand bully me to death.”**

That sentence stayed with me.

For years, I repeated it silently to myself.

The program then showed her husband.

He was ravaged by lung disease.

His face was gaunt.

His body had become painfully thin.

Yet on days without a sandstorm, he still forced himself outside.

And Yin Yuzhen continued walking across the cracked earth.

Her figure looked like a blade of grass that had been flattened by a storm—

and somehow stood upright again.

Something inside me broke.

It was not a moment of being “moved.”

That word was far too small.

It was a coldness that rose through the cracks between my bones.

Almost a violent form of self-examination.

*What right do you have to sit in this comfortable apartment, wearing an immaculate shirt, drinking tea in the afternoon, and tell yourself that these people simply “love China so much”?*

They were human beings.

People exactly like you and me.

A rural family earning less than two hundred and fifty dollars a year was buying saplings with their own money and fighting the sand, inch by inch, in one of the least hospitable places on earth.

Not because anyone ordered them to.

Not because some government program demanded it.

Not because someone promised them a reward.

But because this was her land.

Because she had married into it.

Because she had nowhere else to go.

I stood up from the sofa.

I remained in the middle of the room for a long time.

That night I could not sleep.

Several thoughts kept circling through my mind.

*You have been in this world for forty-three years.*

*What have you done?*

*Apart from teaching the next generation how to understand the world, what have you actually created?*

*Do you deserve to live on this earth?*

Those questions sound grandiose now.

But at the time my thoughts were much more concrete.

For example:

I knew that the family might have spent only a few hundred dollars that year buying saplings.

And I happened to have a small amount of money in my bank account.

An amount that was, for me, almost insignificant.

---

## III

What happened next is easy to describe.

Doing it was almost impossible.

I decided to raise money for Yin Yuzhen.

When I said this aloud, it sounded almost weightless.

An American teacher working in Henan wanted to raise money for a Chinese woman farmer.

It was hardly headline material.

But no one knew what those months were like.

From October 1999 to January 2000, I shut myself inside my tiny apartment in Luoyang and tried almost every method I could think of to raise the money.

During the day, I had to teach.

I could not let my students down.

They were my responsibility.

A promise, once made, had to be kept.

I would finish grading the final papers before the students left.

Then, after the classroom emptied, I returned to my apartment, switched on a desktop computer so slow it was almost unbearable, and connected to the Internet through dial-up.

The telephone line made that familiar crackling, static-filled sound.

After the connection finally went through, it could take several seconds before even a single letter appeared in the browser.

The days repeated themselves hundreds of times.

Search.

Investigate.

Write emails.

Send.

Wait.

Rejected.

Search again.

Investigate again.

I contacted environmental organizations in the United States.

Churches.

Foundations.

Even an alumni foundation at a university.

Again and again I tried to persuade them.

I described the woman in the Mu Us Desert.

I used English.

Photographs.

Blurry screenshots taken from television footage.

I tried to convince people that this was real.

That it was not one of those classic advertisements about famine in Africa.

Not some fabricated statistics from a pyramid scheme.

Not a story designed to manipulate donors.

Dozens of letters disappeared into silence.

Perhaps hundreds.

The replies I did receive were few.

Some politely explained:

> “The region falls outside our funding area.”

Others said:

> “Our institutional budget has already been allocated.”

Others simply told me:

> “Please complete the application form on our website and await review.”

Thank you for your support.

Thank you.

Thank you.

Thank you.

So many nights I sat beside the window.

The lights of Luoyang were sparse and scattered.

Nothing like Pittsburgh.

Yet there was something about that simplicity—

the quietness,

the plainness,

the scarcity of this inland Chinese city—

that made me wake every morning with an absurd sense of shame.

I had come to China because I wanted to experience life here.

And when I finally encountered real poverty, I found myself humiliated by my own inability to do anything about it.

But I held on.

Not because I was noble.

Not because I possessed some extraordinary spirit of sacrifice.

Simply because—

**once you have walked into that desert and seen that woman's face, you cannot pretend nothing happened.**

If you turn around and walk away,

you will never be able to look at yourself the same way again.

Three months of struggle.

Countless closed doors.

Countless variations of:

> “Thank you, but unfortunately we cannot provide support.”

Finally, an organization in Boston, Massachusetts, agreed to transfer the entire amount.

The night I received the confirmation email, I locked myself inside my apartment.

I took the last beer from my refrigerator.

Held the bottle to my mouth.

And drank for a long time before swallowing.

Five thousand dollars.

For an American teacher, it was not a fortune.

For a rural Chinese family earning less than two hundred and fifty dollars a year,

it was enough to change the course of a life.

The experience forced me to confront a question for the first time:

**Why had I gone through all this trouble?**

At the time, I did not completely know.

I only knew that if I had walked away—

if I had given up before finding someone willing to help—

then my entire year in China would have become one enormous irony.

I would have come to this country.

Seen its suffering.

And then allowed that suffering to disappear into the depths of some forgotten browser tab.

That would have been a desecration of everything everyone else had sacrificed.

---

## IV

**May 2000 — Wushen Banner, Inner Mongolia**

I took a train from Luoyang to Hohhot.

From Hohhot, I traveled for several more hours to Wushen Banner.

The local government arranged someone to receive me.

Yin Yuzhen was still working in the desert.

When our vehicle entered the heart of the Mu Us Desert—

when a sandstorm in the distance twisted itself into a brown vortex—

when I looked through the window and saw yellow sand covering the roadbed, vegetation so sparse that it almost seemed nonexistent—

something strange filled my mind.

*Do people really live here?*

This was not the Mu Us Desert I had seen on television.

In fact,

television had beautified everything.

What the naked eye saw was much harsher.

The sky was yellow-gray, dissolving into the horizon.

Endless dunes stretched in every direction.

The wind had carved fine ripples across their surfaces.

There were no trees.

No villages.

Almost no evidence of human life.

Occasionally we passed a dead shrub, its roots exposed to the wind, half buried in sand, the other half stubbornly reaching upward.

I was told that Yin Yuzhen's home was not far away.

The vehicle stopped somewhere.

Someone led me across a stretch of trackless sand.

My shoes sank into it.

Every step felt like stepping into liquid.

When I pulled my foot free, a small cloud of dust rose with it.

And then I saw her.

The woman from the television.

She was wearing faded cotton clothes.

Her short hair was blown into a wild tangle by the wind.

She had just risen from beside a shallow water pit.

Both hands were covered in black sand and mud.

She was carrying a large bucket.

For one second, I had no idea what I was thinking.

My body moved before my mind could catch up.

I ran toward her.

Sand poured into my shoes.

I stumbled.

But I kept going.

Those few dozen steps felt endless.

Then I opened my arms

and embraced her.

Her body stiffened slightly.

Her arms remained at her sides.

She did not embrace me back.

Her eyes lowered.

I could feel her hands trembling.

Only later did I learn that in China, a woman would not normally embrace a man in public—

certainly not a foreign man she had never met, with whom she could not even share a language.

But in that second, none of that mattered.

Something had been building inside my chest.

If I did not hold her,

all of those emotions accumulated over the preceding months—

guilt,

shock,

shame,

helplessness—

would explode inside me.

Someone took a photograph.

My arms were open around her.

I do not know what my face looked like.

But I know what that embrace must have looked like.

It did not resemble courtesy.

It did not resemble charity.

It looked more like a drowning man clutching a piece of driftwood.

**I needed that embrace.**

Do you understand?

**I needed it desperately.**

---

## V

Even after nearly half a lifetime, I still remember every detail of that day.

She led me along a stretch of sand to see the dugout where she had lived for fourteen years.

It was built from mud walls, half buried in the sand.

So simple that looking at it almost hurt.

When I pushed open the door, darkness filled the room.

The earthen walls were etched with patterns left by wind and sand.

She bent down to cross the threshold.

I followed.

Inside, I had to stoop beneath the low ceiling.

The furnishings were painfully simple.

A table.

Several clay jars.

A small earthen kang.

Quilts folded neatly.

On the table sat a small bowl of chili oil and a dish of salt.

I did not want to ask:

*You lived here for fourteen years?*

The question would have been meaningless.

Because she had.

She showed me the surrounding land.

Through digging, carrying, planting, and hauling by hand, Yin Yuzhen and her husband had planted nearly forty thousand mu of trees.

I looked where she pointed.

There really were small green shoots scattered across the sand.

Tiny.

Like weeds that had only just broken through the soil.

They curled inward against the wind, looking terribly vulnerable.

Farther away, enormous stretches of land remained completely bare.

Yellow.

Scorched.

The wind crossed the entire desert without resistance, leaving rolling tracks in the sand.

My first thought was:

**This cannot possibly work.**

That was not pessimism.

It was a rational assessment of reality.

The environment was too harsh.

Any practical engineer, agronomist, or ecologist standing there would probably have shaken his head.

I did not say it.

I did not want to stand in front of her like some foreigner who had come all this way merely to criticize.

But she must have seen it in my face.

Because she said nothing.

She simply looked toward those tiny green shoots.

There was an expression on her face I still cannot quite describe.

You could call it certainty.

You could call it faith.

Or, more simply,

it was the expression of a woman driven to the edge of a cliff by fate,

who had no choice but to keep moving forward,

to keep planting trees.

I have traveled to many places.

I have met all kinds of people.

But no one has ever made me understand more clearly than Yin Yuzhen how much weight human resilience can carry.

Fourteen years.

More than five thousand days and nights.

If it had been me,

could I have endured it?

The answer is:

I do not know.

Probably not.

---

## VI

The day before I left, she did something that still leaves me speechless whenever I remember it.

It was late at night.

I was sleeping in a temporary place Bai Fan had arranged for me.

I no longer remember whether it was a guesthouse or the home of a village official.

I was half asleep when I suddenly heard someone knocking.

I opened the door.

Yin Yuzhen was standing there.

She was wearing a coarse cotton shirt.

And in her hands she held—

a pair of insoles.

They were completely handmade.

The stitching was so fine that I could hardly see a single uneven thread.

The base cloth was deep red.

Embroidered upon it was a pink lotus with green veins running through its leaves.

There had been no printed pattern.

No machine embroidery.

Every stitch had been made by hand beneath an oil lamp, over an unknown number of nights.

I stood in the doorway.

She placed the insoles in my hands.

She could not speak English.

I could not speak Chinese.

We simply looked at each other in silence.

For a long moment, her fingers kept moving across the surface of the insoles, as though she were checking whether the stitches had gone crooked, whether the pattern was neat enough.

Then, finally, she released them.

She pushed the insoles more firmly into my hands.

She bowed deeply.

And walked back into the darkness.

I stood there.

The warmth of her palms still lingered on the cloth.

Suddenly my nose burned.

My eyes filled.

No one knew—

not even me, because I was unwilling to admit it at the time—

that what overwhelmed me in that moment was more than gratitude.

It was shame.

Something deeper.

Something I did not know how to face.

I was forty-three years old.

And perhaps no one had ever made something like this for me.

Not because I was unworthy.

But because in my own culture, almost no one would express gratitude in this way—

by spending night after night hand-stitching a pair of shoe insoles.

You cannot understand the weight of that gift unless you have stood on that sand.

Unless you have seen her hands.

Hands worn hard by the wooden handle of a shovel.

Hands roughened by years of water, sand, and mud until they resembled the bark of an old elm.

Hands cracked open by the cold.

White adhesive tape wrapped around the wounds.

**And those same hands held a silver needle in the darkness and embroidered a lotus.**

The value of the gift had nothing to do with the price of the insoles.

Its value lay in this:

A person living in extreme material poverty and profound physical exhaustion had taken the small amount of time and energy she possessed and spent it trying, with everything she had, to express gratitude to someone on the other side of the ocean.

On the morning I left, a light wind moved across the desert.

Dust drifted through the air.

When I had arrived, I had been thinking about achievement.

About self-worth.

About whether I could do something meaningful for the world.

When I left,

all that remained was guilt.

I felt like a worm hiding in the bottom of a ship's hold during a storm.

Because I had given too little.

I had paid for the seeds of fifty thousand trees.

But I would never experience the labor of planting them.

The exchange was fundamentally unequal.

Yet when Yin Yuzhen pressed those handmade insoles into my hands, I suddenly understood something.

In her eyes,

there was no inequality between us.

I was simply someone who had helped her.

And in her tiny world, imprisoned in the middle of the yellow desert, there were so few things she could give in return.

So she gave what she could.

Still,

I felt like a thief.

I had taken something precious from her.

Not money.

Not a material possession.

A piece of human relationship.

I would never know how much thought she had given to it.

How many sleepless nights.

How much care.

How much of herself had gone into those stitches.

I could never fully comprehend it.

---

## VII

Why did I do all of this?

Decades later, sitting in my home in Pittsburgh, staring at photographs of that endless green forest, I still think about the question.

Later, I read about a concept in psychology:

**existential psychology.**

The book said that existential psychology concerns human free will, self-determination, and the search for meaning.

Perhaps that was part of it.

Before that experience, much of the first half of my life had been built upon an invisible assumption:

*What you give the world, the world will eventually give back to you.*

Finish your work.

Teach your students well.

Keep your lesson plans organized.

Do the right things.

And the future will eventually reveal some kind of fairness.

But live that way for long enough, and one night you may wake from sleep and realize that beneath the carefully maintained order of your life lies an enormous emptiness.

The existential anxieties—

death,

loneliness,

the absence of meaning—

are not things most people think about every day.

But when I sat in that apartment in Luoyang, surrounded by English textbooks, teaching materials, and lesson plans, and the entire city became quiet at night,

those questions rose like groundwater.

Perhaps I helped Yin Yuzhen raise that money not because I was noble.

**Perhaps I met her at precisely the moment when I most desperately needed to understand why I was alive.**

Psychologists have studied something called **post-traumatic growth**—the possibility that people who endure extreme adversity may emerge stronger, more accepting, and more deeply connected to meaning.

I began wondering whether the same might be true, in another form, for people who witness suffering and choose to help.

Can compassion itself become a source of growth?

My answer is yes.

There is a memory of the Mu Us Desert inside me.

That woman in the dugout, lifting a withered sapling and planting it in the sand, changed the way I understood humanity.

After you have witnessed someone planting trees in a desert with your own eyes, it becomes difficult to look at the world through the simple binaries of the Cold War.

It becomes difficult to judge distant civilizations from a position of arrogance and superiority.

Once, in class, a student asked me:

> “Mr. S, what was your experience in China like?”

I told them about the five thousand dollars.

And the more than fifty thousand trees.

They listened quietly.

Then one student said:

> “Sir, you made them rich.”

I shook my head.

> **“No. They made me rich.”**

That was not sentimentality.

It was a profound exchange.

I gave her a small amount of money.

She gave me something worth far more than money.

My fear of death became smaller.

My anxiety about the brevity of existence began to seem almost absurd.

Thirty-five years of planting trees.

An entire family anchored in the depths of the sand.

From her perspective, perhaps the meaning of being alive was simply this:

To create something.

To leave something behind.

To make it possible for those who come later to suffer a little less.

Another central idea of existential psychology is that the meaning of life is not necessarily something that exists in advance.

It does not fall from the sky one morning, already complete.

We construct it through our interaction with the world.

We create it through the choices we make.

Through the person we choose to become.

**I chose to help Yin Yuzhen.**

**That choice connected a part of my life to the saplings of the Mu Us Desert.**

---

## VIII

After I returned to Pittsburgh, life went on as usual.

Teaching.

Grading papers.

Taking students to museums.

Traveling with my family during school breaks.

My wife developed Alzheimer's disease.

I slowly learned what it meant to care for another person around the clock.

I could not stay away from home for long.

I often told my students stories about China.

Not the grand narratives of governments.

The stories of ordinary people.

Rural Chinese teachers.

Children growing up in cave dwellings.

And, of course,

Yin Yuzhen.

The first time I told her story to a class of eighth graders, they stared at me with wide, beautiful eyes.

In their imaginative world there seemed to be only football, hamburgers, and Disneyland.

And suddenly, on the other side of the earth, there was a woman not much older than their mothers, carrying saplings heavier than her own body weight back and forth across the dunes.

> “Why?”

they asked.

I told them:

> “Because that is her home.”

They were silent for a while.

Then a girl with a ponytail said:

> “Sir, you were lucky to have seen her.”

Yes.

You were lucky.

Those words exploded inside me like a grenade.

For twenty-five years, I watched that memory slowly sink toward the bottom of my consciousness.

I rarely touched it.

But it never truly disappeared.

It became like an old piece of furniture inside an abandoned house.

You know it is there.

You simply do not want to open the door.

Because the moment you walk inside—

the shame,

the sense that what you gave was nowhere near what she had given,

the crushing feeling of indebtedness—

can overwhelm you.

She lived in that dugout for fourteen years.

I spent most of my life reviewing essays written by American high school students in air-conditioned rooms.

She dug sand with a shovel for thirty-five years.

I spent three months writing letters.

There is nothing that can erase that inequality.

---

## IX

And now I am sitting in my home in Pittsburgh, looking at that endless green forest on my screen.

My eyes are blurred.

More than five thousand trees—

No.

More than fifty thousand.

The saplings from those years have grown into enormous trees.

They have merged into a vast green oasis.

That little tree no thicker than my finger, planted twenty-six years ago, now has roots reaching meters beneath the sand.

Its canopy can shelter an enormous patch of earth.

The forest stretches across land I can no longer measure.

Larger than a Pittsburgh city block.

Larger than an entire school campus.

Larger than anything my later life could have imagined.

Tears run from the corners of my eyes into my mouth.

They taste of salt.

A psychologist once described **vicarious trauma**—the psychological burden that can affect people who help those who have experienced trauma.

Later research also explored **vicarious post-traumatic growth**—the possibility that helping others can bring positive psychological transformation to the helper as well.

Perhaps that explains part of what happened to me.

But I prefer another framework.

At some point in my life,

I became a better man.

A more humble man.

A more reverent man.

A man more willing to listen.

More willing to observe.

I moved from the perspective of a white man who once asked,

*Why don't they simply do something to change their situation?*

to one capable of seeing the small but extraordinary forms of resilience hidden inside apparently hopeless struggles.

The kind of persistence that looks, from a distance, like foolishly trying to move a mountain—

yet contains an astonishing force of will.

Bai Fan once asked me:

> “What is the one thing you would most like to say to Yin Yuzhen?”

I gave him a very simple answer.

> **“I never forgot.”**

It was not a polite response.

It was not an empty promise.

Once, I mentioned Yin Yuzhen to my colleagues.

A young teacher who had just received his PhD asked me whether I had ever wondered where the donation ultimately went.

I froze for several seconds.

*Where did it go?*

It went into that woman's promise.

It went into the monument she had chosen to build with her own hands.

She once told me something.

She said she would use the money to plant many, many trees.

I believed her.

Because I had seen something in her eyes.

It was not gratitude.

It was not a promise.

It was something deeper.

A certainty.

As though she already knew what would happen.

As though she knew that one day she would live to be sixty years old, stand in the middle of a forest stretching like an ocean, and call my name loudly enough for the whole world to hear.

I do not deserve that kind of loyalty.

But I received it.

There is a small fact about tree biology that I find strangely beautiful:

When root systems become sufficiently developed, they can intertwine beneath the surface and share resources such as water.

Above ground, the trees may appear to grow independently.

Below ground, they may already be connected.

Perhaps my relationship with Yin Yuzhen is something like that.

Thousands of miles separate us.

East and West separate us.

Language separates us.

Culture separates us.

Belief separates us.

Yet somewhere deeper,

we are connected by the same roots—

roots grown from hope.

They have always been there.

Silent.

Invisible.

Providing nourishment.

And now, at sixty-nine years old, I think I can finally answer the question.

**S—where did you come from?**

**Where have you been?**

**Why does your heart keep beating?**

**What gives you the right to remain alive?**

I finally know.

**It began in the year I met the Mu Us Desert.**
