# **A Dream in the Garden**

**April 7. Night. Windy.**

Tonight, I heard that tune again.

It was drifting through the radio next door, coming and going, as though it were carried through water.

*“The flowers—so dazzling, purple and crimson—have blossomed everywhere…”*

That was all I heard.

Just that one line.

Yet it stayed in my head the whole night, refusing to leave.

---

## **I**

I dreamed that I was standing on a stage again.

When the lights came on, I saw a sea of dark heads below me. Thousands of faces tilted upward, their eyes shining.

I opened my mouth and began to sing.

The voice flowed from my throat like water, like silk. I was intoxicated by it before anyone else could be.

It was *The Fairy Maiden*, the song about the birds in the trees flying in pairs, the Seventh Fairy descending from heaven.

But as I sang, the audience disappeared.

The chairs were empty, row after row, stretching farther than I could see.

The lights remained, but there was no one left for them to illuminate.

I wanted to stop.

I couldn't.

My mouth kept moving. The voice kept pouring out, as though something beyond me were pushing it into the empty hall.

Then I saw a garden.

Not a garden from the opera, but the back garden of an old mansion—rockeries, pavilions, flowers already past their prime.

I walked along a path paved with small stones. They hurt my feet.

The sky was gray.

The flowers were gray.

Even the reflection in the pond was gray.

But that pavilion—

Why did it look so familiar?

The upturned eaves. The vermilion pillars. The carved flowers along the railing.

Where had I seen it before?

In the dream, I couldn't remember.

After I woke, I did.

**Nanjing. The Gan family's garden.**

I was eighteen that year.

Or nineteen.

I can't remember anymore.

I only remember hearing Kunqu opera there for the first time.

Someone was singing *The Peony Pavilion*. When they reached the line,

*“What a lovely day, what beautiful scenery…”*

I stood beneath the corridor and listened.

My whole body trembled.

It wasn't cold.

It was fear—

fear that something so beautiful existed, and that I might spend my entire life without ever being able to sing it.

Later, I really did learn.

One word at a time.

One phrase at a time.

I picked apart every note until I could put it back together.

My teacher said I had a good voice.

But Kunqu was not simply a matter of having a good voice.

You had to **understand**.

Understand what?

He never told me.

Now I think I know.

You had to understand the two words:

**What a pity.**

Flowers in every color—

and yet, alas, they are given over to broken wells and ruined walls.

Such a beautiful day, such beautiful scenery—

and yet, alas, what does tomorrow bring?

I didn't understand it then.

I only thought it was beautiful.

I only thought it sounded good.

Now I understand.

But what good does understanding do?

---

## **II**

Someone once told me that a human life is made of countless versions of oneself, layered one upon another.

At every crossroads, a different self walks away.

Those selves follow different roads, live different lives, and never meet again.

Only when we are close to the end do we look back and think:

**If only I had…**

What if I had never learned opera?

I was ten that year.

Or eleven.

I don't remember.

The people in my clan did not want girls to perform on stage. They said it was shameful, beneath a decent family.

I insisted anyway.

I sang along the paths between the fields.

I sang on the threshing grounds.

I sang wherever no one was watching.

Later, my teacher took me as a pupil.

He said I had been given a bowl by the patron god of opera.

I didn't understand what he meant by a bowl.

All I knew was that when I stood on stage, my whole body came alive.

Something inside me seemed to overflow.

What if I had listened to them?

What if I had stayed home, married, had children, worked the land—

**would I still have been me?**

That other version of me would probably never have known what a standing ovation felt like.

She would never have known what it meant to be called a great singer.

She would never have stood beneath stage lights while thousands of eyes watched her.

Perhaps she would have lived an ordinary, peaceful life.

She would grow old sitting outside her house in the sun, watching her grandchildren run across the courtyard.

Would she ever dream of singing?

Would she dream of the lights falling onto the stage, of applause rising from the darkness?

No.

She could not even dream of it.

Because she had never known such a world existed.

Thinking about it that way, perhaps I didn't lose so much after all.

---

## **III**

There were other versions of me.

One of them stayed in Nanjing.

She never returned to Anqing.

She never learned Huangmei opera.

She simply stayed with a Kunqu troupe, starting with small roles and slowly working her way upward, phrase by phrase, year by year.

Perhaps she would eventually become a leading actress.

Perhaps she wouldn't.

Perhaps she would spend her whole life as a supporting player, singing *“Morning flights and evening scrolls, clouds and mist above painted terraces…”* from somewhere in the corner while no one listened.

What would that version of me have done in 1968?

She would probably have been struggling too.

Kunqu would have been considered even more “old,” even more “feudal,” even more deserving of destruction.

Perhaps her fate would have been worse than mine.

She would not have had *The Fairy Maiden*, the film that brought Huangmei opera into millions of homes.

She would not have had an audience who knew her name.

She would simply have been another woman who sang old plays.

Perhaps she would have stopped singing altogether.

She might have swept streets.

Made paper boxes.

Done whatever was necessary.

**As long as she stayed alive.**

As long as she stayed alive?

---

## **IV**

And there was another version of me who left the world much earlier.

1946.

Or perhaps 1947.

She died in Anqing.

Or Nanjing.

It doesn't matter.

She died of illness.

Or hunger.

Either would do.

Better still, she died on stage.

She was singing when her breath suddenly failed.

She collapsed with a sleeve still caught in her hand.

At first, the audience thought it was part of the performance.

Then they realized it wasn't.

That would have been beautiful in its own terrible way.

That version of me would have remained forever young.

People would remember her and say,

*What a pity. Such a beautiful voice.*

In the photograph, she would be wearing her costume, her eyes curved into a smile, like the Seventh Fairy who had only just descended from heaven.

She would never know what came afterward.

She wouldn't have to.

Thinking of it that way,

perhaps dying young was a kind of mercy.

---

## **V**

But I did not die.

I lived.

I lived until 1968.

I lived until tonight, until I heard that line leaking through the radio next door:

*“The flowers—so dazzling, purple and crimson—have blossomed everywhere…”*

I suddenly remembered a word I had once heard someone use.

**The kill line.**

A line you cannot see.

You walk and walk, and at some point you cross it.

After that, there is no way back.

You don't know where the line is.

By the time you realize you've crossed it, it is already too late.

When did I cross mine?

Was it the first time I stepped onto a stage?

When I began studying Kunqu?

When I made the film?

When I went to Beijing and performed before important people?

Or—

was it when I returned to Anqing and continued to sing?

Every step carried me forward.

Every step brought me closer to that invisible line.

But I couldn't see it.

All I could see was the audience.

All I could hear was applause.

Someone shouting,

**“Again!”**

I thought the road was wide.

I thought it was bright.

I thought it would go on forever.

No one told me that the road would end.

Just like Du Liniang entering the garden, seeing flowers in every color, and believing that spring would last forever.

She didn't know the flowers would fade.

She didn't know the garden would become desolate.

She didn't know she would die.

Well—

she learned.

She died in the play and came back to life in the play.

That is what happens in an opera.

I am not Du Liniang.

There is no Liu Mengmei to dig up my grave.

There is no resurrection waiting for me.

I am the woman who sings the opera.

I am not the woman inside the opera.

And yet I have spent my whole life performing.

Now, at the end,

I can no longer tell where the play ends and life begins.

---

## **VI**

Dawn is coming.

Outside the window, a bird is calling.

A thin, distant sound, almost like someone practicing their voice far away.

Not Huangmei.

Not Kunqu.

Just a bird.

I suddenly remembered something my teacher once told me.

He said that a performer spends an entire lifetime living for a handful of moments.

The moment the applause begins.

The moment the stage lights fall upon you.

The moment you sing the first line and the entire hall goes silent.

Those moments.

That's enough.

Everything else is endurance.

He was right.

I had those moments.

In Anqing.

In Nanjing.

In Beijing.

Before the camera.

On the stage.

Those moments, layered one upon another, became a lifetime.

And the other moments—

being denounced,

wearing placards,

writing self-criticisms,

hearing people shout *“Down with her!”*—

those were moments too.

But there were too many of them.

So many that they nearly buried everything good.

I try to push the bad ones aside and look only at what remains.

Like opening an old chest.

Lifting one thing after another.

Digging all the way to the bottom.

And finding that old costume.

That old photograph.

That little girl standing beneath the corridor of the Gan family's garden, listening to Kunqu.

She is standing there, trembling.

Not from the cold.

From fear—

fear that something so beautiful might exist, and that she might never be able to sing it.

**She did sing it.**

**That is enough.**

---

## **VII**

Du Liniang meets Liu Mengmei in a dream.

She dies because of love, and is brought back to life because of love.

Her garden is imaginary.

But her longing is real.

My garden was imaginary too.

The pavilions and terraces on stage were only scenery.

But I lived inside them.

When I threw out my long silk sleeves, I felt as if I were truly flying.

When I sang of two lovers becoming birds flying side by side, I felt as if I truly knew what love was.

**Those things were real.**

**No one can take them away from me.**

The radio sounds again.

The same passage.

The same line:

*“The flowers—so dazzling, purple and crimson—have blossomed everywhere…”*

I hum along.

My voice is hoarse.

My breath is weak.

I sing off-key.

But I sing anyway.

**This is my own voice.**

**My last voice.**

Before dawn, I fold my costume and place it beside my pillow.

The gold thread has long since faded.

The silk is worn thin.

Yet I fold it carefully, almost reverently,

as though I were holding a handful of water

and afraid that it might spill.

Outside the window,

the sun of **April 7, 1968**

is about to rise.
