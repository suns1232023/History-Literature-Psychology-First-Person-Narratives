# **The White Dragon Horse’s Post-Pilgrimage Diary**

## **What Became of the Demons Afterward**

**Author: Ao Lie**
**The Eight-Part Heavenly Dragon, Bodhisattva of Vast Strength**

---

## Prologue: Why I Remember Them Beneath Vulture Peak

**Twenty-third day of the twelfth lunar month, Bingwu Year**
**By the Dragon Pool at Spirit Mountain — Snow**

It snowed at Spirit Mountain today.

I coiled myself around the stone pillar beside the Dragon Pool and watched the new arrivals—carp, loaches, little creatures that had spent their whole lives in muddy ponds—thrashing about in the water.

They all came here to be **“upgraded.”**

A little time in the pool, and they would shed their mortal forms, grow scales, sprout claws, and become dragons.

It reminded me of myself.

Five hundred years ago, I was pulled out of Eagle Sorrow Stream.

Back then, I was still known as the **Third Prince of the Western Sea**, the Jade Dragon Prince. I had burned a precious pearl in my father’s palace and nearly lost my head for it. Guanyin Bodhisattva intervened and gave me another fate:

Become a horse.

Carry a monk.

Walk fourteen years.

For fourteen years, I saw more demons than I care to remember.

Some died.

Some survived.

And some survived in ways that were worse than dying.

Today, with nothing better to do, I decided to write down what became of them—the demons who once tried to eat my master, and who later went their separate ways.

I suppose you could call this a psychological study.

Or perhaps it is simply the diary of an old creature who was once a horse, is now a dragon, and has never quite figured out who he is.

---

# Chapter One: Kui Mulang — The Loneliest Star in Heaven

**Third day of the second lunar month, Xinchou Year**
**A quiet room at Spirit Mountain — Overcast**

I ran into Kui Mulang at a meeting in Heaven today.

He stood at the very edge of the Twenty-Eight Mansions, head lowered, speaking to no one.

When the meeting ended, I called out:

“Old Kui. Long time no see.”

He looked up.

For a moment, he did not recognize me.

Fair enough.

I used to be a horse.

Now I am a dragon.

Eventually, he smiled.

“Ao Lie,” he said. “I heard you became a Bodhisattva. Congratulations.”

“And you?”

He was silent for a long time.

“I'm fine,” he finally said. “Back in Heaven. Still serving as a star official. I even helped you people fight the rhinoceros demons last time.”

I did not ask anything more.

But I remember what happened.

Back then, he was the **Yellow-Robed Monster**, ruling the Wave-Moon Cave in Bowl Mountain.

He had taken Princess Baihua as his wife.

They had two children.

He lived there for thirteen years.

Not thirteen years as a demon.

Thirteen years as a **husband and father**.

### A note from a psychological perspective

Kui Mulang's story resembles a classic case of **identity conflict**.

He was originally a celestial deity. He descended into the mortal world because of a forbidden relationship with a celestial maiden. That maiden was later reincarnated as Princess Baihua, without remembering their former life.

Kui Mulang, however, remembered.

He abducted her and made her his wife.

By day, he was a demon king.

By night, he was a husband and father.

Two identities.

Two moral worlds.

One body.

And then the pilgrims arrived.

His family was destroyed.

His children were killed.

His wife was taken away.

And he was sent back to Heaven, where almost no one spoke of what had happened.

I once asked him:

“What happened to Baihua?”

His face changed.

He said nothing.

He simply walked away.

Later, I learned that Baihua had been brought back to Heaven—not as his wife, but as the reincarnation of a celestial maid, assigned to serve under the Queen Mother of the West.

They never saw each other again.

Old Kui, I think the hardest part was not being punished.

It was having once had a home—

and then being expected to behave as though you had never had one.

People call this **traumatic forgetting**.

But forgetting is not always forgetting.

Sometimes it means remembering something so painful that you learn to pretend you do not.

On nights when the moon is full, I have seen him standing alone at the Southern Heavenly Gate, staring down toward the mortal world.

Toward Bowl Mountain.

Toward the place where his home used to be.

# Chapter Two: Red Boy — The Wild Child Who Finally Grew Up

**Seventh day of the seventh lunar month, Renyin Year**
**Mount Putuo, South Sea — Cloudy**

I visited South Sea today with Guanyin Bodhisattva.

In the Purple Bamboo Grove, I ran into **Sudhana**, the Good Wealth Child.

You may remember him by another name.

**Red Boy.**

Five hundred years have passed.

He has changed.

Back then, he was the little demon king of Flaming Cloud Cave—riding on wind and fire, brandishing the Fire-Tip Spear, and breathing the Three-M昧 True Fire.

The first thing he said when he saw my master was:

“So you're Tang Sanzang? Tie him up!”

Now?

He wears a spotless novice's robe.

He stands obediently beside Guanyin.

He carries the Pure Vase.

And he has mastered the professional smile of someone who has spent five hundred years being told to behave.

When he saw me, he bowed.

“Senior Brother, Eight-Part Heavenly Dragon.”

I almost did not recognize him.

Later, while Guanyin was speaking with the other Bodhisattvas, I slipped away and found him sitting alone on a rock overlooking the sea.

“Red Boy.”

He turned.

“Are you used to this life?”

He smiled.

“What else can I do? It's been five hundred years.”

“Do you miss home?”

He was quiet.

“My father was taken away by the Buddha. My mother disappeared into a life of cultivation. Home has been gone for a very long time.”

### A note from a psychological perspective

Red Boy's story resembles a case of **forced socialization**.

He was born fiercely independent, powerful, impulsive, and defiant.

After being subdued by Guanyin, he was given a new identity and a new social role.

He learned obedience.

He learned discipline.

He learned the language of religious service.

But obedience is not necessarily the same thing as identity.

Sometimes what looks like perfect adaptation is simply a highly effective survival strategy.

I asked him:

“I heard your mother eventually attained spiritual realization?”

His eyes brightened.

“Yes. My mother—Princess Iron Fan. She's cultivating now. Sometimes I get to see her.”

“And your father?”

He said nothing.

The silence lasted longer than any answer could have.

Then he suddenly said:

“Sometimes I envy you.”

“Me?”

“You have a horse's body, but a dragon inside. You can be a horse or a dragon. You know what you are.”

I laughed.

“I don't.”

He looked at me.

“That's exactly what I mean.”

Then he stood up, straightened his robe, and returned to the Purple Bamboo Grove.

The question he left behind was the same one I had carried for centuries:

**When someone takes away your old identity and gives you a new one, how much of the original you remains?**

# Chapter Three: Benbo'erba and Babo'erben — Survivors at the Bottom

**Fifteenth day of the ninth lunar month, Guimao Year**
**The ruins of the Kingdom of Sai Tai — Light rain**

I passed through the old Kingdom of Sai Tai today.

Not intentionally.

I simply took a wrong turn while riding the clouds.

I looked down and recognized the Golden Light Temple.

Memories came back immediately.

The sacred relic had been stolen.

The Nine-Headed Insect had fled.

And two very frightened little demons had once served him there.

**Benbo'erba.**

**Babo'erben.**

A catfish spirit and a black-fish demon.

Five hundred years later, they are still alive.

I found them at the foot of the temple tower.

They saw me and immediately fell to their knees.

“Dragon Grandfather!”

“Get up,” I said. “What are you doing here?”

They looked at each other.

“Well…”

Babo'erben scratched his head.

“When the Nine-Headed Insect ran away, we had nowhere to go.”

“So?”

“We came back here.”

“Why?”

“Because we know this place.”

Now they work as temple guards.

They wear patched clothes.

They carry brooms.

And, strangely enough, they look happy.

“What happened to the Nine-Headed Insect?”

“Gone. Never came back.”

“Didn't you think about following another demon king?”

Benbo'erba laughed.

“Dragon Grandfather, creatures like us don't have powerful friends. No family connections. No great abilities. Whoever we followed, we'd just end up as cannon fodder.”

He shrugged.

“At least here we have food.”

I looked at the two of them.

Five hundred years.

No enlightenment.

No immortality.

No divine title.

Just two former demons sweeping the floor of a temple.

And somehow, they were still alive.

### A note from a psychological perspective

These two may represent the **bottom layer of the survival hierarchy**.

The powerful demons were defeated, captured, recruited, or pardoned.

The weak ones were usually killed.

The insignificant ones disappeared without leaving a name.

Survival, therefore, was not necessarily a reward for virtue.

Sometimes it was simply the result of being useful enough, harmless enough, or lucky enough to be overlooked.

Before I left, Benbo'erba suddenly asked:

“Dragon Grandfather… are we still demons?”

I stopped.

“Of course you are.”

“But we don't hurt anyone anymore.”

He looked at his broom.

“We used to be demons. Now we don't do what demons do.”

“So what are we?”

I had no answer.

On my way out, I quietly left them several fruits from Spirit Mountain.

They thanked me so enthusiastically that they followed me for two miles.

Watching them disappear into the distance, I thought:

Perhaps this is the fate of most demons.

Not Buddhahood.

Not immortality.

Not glory.

Just enough food.

A place to sleep.

And another ordinary day.

# Chapter Four: The Scorpion Demon — The Woman Who Once Hurt the Buddha

**Third day of the third lunar month, Jiachen Year**
**Former territory of the Kingdom of Women — Strong wind**

I passed the Kingdom of Women today.

The Pipa Cave still stands.

Grass has grown over the entrance.

I stood there for a long time.

I remembered the woman who once lived inside.

The **Scorpion Demon**.

During our journey through the Kingdom of Women, my master was almost married to the queen.

I could tell he was tempted.

Then the Scorpion Demon appeared.

She abducted him before he could decide anything.

She was beautiful.

Dressed in red.

Her voice soft and playful.

She called him:

“Brother Monk.”

But there was something else about her.

She was the only demon I knew who had once **hurt the Buddha himself**.

### A note from a psychological perspective

Her story is unusual.

She had once listened to Buddhist teachings at Spirit Mountain.

One day, the Buddha pushed her aside.

She reacted instinctively and stung him.

The Buddha felt pain.

He ordered the Vajra guardians to capture her.

From that moment onward, she carried a permanent label:

**Dangerous.**

She fled to Pipa Cave and became a demon.

Her later crime was not eating Tang Sanzang.

She wanted to marry him.

She wanted a home.

She wanted someone who would stay.

But she was already marked.

Once the label **“dangerous demon”** was attached to her, nothing she did afterward could completely erase it.

She had become what everyone expected her to be.

She died terribly.

The Rooster Star God revealed her true form, and Pigsy crushed her beneath his rake.

I do not know whether she regretted that first sting.

But I sometimes wonder whether the entire tragedy began with a single moment:

Someone pushed her.

She pushed back.

And the heavens remembered only the second part.

That is what people mean by **stigma**.

Once the world decides what you are, it becomes very difficult to persuade the world otherwise.

Before leaving, I stood outside the cave and recited a prayer for her.

The wind moved through the grass.

For a moment, I thought I heard someone sigh.

# Chapter Five: Princess Iron Fan — The Best Ending

**Fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month, Yisi Year**
**Flaming Mountain — Clear**

It was the Mid-Autumn Festival.

I found Princess Iron Fan today.

She no longer looks like a demon.

At least, not the kind of demon we used to fear.

She lives in a small village.

She built two simple houses.

She grows corn.

She keeps chickens.

And beside her house lies an old ox.

When she saw me, she stared for a moment.

Then she smiled.

“Little White Dragon? What brings you here?”

“I was passing by.”

She poured me a cup of tea and returned to shelling corn.

The old ox lifted its head, looked at me, and went back to sleep.

I hesitated before asking:

“What about the Bull Demon King?”

“I don't know.”

She said it as calmly as though she were talking about a stranger.

“Red Boy?”

“I heard he's doing well.”

She smiled.

“He sent word recently. Said he is fine in the South Sea and told me not to worry.”

I looked at her.

Her hair had turned white.

Her face was lined with age.

But her eyes were peaceful.

That kind of peace is rare among immortals.

I have seen it more often in ordinary mortals—the ones who have finally stopped fighting the life they were given.

### A note from a psychological perspective

Princess Iron Fan may have experienced one of the healthiest forms of recovery among the demons of our journey.

She lost almost everything.

Her son was taken.

Her husband disappeared.

Her kingdom collapsed.

Her magical power became irrelevant.

And yet she eventually stopped asking:

**“Why did this happen to me?”**

and began asking:

**“What can I do with what remains?”**

This is close to what mortals call **psychological resilience**.

Perhaps even **post-traumatic growth**.

I finally asked her:

“Princess… do you still hate them?”

She stopped shelling the corn.

“Hate whom?”

“Guanyin. The Buddha. The Heavenly Court. Your husband.”

She thought for a while.

“Why would I hate Guanyin for taking my son? That was his destiny.”

“Your husband?”

“He had his own fate.”

“The Buddha?”

She laughed softly.

“He never knew I existed.”

Then she looked at me.

“When I was young, I hated everyone.”

She turned another ear of corn in her hands.

“I carried that fan everywhere. Whoever angered me, I wanted to blow away.”

“But eventually I understood something.”

“Hate doesn't bring anyone back.”

She smiled.

“Red Boy can't come home.”

“The Bull Demon King can't come home.”

“So I planted corn.”

“I raised chickens.”

“I waited for my son to visit when he had time.”

“That is enough.”

Before I left, she handed me several ears of corn.

“Tell Red Boy his mother is fine.”

I promised I would.

As I rose into the clouds, I looked back.

She was sitting in the courtyard.

The old ox lay beside her.

Smoke curled upward from the chimney.

For the first time, I understood:

Perhaps the best ending is not becoming a Buddha.

Not becoming an immortal.

Not defeating anyone.

Perhaps the best ending is simply becoming an ordinary person who can wake up in the morning without hatred.

# Chapter Six: The Nameless Ones

**Thirtieth day of the twelfth lunar month, Bingwu Year**
**Spirit Mountain — Heavy snow**

It is New Year's Eve.

Even Spirit Mountain celebrates.

The Buddha lectures.

The Bodhisattvas chant.

The Vajra guardians patrol.

The celestial maidens dance.

And I sit alone beside the Dragon Pool.

For some reason, I keep thinking about names.

White Bone Demon.

The three demons of the Kingdom of Chechi.

The Nine-Tailed Fox.

The seven Spider Spirits.

The Python Demon.

The Leopard Demon.

The Yellow-Lion Demon.

The three rhinoceros demons.

They are all gone.

Some had names.

Many did not.

They spent centuries cultivating.

They struggled.

They survived.

They became powerful.

And then one day, a golden staff came down.

And that was the end.

People like to say that demons deserve what happens to them.

But I sometimes wonder:

What exactly did they do that was so different from everyone else?

The White Bone Demon wanted to eat humans.

Tigers eat humans too.

The three demons of Chechi helped the king bring rain and, for a time, served the kingdom.

The Spider Spirits lived in a cave and minded their own business until they decided they wanted to eat my master.

The Yellow-Lion Demon was hardly the greatest threat in the world.

And the rhinoceros demons?

Their horns were valuable.

Perhaps that was enough.

### A note from a psychological perspective

Mortals have a strange habit of explaining outcomes after the fact.

The survivor is called worthy.

The dead are called evil.

The powerful are described as legitimate.

The powerless are described as dangerous.

Perhaps this is a form of **attribution bias**.

The powerful are given explanations.

The weak are given labels.

The protected are given second chances.

The unprotected are given graves.

The Great Sage once said:

**“Heaven and earth are inherently incomplete.”**

Maybe he was right.

The world is not fair.

It never promised to be.

Still, when I think about those demons, I wonder what they thought in their final moments.

Did they regret spending centuries cultivating?

Did they curse the immortals who came to kill them?

Did they wonder why some demons were rescued while others were destroyed?

Or perhaps they thought nothing at all.

The staff came down.

And everything became quiet.

The snow is getting heavier.

I am tired.

# Epilogue: What Became of Me

**Second day of the second lunar month, Dingwei Year**
**Dragon Pool — Clear**

Today is the Dragon Raising Day.

My birthday.

I have been at Spirit Mountain for five hundred years.

Most days I coil around this pillar and watch the sun rise and set.

Occasionally a Bodhisattva passes by.

They nod.

“Eight-Part Heavenly Dragon.”

I nod back.

Sometimes I look into the water and ask:

**Who are you?**

Am I Ao Lie?

The Third Prince of the Western Sea?

The Little White Dragon?

The White Dragon Horse?

The Eight-Part Heavenly Dragon?

The Bodhisattva of Vast Strength?

Perhaps all of them.

Perhaps none.

Long ago, I burned a pearl in my father's palace.

My father accused me of rebellion.

The Jade Emperor wanted me dead.

Guanyin saved me.

Then she turned me into a horse.

And I carried a monk for fourteen years.

During those fourteen years, I rarely spoke.

They were disciples.

They were brothers.

They argued.

They laughed.

They fought.

I was the horse.

Once, in the Kingdom of Bao, I transformed back into my dragon-human form and fought to save my master.

I was wounded.

Afterward, no one mentioned it.

As though nothing had happened.

### A final psychological note

Perhaps that is what psychologists would call **role fixation**.

Once a system decides what you are, everything you do is interpreted through that role.

I was the horse.

Therefore, I was useful.

But usefulness is not the same as recognition.

When the pilgrimage ended, the Buddha gave me a magnificent title:

**The Eight-Part Heavenly Dragon, Bodhisattva of Vast Strength.**

It sounds impressive.

In practice, I spend most of my time coiled around a pillar beside a pool.

New fish arrive.

They bathe.

They become dragons.

They fly away.

I remain.

Sometimes I wonder about the demons I wrote about.

Kui Mulang returned to Heaven but never found his home again.

Red Boy became a holy attendant but lost the boy he once was.

Benbo'erba and Babo'erben survived, but remained small demons.

The Scorpion Demon died alone.

Princess Iron Fan survived and found peace.

And me?

I am a dragon.

I am a horse.

I am a Bodhisattva.

And I still don't know who I am.

Perhaps that is the fate of all of us.

There may be no final answer to the question **“What are you?”**

There is only the question:

**“What have you become?”**

I was a prince.

Then I became a horse.

Then I became a Bodhisattva.

Whatever I became, I kept moving.

Perhaps that is enough.

The snow has stopped.

The sun has come out.

The Dragon Pool is glittering.

Several newly transformed dragons are splashing in the water.

They look at me with curiosity.

They are wondering why the old dragon has been coiled around the same pillar for so long.

I ignore them.

I close my eyes.

I let the sun warm my scales.

Perhaps one day they will understand.

A destination is not always something you reach.

Sometimes it is simply the place where you stop asking where you ought to be.

So I will stay here.

Watch the clouds.

Listen to the wind.

Remember a few old friends.

Write down a few stories.

And let the centuries pass.

That is enough.

**— Ao Lie**

**The Eight-Part Heavenly Dragon, Bodhisattva of Vast Strength**

## Final Reflection

Looking back at the pilgrimage, I have begun to notice something strange.

The demons did not all receive the same ending.

Some were rescued.

Some were recruited.

Some were forgiven.

Some were killed.

Some simply disappeared.

And the difference was not always goodness.

Sometimes it was **who they knew**.

Sometimes it was **how powerful they were**.

Sometimes it was whether someone important could say:

> “This one belongs to me.”

Perhaps the journey taught me less about demons than it did about systems.

A system does not treat every individual equally.

It remembers some.

It forgets others.

It gives second chances to some and final judgments to others.

And sometimes the difference between a demon and a celestial being is not what lives inside the body.

It is **who is standing behind you**.

But there is another lesson I have learned after five hundred years:

Survival itself is not the same thing as victory.

Kui Mulang survived, but lost his home.

Red Boy survived, but lost his childhood.

Iron Fan survived, but lost almost everyone she loved.

I survived too.

I became a dragon.

I became a Bodhisattva.

I received a title.

And yet, after all these centuries, I still sometimes look into the water and see the frightened young prince who once burned a pearl and did not understand what his life was about to become.

Perhaps identity is not something we discover.

Perhaps it is something that accumulates.

A prince.

A prisoner.

A horse.

A pilgrim.

A dragon.

A Bodhisattva.

A witness.

A storyteller.

All of them remain.

And perhaps that is why I keep writing.

Because as long as I remember them—

**the dead have not completely disappeared.**

And perhaps, as long as someone remembers who we used to be,

we are never entirely reduced to what the world decided we should become.

**End.**
