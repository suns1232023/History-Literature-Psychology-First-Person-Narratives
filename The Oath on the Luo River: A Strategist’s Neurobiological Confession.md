
# The Oath on the Luo River: A Strategist’s Neurobiological Confession

### — The Game Diary of Sima Yi

**【The Tenth Year of Zhengshi · After the Coup at Gaopingling】**

**The 28th Day of the First Month · Overcast**

The Luo River is still flowing.

I stood on its bank, watching the muddy water move eastward, as though I were watching time itself. Three days ago, I stood here and swore that if Cao Shuang surrendered military power, I would guarantee his wealth and safety.

Now Cao Shuang and his entire clan have become nothing more than piles of earth outside Luoyang.

Everyone says I betrayed the sacred Oath of the Luo River. They say I shattered the sanctity of my own promise.

They are right.

And they are wrong.

They are right in saying that I broke my oath. They are wrong in thinking that I cared.

That physician from the Western Regions once told me that the human brain contains something called the **prefrontal cortex**, which governs rational decision-making. There is also something called the **amygdala**, which governs fear and emotion.

For ordinary people, decisions are made while these two regions struggle against each other.

But people like me are different.

My prefrontal cortex is too thick, too powerful—powerful enough to completely suppress the screaming of the amygdala.

So when I said, *“If I break this oath, may Heaven and Earth destroy me,”* my amygdala did not tremble. My oxytocin system did not release a single signal of trust. My dopamine system produced neither pleasure nor guilt.

I was simply calculating.

Calculating the cost of those words.

Calculating their return.

Calculating how much time, how much power, and how much room for survival those words could buy me.

**A person who does not understand their own value is inviting others to determine their price.**

Cao Shuang was such a person.

He believed that because he belonged to the imperial Cao clan, power was his birthright. But he did not understand that on the chessboard of power, aristocratic lineage is merely starting capital—not a guarantee against death.

When you yourself do not know what you are worth, someone else will decide your price for you.

And they will almost certainly price you downward.

**Outsiders, by default, will try to push your price toward zero.**

When Cao Cao first summoned me, I pretended to be ill and refused to go.

It was not because I did not want office.

It was because I knew that things obtained too easily are rarely valued.

I wanted him to feel that I was not someone who could simply be summoned at will. I wanted him to “discover” my value himself, rather than have me actively “sell” it to him.

Later, when he forcibly summoned me, I went.

But I went as though I had been compelled.

That way, he would feel that he owed me something.

**Many times, the people you think are driven by ideals are also doing it for money.**

Zhuge Liang marched north under the banner of “restoring the Han.”

What a noble ideal.

But think carefully.

If the Han dynasty had truly been restored, what would Zhuge Liang have become?

Prime Minister.

Power broker.

The man who actually governed the state.

Idealism was the outer garment.

Power was the core.

**And the people you think have no shame may actually live more honestly, as anyone who truly understands the world knows.**

I, Sima Yi, am that “shameless” man.

I do not pretend to be a loyal minister.

I do not pretend to be a gentleman.

I do not pretend to be righteous.

If I want power, I compete for it.

If I want to survive, I calculate.

I openly acknowledge my desires and openly use whatever means are necessary.

Because I know that in an age of chaos, those who pretend to be morally superior are often the first to die.

---

**The 29th Day of the First Month · Rain**

Jiang Ji died today.

He was literally angered to death.

Only a month ago, he had stood beside me and sworn by the Luo River, pledging his reputation as a guarantee that I would honor my promise.

Now he lies in his coffin, his eyes still open.

I sent people to offer condolences and brought a great deal of money.

His family refused it.

They said:

*“We dare not stain our father’s good name.”*

A good name?

Hah.

That physician from the Western Regions once told me that the human brain contains something called the **default mode network**, which is involved in social cognition and the evaluation of relationships.

When someone violates deeply held social norms, this network becomes active, producing feelings such as guilt and shame.

Jiang Ji’s default mode network must have been activated to its absolute limit.

That is why he could not survive.

And mine?

Perhaps it had already broken long ago.

Or perhaps it was not broken.

Perhaps I simply chose to switch it off.

In the thirteenth year of Jian'an, Cao Cao forcibly summoned me into his service.

I was still young then. I still believed in the Confucian ideals of loyalty to the ruler and devotion to the state.

I thought I could become a pure and loyal minister, like Xun Yu—serving until the very end.

But then what did I see?

I saw Xun Yu driven to suicide after opposing Cao Cao’s elevation to Duke of Wei.

I saw Cui Yan imprisoned and executed over a casual remark.

I saw Yang Xiu, brilliant as he was, killed under a convenient pretext.

**They were all men of ideals.**

Xun Yu was loyal to the Han.

Cui Yan was uncompromising in his integrity.

Yang Xiu was extraordinarily talented.

And what became of them?

They died.

All of them.

That was when I finally understood:

**Ideals are a luxury. Only those who survive have the right to speak about them.**

My prefrontal cortex began developing rapidly.

It told me:

Shut down your default mode network.

Shut down your emotional system.

Devote every available cognitive resource to calculating the probability of survival.

Let dopamine respond not to moral gratification, but to increases in power.

Let oxytocin serve not genuine trust, but strategic alliances.

I began to learn.

I learned Cao Cao’s ruthlessness.

Liu Bei’s patience.

Sun Quan’s adaptability.

I observed everyone’s weaknesses.

Calculated everyone’s value.

Assessed everyone’s risks.

Cao Pi liked hearing flattering words, so I gave him flattering words.

Cao Rui was suspicious, so I pretended to be ill.

Cao Shuang was arrogant, so I made myself appear weak.

I was not acting.

**I was surviving.**

---

**The First Day of the Second Month · Clear**

Today I finished dealing with Cao Shuang’s remaining supporters.

More than five thousand households.

More than two hundred thousand people.

Someone asked me:

“How can you sleep after killing so many people?”

I sleep perfectly well.

Not only do I sleep—I sleep deeply.

Because I know that if I did not kill them, they would kill me.

If I did not destroy their clans, my clan would be destroyed.

This is not a moral question.

**It is a mathematical question.**

That physician from the Western Regions once told me about an experiment known as the **trust game**.

Two people participate.

A gives money to B.

B may return some of it—or keep it all.

Experiments show that most people choose to trust during the first interaction because the brain’s social systems encourage cooperation and trust.

But if B betrays that trust once, A’s next decision changes.

The prefrontal cortex intervenes.

It calculates risk.

It evaluates potential gain.

And then it decides:

**Do not trust again.**

My confrontation with Cao Shuang was simply a trust game magnified millions of times.

Did he ever trust me?

No.

He marginalized me.

He excluded me.

He wanted me dead.

I acted weak.

I pretended to be ill.

I withdrew.

He thought I was old.

Finished.

No longer a threat.

So he lowered his guard.

And that was why I waited for my opportunity at Gaopingling.

When I launched the coup, what happened inside my brain?

The reward network screamed.

The ventral striatum released dopamine:

**Power. More power.**

The salience network assessed the risks.

The amygdala and anterior insula calculated the probability of a counterattack.

The central executive network made the decisions.

The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex issued its commands:

Control the capital.

Control the Empress Dowager.

Control the narrative.

And my default mode network?

It was silent.

Silent as death.

Because I knew that in this game, emotion was unnecessary.

Morality was a burden.

Trust was fatal.

---

**The Third Day of the Second Month · Overcast**

Today I found an essay I wrote when I was young.

Back then, I still believed in:

*“The gentleman understands righteousness; the petty man understands profit.”*

I believed in keeping one’s word.

I believed in loyalty, filial piety, integrity, and honor.

Reading those words now, I find them almost laughable.

Not because my younger self was naïve.

But because the world itself was hypocritical.

Everyone says I treated my oath as a joke.

But they forget:

In an age of chaos, an oath was always a joke.

Dong Zhuo swore to restore the Han when he deposed the young emperor and installed Emperor Xian.

What happened?

He burned Luoyang.

He moved the capital to Chang'an.

He turned the imperial court into his personal toy.

Lü Bu swore loyalty to Dong Zhuo as his adoptive father after killing Ding Yuan.

What happened?

He killed Dong Zhuo for Diaochan.

Cao Cao swore that he would eventually return imperial authority when he held the emperor and commanded the feudal lords in his name.

What happened?

His son Cao Pi eventually usurped the Han throne.

**What is an oath?**

A tool.

A means.

A bargaining chip.

When you need it, it is priceless.

When you no longer need it, it is worthless.

When I swore beside the Luo River, Cao Shuang believed me.

Why?

Not because my oath was sincere.

But because **his brain wanted to believe it.**

His reward system calculated:

Surrender military power.

Stay alive.

Keep his wealth and status.

The anticipated reward was so attractive that it blinded him to the risks.

His salience network should have warned him:

Sima Yi cannot be trusted.

Sima Yi has a history.

Sima Yi is an old fox.

But his amygdala was overwhelmed by fear.

Fear of death.

Fear of loss.

Fear of the unknown.

His central executive network should have made the rational decision:

Go to Xuchang.

Seize the emperor.

Raise an army.

Resist.

But his prefrontal cortex was hijacked by dopamine.

Dopamine told him:

Surrender is the shortcut.

Surrender is the easy path.

Surrender is the option with the lowest immediate risk.

So he believed me.

And so he died.

**That was not my fault.**

It was the fault of his brain.

---

**The Fifth Day of the Second Month · Windy**

Today my son, Shier, came to me.

“Father,” he asked, “we have killed so many people. How will future generations judge us?”

I looked into his eyes.

They were still too clear.

Still untouched by power.

I said:

“They will condemn us.”

“They will call us traitors, usurpers, oath-breakers, and men without honor.”

He asked:

“Then why do you still do this?”

I smiled.

“Do you know why animals sometimes eat their own young?”

He shook his head.

“Survival.”

“When resources become desperately scarce, when survival itself is threatened, morality, ethics, family bonds—all the things constructed by civilized society—can collapse.”

“What remains is the most primitive instinct of all.”

**Stay alive.**

“Our Sima clan is now facing precisely such a moment.”

“If Cao Shuang lives, we die.”

“If his supporters survive, we perish.”

“This is not a question of choice.”

**It is a question of survival.**

That physician from the Western Regions once told me that when people face extreme survival pressure, the HPA axis becomes activated.

Cortisol surges.

The prefrontal cortex can temporarily lose regulatory control.

More primitive regions—the amygdala, the hypothalamus—begin to dominate decision-making.

At such moments, a human being becomes an animal.

I, Sima Yi, am an animal trying to survive in an age of chaos.

But I am not an ordinary animal.

**I am an animal with a prefrontal cortex.**

I can become an animal while retaining the capacity for rational calculation.

I can tear apart my prey while planning the location of the next hunting ground.

That is my advantage.

That is why I am still alive.

---

**The Eighth Day of the Second Month · Clear**

Today, while handling government affairs, I suddenly remembered what that physician called **“instrumentalization.”**

He said that when a person is treated as a tool for long enough, the brain itself begins to change.

The dopamine system no longer becomes excited by autonomous choice.

It becomes excited by being useful.

The oxytocin system no longer responds to genuine emotional attachment.

It responds to the performance of loyalty.

The amygdala no longer fears genuine threats.

It fears losing one’s value as a tool.

Was I not a tool of Wei?

Cao Cao used me because I was useful.

Cao Pi used me because I was useful.

Cao Rui used me because I was useful.

They never truly regarded me as a human being.

They regarded me as a blade.

A shield.

A chess piece.

So I learned to reverse the relationship.

I treated them as tools too.

Cao Cao was my stepping stone.

Cao Pi was my protective umbrella.

Cao Rui was my stage.

Between tools, there is no affection.

**Only utilization.**

Cao Shuang did not understand this.

He thought he was a member of the imperial clan.

He thought he was born to be the master.

He thought everyone should serve him.

Everyone should be loyal to him.

So he lost.

He lost because he treated himself too much like a human being—and everyone else too much like a tool.

**A person who does not understand their own value is inviting others to determine their price.**

Cao Shuang did not understand his own value.

He believed his value came from his bloodline.

His surname.

His inherited status.

But he forgot that in the marketplace of power, value is determined by supply and demand.

When you need others and others need you, you have value.

When you need others but nobody needs you, your value is zero.

I understood my own value.

I knew I could wage war.

I could govern.

I could balance competing factions.

I knew Wei needed me the way it needed air and water.

So I could negotiate.

I could make myself scarce.

I could wait for the right price.

And that was how I rose from a minor literary official to Grand Tutor, to Grand General, and eventually to the man who effectively controlled the state.

**I was not betraying anyone.**

**I was realizing my own value.**

---

**The Twelfth Day of the Second Month · Overcast**

I dreamed of the Luo River again.

In the dream, I stood beside the river and swore an oath.

But this time, I was not swearing it to Cao Shuang.

I was swearing it to myself.

I said:

“Sima Yi, remember this.

All your oaths are tools.

All your promises are bargaining chips.

All your morality is performance.”

When I woke, I sat on my bed for a long time.

That physician from the Western Regions once told me that the human brain has a **self-concept network**, involving the medial prefrontal cortex.

This network helps construct the answer to one fundamental question:

**Who am I?**

When a person’s behavior repeatedly violates their self-concept, the resulting conflict can produce cognitive dissonance.

But I seem to have no such conflict.

Because my self-concept had already been reconstructed.

I was no longer the Sima Yi who studied the sages and dreamed of becoming a pure and loyal minister.

I was the Sima Yi who fought in the arena of power.

Who walked the line between life and death.

Who tested the boundaries of morality.

My prefrontal cortex told me:

Survival comes first.

Power guarantees survival.

Morality is merely an ornament of power.

So I could swear beside the Luo River and then turn around and break my oath.

I could weep before Cao Shuang and say, *“I will preserve your wealth and honor,”* and then calmly destroy his entire clan.

I could read the *Analects* with one hand while calculating how to kill more people with the other.

This was not fragmentation.

**This was evolution.**

In a world where human beings devoured one another, those who were not divided enough had already been eaten.

---

**The Fifteenth Day of the Second Month · Rain**

Today I received a letter concerning Zhuge Liang.

It was not written by him personally. It was one of the documents that circulated from Shu after his death.

Among them was his famous *Admonition to His Son*:

*“Without tranquility, one cannot clarify one’s aspirations; without detachment, one cannot achieve lasting purpose.”*

I read it.

And laughed.

Detachment?

Tranquility?

He marched north repeatedly, exhausting the strength of Shu Han.

He held enormous power at court and personally handled nearly every affair of state.

He even personally concerned himself with the punishment of soldiers.

And this was called tranquility?

**Many times, the people you think are driven by ideals are also doing it for money.**

Zhuge Liang’s ideal was “restoring the Han.”

But what lay behind that ideal?

Power.

Reputation.

A place in history.

If he had truly been detached from fame and power, he should have lived according to his own words:

*“Preserve one’s life in a chaotic age, without seeking fame among the feudal lords.”*

But he did not.

He chose to leave seclusion.

He chose political struggle.

He chose to devote his entire life to the cause.

Why?

Because he could not tolerate obscurity.

Because he needed a stage.

Because he needed to prove himself.

So did I.

But I am more honest than he was.

I admit that I want power.

I admit that I want survival.

I admit that I want to secure a future for the Sima family.

**And the people you think have no shame may actually live more honestly, as anyone who truly understands the world knows.**

I had no shame.

So I survived.

Zhuge Liang cared about his reputation.

So he worked himself to death.

That is all.

---

**The Twentieth Day of the Second Month · Clear**

Today I spoke at length with Shier and Zhao'er.

I told them:

“Our Sima family now stands at a crossroads.”

“One step forward leads toward supreme power.”

“One step backward leads to total destruction.”

Shier asked:

“Father, must we really go that far?”

I said:

“It is not that we want to go there.”

“It is that circumstances are forcing us there.”

The mandate of Wei is already exhausted.

After Cao Rui’s death, after Cao Shuang seized power, after the coup at Gaopingling, Wei was already dead in all but name.

The child sitting on the throne now is merely a puppet.

The court.

The military.

The government.

Inside and outside.

They are all increasingly controlled by the Sima family.

At such a moment, if I do not move forward, others will push me forward.

If I do not compete for the throne, someone else will.

**A person who does not understand their own value is inviting others to determine their price.**

The value of our Sima family now lies in our ability to replace Wei, end the chaos, and perhaps give the realm peace.

If we ourselves refuse to acknowledge that value, others will acknowledge it for us—and then drive our price downward.

They will reduce us back to what we once were:

Ministers.

Tools.

Chess pieces.

Objects that can be discarded whenever convenient.

I cannot allow that to happen.

So I must move forward.

I must break the Oath of the Luo River.

I must kill Cao Shuang.

I must destroy his clan.

I must eliminate those who oppose us.

This is not betrayal.

**This is evolution.**

From tool to master.

From chess piece to chess player.

From the person whose price is defined by others to the person who defines the price.

---

**The Twenty-Fifth Day of the Second Month · Overcast**

Today I went to the Luo River again.

The river was the same.

Muddy.

Turbulent.

Flowing eastward.

Never looking back.

I stood on the bank and remembered the oath I had sworn there three days earlier.

I remembered the way Cao Shuang looked when he believed me.

I remembered Jiang Ji staking his reputation on my word.

I remembered the eyes of those I had killed in the final moments of their lives.

I felt no guilt.

Because I knew that if the positions had been reversed, they would have done the same.

If Cao Shuang had the opportunity, he would have killed me.

If Jiang Ji had the opportunity, he would have stood with the winner.

If those I killed had the opportunity, they would have killed me instead.

This is the game of power.

There is no right or wrong.

Only victory and defeat.

No morality.

Only survival.

When the physician from the Western Regions finally left, he said to me:

“Lord Sima, your brain is the most unusual I have ever encountered.”

“Your prefrontal cortex is so highly developed that it can almost completely suppress your emotional systems.”

“Your reward network responds primarily to increases in power.”

“Your default mode network is barely activated.”

He said:

“Medically speaking, this may be a disorder.”

I asked him:

“How long will I live?”

He answered:

“For a very long time.”

“Longer than almost any normal person.”

I laughed.

A disorder?

No.

**This is not a disorder.**

This is evolution.

The ultimate form produced by the need to survive in an age of chaos.

My brain tells me:

An oath is a tool.

Morality is decoration.

Trust is a trap.

My brain tells me:

Survive.

Gain power.

Pass it on.

My brain tells me:

Sima Yi, you are who you are.

No explanation is necessary.

No defense is necessary.

No guilt is necessary.

Because history is written by the victors.

**And I will be the victor.**

---

**The Twenty-Eighth Day of the Second Month · Clear**

This is where I will end this diary.

I did not write it for the people of my own time.

I wrote it for those who will come a thousand years after me.

For those who struggle in the arena of power.

For those who walk the boundary of morality.

For those who fight to survive on the edge of existence.

I want to tell them:

**A person who does not understand their own value is inviting others to determine their price. And outsiders will, by default, try to push that price toward zero.**

So define your own value.

Know what you want.

Know what you are willing to give.

Know what you are capable of enduring.

Before others put a price on you, **put a price on yourself.**

**Many times, the people you think are driven by ideals are also doing it for money. And the people you think have no shame may actually live more honestly, as anyone who truly understands the world knows.**

So do not be deceived by appearances.

Do not allow morality to be used as a chain.

Do not let emotion make your decisions.

See through to the underlying structure.

Calculate the costs and benefits.

Then choose.

I broke the Oath of the Luo River.

But in return, I secured the future of the Sima family.

I laid the foundation for the Jin dynasty.

I created the possibility of ending the age of chaos.

Was the trade worth it?

I believe it was.

Because in this world, some things are more important than an oath.

Such as survival.

Such as power.

Such as ensuring that my descendants will never again have to become someone else’s tools.

**Sima Yi, at the Grand Tutor’s Residence in Luoyang**

**The Tenth Year of Zhengshi · Second Month**
