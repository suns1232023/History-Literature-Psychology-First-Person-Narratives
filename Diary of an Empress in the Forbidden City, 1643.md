# Diary of an Empress in the Forbidden City, 1643

## On the Melancholy Mindset in Tang Xianzu's *The Peony Pavilion*

**Keywords:** `#CultureAndMedia`

**Diarist:** Empress Zhou, wife of Emperor Chongzhen of Ming. Aged 32, mistress of the Palace of Earthly Tranquility. Originally from Suzhou, born to a poor family; later became Princess of Xin, and was created Empress after Chongzhen ascended the throne.

At this time, the Great Ming is beset by internal turmoil and external threats, and the national treasury is empty. The Emperor and Empress maintain their dignity through frugality, with the Empress often mending the Emperor's clothes.

---

## March 20 — Night, Light Rain

The rain in the palace falls on the glazed tiles with a solid sound — unlike in my childhood in Suzhou, where rain striking banana leaves sounded hollow and fragile, easily broken.

Tonight, the Emperor summoned cabinet ministers for an audience in the Hall of Heavenly Purity. I heard they talked until midnight again. Sitting under the lamp in the Palace of Earthly Tranquility, I work on my needlework — an undershirt of the Emperor's whose cuffs have worn through again. Over all these years, I can no longer count how many garments I have mended for him.

Mending clothes is quite laughable when you think about it. The Emperor is the ruler of a nation, the lord of millions of subjects, yet apart from his dragon robe worn for imperial audiences, all his other clothes are made of coarse cloth, patched by my hands whenever they tear.

It is not that we lack the Imperial Weaving and Dyeing Bureau or the Directorate of Palace Apparel, but the Emperor says those places spend too much, and we must save wherever possible. His dragon robe has been worn so long that its color has faded, yet he refuses to replace it.

I remember one year when the Ministry of Rites submitted a memorial requesting the Emperor to replace his ceremonial robes; he endorsed it with just four characters:

> **"For now, keep it simple."**

Sometimes I think there are probably no poorer emperor and empress in this world than us.

Yet I do not feel it is bitter. I grew up in poverty and managed household chores from a young age, so I am used to doing everything myself. When I married Youjian, he was still the Prince of Xin, living in the Prince's residence outside the palace.

Though life was frugal, we lived in complete harmony as husband and wife. Back then, he would read while I did needlework beside him. Occasionally, we would look up, meet each other's eyes, and smile — without a word spoken, our hearts felt warm.

Now that we are Emperor and Empress, life has become even more exhausting.

Not physically exhausted, but weary in spirit.

Every day, the Emperor reviews memorials deep into the night, his brows perpetually furrowed. Military dispatches from the front arrive one after another — if not a lost territory here, then a defeat there.

The national treasury is empty, military pay cannot be issued, and the soldiers fight on empty stomachs. Common people are displaced and homeless; everywhere there is famine, plague, and rebellion.

The Emperor wants to accomplish so much, yet there is so little he can do.

He often stands alone under the veranda of the Hall of Heavenly Purity, staring at the southern sky for nearly an hour at a time, without saying a word.

Standing behind him, watching his gaunt silhouette, his slightly stooped shoulders, and his temples already silvered with grey hair — he is only thirty-two years old, the same age as me, yet he already resembles an old man.

I do not know how to help him.

All I can do is manage the inner court well, avoiding adding to his troubles or making him worry over small matters. I reduced inner court expenditures, refrained from requesting honors and rewards for my family, and strictly followed ceremonial rules for holiday gifts to noblewomen without extravagance.

The palace servants say I am too frugal, but what else could I do?

The silver saved can be sent to the front as pay for the soldiers.

Even if one more soldier gets a full meal, perhaps one more city can be held.

I understand all these reasons.

I understand everything the Emperor does.

Yet sometimes, in the quiet of the night, sitting alone here in the Palace of Earthly Tranquility facing this lamp, my heart suddenly feels empty, as if something has been drawn out of me.

I cannot describe what it is.

Is it the plum blossoms in my old home of Suzhou?

Is it the alley where I lived as a child?

Is it the handkerchief I secretly embroidered in my maiden room before marriage?

It is all of them, yet none of them.

It is a feeling — an unspoken feeling of being trapped by something.

Just like the flowers outside the window.

In March in the Forbidden City, the flowers in the Imperial Garden should be in bloom. But I haven't gone to see them.

Not because I don't want to, but because I dare not.

I fear that if I go and see those flowers blooming so splendidly, I won't be able to stop myself from thinking:

> **Whom do they bloom for?**

No one looks at them.

Just like the flowers I embroider — stitched onto the Emperor's clothes, onto my nightwear, embroidered for so many years, yet no one takes a close look.

They bloom on fabric, in the stitches, in corners no one notices.

This afternoon, I chanced upon eunuchs speaking softly under the veranda. They mentioned a play being performed outside the palace recently called *The Peony Pavilion*, said to be written by someone named Tang Xianzu during the Ming Dynasty.

One eunuch said there was a woman in the play who strolled in a garden, had a dream, met a scholar in her dream, fell sick with lovesickness, and ultimately died — only to come back to life.

Hearing this, I found it strange:

> **How could a woman die just because of a dream?**

Yet somehow, these words kept spinning in my heart.

Even as I lay in bed, I was still thinking:

What did that woman see in her dream?

What did the scholar in her dream look like?

Why would she die for the sake of a dream?

I tossed and turned, unable to sleep.

Perhaps it was because what she saw in her dream was better than reality.

Perhaps it was because after waking up, she realized reality held nothing, and so she no longer wished to live.

I do not know.

But I know that feeling of **"waking up only to find nothing there."**

Every day when I wake up, looking at the ceiling of the Palace of Earthly Tranquility, at those painted dragon and phoenix patterns, listening to the night watchman's drums outside, I sometimes fall into a trance:

> Where am I?
> Who am I?

Then I remember.

I am the Empress.

I am the Empress of the Great Ming Dynasty.

> **I cannot dream.**

## March 21 — Overcast

Today the weather was gloomy, as if it wanted to rain but never did.

The Emperor came over for lunch, looking very pale and upset. I asked him what was wrong; he said Yang Sichang's memorial had arrived, reporting another defeat at the front.

I said:

> "Your Majesty, please eat first, and we can talk afterward."

He nodded, picked up his bowl, took a few bites, and set it down again.

> "I have no appetite," he said.

I did not urge him further. I knew urging would do no good.

After he left, I asked the maid to bring the half-mended garment and continued my needlework.

Working with needle and thread actually brings quiet to my mind.

Every stitch is orderly, free from surprises — unlike this world, where everything goes wrong.

As I worked, I suddenly recalled that woman named Du Liniang.

I did not know her story, but I remembered her name.

**Du Liniang. Du Liniang.**

The three characters felt soft when recited, like a petal falling onto water, gently swirling around.

She was a lady from a noble official family, living in deep seclusion, never setting foot outside the inner gates.

Only at age sixteen did she walk into her family's backyard garden for the first time.

What did she see in the garden?

She saw flowers.

She saw bright reds and purple blooms in full display, the morning flight of clouds and evening folds of rain, the misty waves and painted boats.

Looking at those flowers, she suddenly realized that though they bloomed so beautifully, no one came to see them; they simply bloomed and withered away, just like her.

> **Just like her.**

My hands trembled as I wrote this line on paper.

How do I know she was like her?

I have never read *The Peony Pavilion*, nor has anyone told me this story.

I was merely — merely guessing.

I just felt that standing in the garden, looking at those flowers, that woman must have felt deeply saddened.

The more exuberantly the flowers bloomed, the colder and lonelier she felt.

The brighter the flowers, the grayer her own days seemed.

She lacked neither food nor clothing, nor maidservants to attend her.

Yet she lacked one thing.

She could not say what it was, but she knew she lacked it.

> **Just like me.**

I have everything.

I am Empress, managing the six palaces, and I have three sons.

Yet sometimes, standing by the window of the Palace of Earthly Tranquility and staring at the sky outside, I feel the world is empty.

Those halls, those palace walls, those glazed tiles, those white marble balustrades surround me layer upon layer, trapping me in the very center.

I am one of the most noble women in this world, yet I am also one of the least free.

Du Liniang at least had a garden.

I do not even have a garden.

There are a few trees in the backyard of the Palace of Earthly Tranquility, but I have looked at them for over a decade and know their shapes even with my eyes closed.

It has been a long time since I walked beyond these palace walls.

When was the last time I left the palace?

Was it accompanying the Emperor to pay respects at the imperial tombs, or which year was it to visit the Imperial Examination Hall?

I can no longer remember.

Du Liniang said:

> **"Without entering the garden, how could one know the brilliance of spring?"**

I do not even have the chance to "not enter."

I live inside the "garden," yet I never dare to truly step into it.

Because if I step in and see the beauty of spring, I will know how much I have missed.

---

## March 22 — Rain

Rain fell heavily today, puddling in the courtyard of the Palace of Earthly Tranquility and reflecting the gray sky like a dirty mirror.

I folded the mended garment neatly and had a maid take it to the Hall of Heavenly Purity.

When she returned, she brought a book, saying the Emperor had sent it to pass my time.

Opening it, I saw it was *The Peony Pavilion: Returns of the Soul*.

I do not know where the Emperor got this book. Perhaps Eastern Depot agents confiscated it outside the palace, or perhaps a minister presented it.

Regardless, holding it in my hands, my heart suddenly skipped a beat.

After lunch, the maidservants withdrew, and I sat alone in the warm chamber, reading the book against the sound of rain outside the window.

I first read the Preface, written by Tang Xianzu.

The very first sentence stunned me:

> **"Love is bound by no origin, yet it runs infinitely deep. The living may die for it, and the dead may return to life. If the living cannot die for it, or the dead cannot return to life, it is not the utmost of love."**

**Love is bound by no origin, yet it runs infinitely deep.**

I recited these characters over and over again.

I do not know what was on Tang Xianzu's mind when he wrote them.

He was a man, a scholar, an official who had spent half his life tumbling through officialdom.

How could he write such words?

How could he understand — that kind of indescribable, mysterious emotion, unknown when it began, unknown why it ran so deep?

I think he must have been someone with wounds in his heart as well.

Later I turned to the scene *An Interrupted Dream* and came across these lines:

> **"So it is that bright reds and purple blooms open everywhere, only to be abandoned to ruined walls and crumbling wells. Fine hours and beautiful scenes — what can be done against Heaven? In whose courtyard lies joy and delight?"**

Reading these few lines, I suddenly wept.

It was not quiet tears, but the kind where — your nose stings and tears stream out all at once.

I covered my mouth with a handkerchief, not daring to make a sound for fear of alarming the maids outside.

Yet the tears would not stop, falling drop by drop onto the book, soaking the page.

> **Fine hours and beautiful scenes — what can be done against Heaven? In whose courtyard lies joy and delight?**

In this lifetime, have I ever had fine hours or beautiful scenes?

Have I ever had joy and delight?

When Youjian and I married, he wore ceremonial red robes, held my hand, and bowed to Heaven and Earth, and to our elders.

Back then, I thought that would be my whole life.

I thought we would go on like that forever — he reading in the study, I doing needlework beside him, occasionally looking up to share a silent smile.

But later, he became the Emperor.

Once he became Emperor, he was no longer my Youjian alone.

He was the Emperor of Great Ming, the Emperor of all people.

His time was not mine, his thoughts were not mine, his joys and sorrows were not mine.

Every evening he came to the Palace of Earthly Tranquility, sat down, drank a bowl of tea, spoke a few words, and then either went to review memorials or fell asleep in the warm chamber.

I was beside him, yet I felt so far from him.

Du Liniang found her person in a dream.

She loved deeply and passionately in that dream, loving to the point of being willing to die for it.

She at least had a dream.

What about me?

I do not even dare to dream.

Lying in bed, I dare not close my eyes.

I fear that if I close them, I will dream.

I fear the person in my dream will not be Youjian.

I fear the person in my dream will be myself — that sixteen-year-old girl running and laughing in the alleys of her Suzhou hometown.

I fear that upon waking, I will find I can never go back.

Du Liniang said:

> **"When flowers and grass are left to one's love, and life and death follow one's wish, then even sweet sorrow brings no grievance."**

When flowers and grass are left to one's love, and life and death follow one's wish.

Yet in this world, how many people can be left to love as they wish?

How many can have life and death follow their desire?

When Tang Xianzu wrote this line, he must have been smiling.

Not a happy smile, but a bitter one.

He knew no such things existed in this world.

He knew being "left to one's love" and "following one's wish" were merely a dream.

He wrote down this dream, let Du Liniang speak it for him, and then sat inside Yuming Hall, staring at the misty rain outside the window, letting out a long, heavy sigh.

I closed the book, wiped my tears, picked up the unfinished garment, and continued my needlework.

The needle pierced through the cloth —

> **stitch by stitch by stitch.**

I think when Tang Xianzu wrote *The Peony Pavilion*, he was also sewing like this, stitch by stitch.

He sewed his own innermost thoughts into Du Liniang's body.

Through her mouth, he spoke the words he never dared to say in his lifetime.

Through her dream, he dreamed the dream he never dared to dream in his life.

He was a man; he could not say "flowers and grass are left to one's love."

How could a scholar, an official, a forty-nine-year-old man say such things?

Yet he wrote it.

He let Du Liniang say it.

He surrendered the softest, most vulnerable, most hidden part of his heart to brush and ink, to paper, and to whoever might read these words four hundred years later.

> **I am that person.**

I am the person who, four hundred years later, reads the words he wrote and cannot help weeping uncontrollably.

Tang Xianzu, do you know?

Four hundred years later, an empress in the Palace of Earthly Tranquility in the Forbidden City reads your *Peony Pavilion*, her face drenched in tears.

She does not weep for Du Liniang.

She weeps for you.

She weeps for herself.

She weeps for everyone in this world who has dreams but dares not dream them, who has love but nowhere to bestow it.

## March 23 — Overcast Turning to Partially Cloudy

The Emperor came again today.

He looked exhausted, with dark circles under his eyes, his beard unshaven for days.

After sitting down, he noticed *The Peony Pavilion* on the table, picked it up, flipped through it, and asked:

> "Have you read it?"

I said:

> "I have."

"How was it?"

I thought for a moment and said:

> "Very good. So good that it makes one sad."

The Emperor said nothing, set the book down, picked up his teacup, and took a sip.

After a moment, he said:

> "I know a bit about this Tang Xianzu. He served as magistrate of Suichang, then resigned and returned home. He wrote plays well, but it is of no use. In times like these, writing plays cannot save the country."

I said:

> "Your Majesty, plays cannot save the country, but they can save people. They can save those whose hearts are bitter."

The Emperor looked at me for a long time.

> "Is your heart bitter too?" he asked.

I did not answer.

I simply picked up the needlework and continued patching the hole on his sleeve that was about to wear through.

> **Stitch by stitch by stitch.**

The Emperor asked no more.

After sitting for a while, he stood up and left.

At the doorway, he suddenly turned back and said:

> "Tonight I will come to the Palace of Earthly Tranquility for dinner."

I nodded.

I knew that was all he could give me.

---

## March 24 — Sunny

Sunlight finally broke through today, so I had the maids bring out the bedding to air.

The sunlight shone into the courtyard of the Palace of Earthly Tranquility, warm and comforting, yet that warmth could not reach my heart.

I flipped through *The Peony Pavilion* once more.

This time, I paid special attention to the line following Tang Xianzu's Preface.

He wrote:

> **"Affairs of the human world cannot be exhausted within the human world."**

I pondered this sentence for a long time.

It means that the affairs of human life cannot be fully told or explained within human life itself.

Some grievances, some bitterness, some unspoken burdens cannot be shared with anyone while living.

If you speak them, no one understands.

If you bottle them up inside, they rot, fester, and consume you whole.

So Tang Xianzu wrote them into plays.

He wrote a dream featuring a garden, a peony pavilion, a peony balustrade, and a scholar approaching with a snapped willow branch.

Those were things he could not obtain in reality.

He committed them to words, allowing them to live within a play, to exist forever and never fade.

> **"Affairs of the human world cannot be exhausted within the human world."**

Meaning that what cannot be finished in life should be left to live on in plays.

What cannot be obtained in life should be fulfilled in dreams.

I copied this sentence onto a sheet of paper and tucked it under my needlework basket.

One day, perhaps someone will see it.

Perhaps not.

But that does not matter.

What matters is that this sentence is true.

I know it is true, and Tang Xianzu knew it too.

We are all people living in the "human world," yet within our hearts are things that the "human world" cannot hold.

That is why we need plays, why we need dreams, why we need *The Peony Pavilion*.

---

## March 25 — Windy

The wind blew all night, and when I woke up this morning, the courtyard was strewn with fallen petals.

Looking at those petals, I suddenly recalled Du Liniang's words:

> **"Ah, this plum tree is so endearing. Should I, Du Liniang, die, to be buried here would be my good fortune."**

A sixteen-year-old girl, possessing no remaining attachment to her life.

She harbored no hatred toward anyone, no resentment toward her parents or the societal rules that locked her in deep seclusion.

She was simply tired.

She felt that if she could die beneath that plum tree and sleep peacefully, it would be the greatest blessing.

I think when Tang Xianzu wrote this scene, he must have been thinking in his heart:

> If one day I die, could I also find a place to lie quietly? Could I stop worrying about court struggles, stop hearing insults, and stop agonizing over things I cannot change?

He was writing about Du Liniang's death, but expressing his own exhaustion.

I am very tired today as well.

Not physically tired, but the kind of weariness where one feels weighed down, unable to catch a breath.

The Emperor spent another night reviewing memorials, and during the morning audience today, I heard he lost his temper terribly, berating all the grand secretaries.

I know he was not truly scolding them — he was cursing this world, cursing this thoroughly decayed Ming Empire.

Yet after his shouting, the sky remained the same sky, the affairs remained the same affairs, and nothing changed.

When Tang Xianzu was forty-nine, he resigned and returned to his hometown in Linchuan.

He was tired too.

He had voiced criticism — against court governance, corrupt officials, and incompetent ministers who held positions without doing their duties.

Yet after voicing it all, he realized he could change nothing.

So he returned to Yuming Hall, closed his doors, and began writing plays.

He poured all his grievances, anger, and unwillingness into *The Peony Pavilion*.

He let Du Liniang die for him, live for him, and love for him.

He let Du Liniang live out the life he could never live.

I think this is the sorrow of a scholar — and also his greatness.

---

## March 26 — Overcast

Today, I finally learned the full story of Tang Xianzu's life.

It was brought by Wang Chengen, sent at the Emperor's behest — a slim volume detailing Tang Xianzu's biography.

I sat under the lamp, turning the pages one by one.

Tang Xianzu was a native of Linchuan, Jiangxi.

He passed the provincial examination at twenty-one, achieving early fame and showcasing extraordinary talent.

However, he refused to curry favor with the powerful Grand Secretary Zhang Juzheng and rejected his overtures.

Consequently, he failed the imperial examination repeatedly, only attaining his jinshi degree at thirty-four.

Thirty-four years old.

A man's prime years, squandered just like that.

Later, while serving as an official in Nanjing, he submitted a memorial impeaching the court administration and criticizing Grand Secretary Shen Shihang.

Enraged, the Emperor demoted him to Xuwen in Guangdong to serve as a minor registrar (*dianshi*).

Xuwen, I have heard, lies at the southernmost tip of the Leizhou Peninsula — a desolate place ridden with miasma.

To banish a scholar to such a location — the bitterness in his heart can well be imagined.

Three years later, he was reassigned as the magistrate of Suichang in Zhejiang, where he served for five years with notable achievements.

Yet, disillusioned by the corruption of officialdom, he resigned at forty-nine and returned to his hometown, never to hold office again.

Returning to Linchuan, he built the Yuming Hall and began writing plays.

It was inside the Yuming Hall that he composed *The Peony Pavilion*.

As I finished reading his biography, my heart felt weighed down, as if blocked by something heavy.

Tang Xianzu spent his entire life contending with reality.

He wanted to be an effective official, to perform practical deeds, and to serve the court.

But reality denied him the chance.

He was sidelined, suppressed, and cast away to the most remote lands.

He struggled for half a lifetime, only to return home in defeat.

He was not a failure of a man.

> **He was a man defeated by reality.**

There is a kind of suffering in this world that stems neither from poverty nor illness, nor from lack of food or clothing.

That suffering is this:

> **You yearn to accomplish something, but you cannot.**

You give it your all, risking your very life, yet you cannot succeed.

You watch helplessly as the things you care about break apart piece by piece; you reach out to grab them, but your hands hold nothing.

This suffering — the Emperor understands it, I understand it, and Tang Xianzu understood it too.

He sewed his own suffering into the body of Du Liniang.

Du Liniang was trapped in her maiden chambers; Tang Xianzu was trapped in officialdom.

Du Liniang longed to love someone but could not; Tang Xianzu wished to achieve great deeds but could not.

Du Liniang found freedom in a dream; Tang Xianzu found solace in his scripts.

He was not writing Du Liniang's dream.

> **He was writing his own fate.**

---

## March 27 — Rain

It rained again today.

Rain pattered against the tiles of the Palace of Earthly Tranquility, dense and steady, sounding like someone weeping on the roof.

Sitting under the lamp, I turned to the last page of *The Peony Pavilion*.

Then I turned back to the first page.

> **"Love is bound by no origin, yet it runs infinitely deep."**

I spent the entire day pondering these eight characters.

I wondered:

When Tang Xianzu wrote these eight characters, was it raining outside his window too?

Was he also sitting alone under the lamp, holding a brush suspended over paper, unable to bring it down for a long time?

His heart must have been full of words he wished to speak.

But to whom could he speak them?

Who would listen?

He had once submitted a memorial to the Emperor, laying out grand principles, only to anger the sovereign and be banished to Xuwen.

He had spoken with colleagues, but some feared being implicated, some found him troublesome, and others wished only for his swift departure.

He had spoken with his family, but they did not understand him — they cared only about when he would become a high official and bring prosperity to the household.

He had no one to talk to.

So he could only speak to paper, to his brush, to his plays.

He poured all his thoughts into *The Peony Pavilion*, letting Du Liniang speak on his behalf, letting Liu Mengmei act on his behalf, and letting that dream live for him once.

When he finished writing, he laid down his brush, looked at the sky outside the window, and perhaps sighed, or perhaps did not.

Yet his heart must have been left empty.

He had poured out every inner thought, leaving himself completely drained.

Then he waited.

He waited for someone who would understand him — someone four hundred years later who would open his book and weep for him.

> **I have wept, Tang Xianzu.**

I do not know if this counts as understanding you.

But every single word of yours feels carved into my heart.

The "blooming colors" you wrote of, the "ruined walls," the "fine hour and beautiful scene" — every line reflects my own destiny.

You were a man, yet you understood women better than many women do.

You were not a woman, yet you portrayed a woman's heart more clearly than a woman could herself.

Because you were not merely writing about a woman.

> **You were writing about yourself — about everyone trapped by reality, longing to fly, yet unable to take wing.**

---

## March 28 — Clear

Today was my birthday.

Following tradition, a modest family banquet was held in the palace.

Crown Prince Cilang, Prince Ding Cijiong, and Prince Yong Cizhao all attended.

Cilang is twelve now, growing more like his father every day, with a spirited resolve between his brows.

Cijiong and Cizhao are still small, running playfully around the table.

Watching them, a surge of sorrow suddenly welled up inside me.

How much longer can this Great Ming Empire endure?

The Emperor exhausts himself every day, yet defeat after defeat continues to arrive from the front lines.

I do not know what these children will have to face in the future.

I dare not think about it.

After the banquet dispersed, I returned to the Palace of Earthly Tranquility and brought out *The Peony Pavilion* once again.

I turned to the scene *In Pursuit of the Dream*.

Du Liniang returned to the garden alone, seeking to recapture that dream.

She searched round and round, but could find nothing left.

Finally, standing beneath the plum tree, she said:

> **"Ah, this plum tree is so endearing. Should I, Du Liniang, die, to be buried here would be my good fortune."**

I read this line three times.

I thought to myself:

> **If one day the sky of Great Ming falls, what will I do?**

Perhaps I, too, will find a place to lie down quietly.

Not for the sake of dying, but to never wake up again.

To never wake up and face a world devoid of dreams.

---

## April 1 — Overcast

A new month has begun.

I do not know how many months Heaven has left for the Ming Dynasty.

The Emperor did not come to the Palace of Earthly Tranquility today.

I heard he had a fierce argument with several ministers in the Hall of Heavenly Purity and smashed a cup.

Wang Chengen quietly told me that the Emperor is reducing palace expenses once more, cutting even the Imperial Kitchen's budget by half.

I nodded and said nothing.

At night, I folded the mended garment neatly and placed it by my pillow, to be sent to the Hall of Heavenly Purity tomorrow.

I can no longer count how many times I have mended this garment.

The cuffs, the collar, the underarms — stitches are everywhere.

Dense and tightly packed, those stitches are like my innermost thoughts, layered upon one another, too numerous to count.

Tang Xianzu's writing of *The Peony Pavilion* was also like needlework.

He stitched a story, unraveled it, and stitched it again, until in the end, even he could not distinguish what was real from what was a dream.

His *Four Dreams of Linchuan* — four dreams that encompassed an entire lifetime.

* *The Dream of the Southern Bough*
* *The Dream of Handan*
* *The Dream of the Purple Hairpin*
* *The Dream of the Peony*

Four dreams.

Four regrets.

Four dreams left unfinished.

He had wanted to achieve great things in officialdom, but reality disheartened him.

He expressed all his reflections on life and his unfulfilled aspirations through his plays.

His dreams were the things he could never obtain in reality.

He wrote them down so they might live forever within the theater.

I am dreaming too.

My dream is for peace across the land, for the Emperor to be less exhausted, for my children to grow up safely, and for a day when I might walk out of these palace walls alongside the Emperor to see the spring outside.

Yet I know this dream will likely never come true.

Reality is too bitter.

Tang Xianzu knew it, and I know it too.

So he wrote *The Peony Pavilion*.

And I can only sit here, mending the Emperor's garment stitch by stitch — a garment that can never truly be finished.

> **Stitch by stitch by stitch.**

Every stitch feels like a character he wrote in *The Peony Pavilion*.

Every character feels like a tear he shed in the Yuming Hall.

The night grows late.

I close this diary and place it under my pillow.

Tomorrow the sun will rise again.

The Emperor will attend court, read memorials, and fret over problems that can never be resolved.

I will continue my needlework, manage the inner court, and wait here in the Forbidden City, day after day.

Waiting for what?

Even I do not know.

Yet I know Tang Xianzu is waiting for me.

Waiting for me, four hundred years later, to open his *Peony Pavilion* and understand his heart.

I have understood it.

That line you wrote —

> **"Love is bound by no origin, yet it runs infinitely deep"**

— is the portrait of my entire life.

My feelings for the Emperor, for this palace, and for this crumbling Great Ming Empire are precisely like that.

**Unbound by origin, running infinitely deep.**

So deep that I myself cannot see the bottom.

So deep that I am no longer afraid even of death.
