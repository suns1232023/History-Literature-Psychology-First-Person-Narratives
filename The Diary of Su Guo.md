# The Diary of Su Guo

### Bianjing · The Twelfth Month of Xuanhe 3 (1121)

---

## Eighth Day of the Twelfth Month · Heavy Snow

The snow in Bianjing falls differently this year.

It does not drift.

It drops.

One flake after another, each carrying its own small weight, striking the paper window like someone tapping on the door with their knuckles.

The old locust tree in the courtyard was planted by Father. We moved it here in the second year after returning to Bianjing. It survived, technically, but it has always looked dispirited.

Today the snow gathered heavily on its branches.

One of them snapped with a soft *crack*.

The sound was faint, but I could hear it from the study.

If Mother were still alive, she would have said:

> “Even a tree cannot always survive the winter.”

Since she died, no one says things like that anymore.

I do not say them either.

Not saying something does not mean I do not think it.

---

I have been going through Father's manuscripts in the camphorwood chest for most of the night.

Letters. Copies of judicial documents. Loose scraps. Broken pieces of ink.

Most I have seen before.

But tonight I found several things I had never noticed.

Among them was a copied letter tucked between correspondence addressed to Wang Shen in the third year of Yuanfeng. The paper was slightly newer than the others, as though it had been copied again at a later date.

It read:

> **“To His Excellency Commandant Wang:**
>
> I am grateful that you have not rejected my recommendation of the clerk Gao Qiu. Having observed the man's quickness of mind and adequate command of brush and ink, I am willing to compose a letter recommending him for presentation before His Highness the Prince Duan…”

The ink was pine-soot ink.

The brush was Father's usual purple-hair brush, cut at an oblique angle.

When he wrote the two characters **Gao Qiu**, the strokes of the character *gao* were slightly hurried, as though someone had urged him to hurry.

I stared at those two lines.

Again and again, my fingertip passed over the paper.

The ink had long since sunk into the fibers.

There was no way to rub it away.

**Gao Qiu.**

Today, no child in Bianjing needs an explanation when that name is spoken.

Grand Marshal.

Commander of the Imperial Guard.

Wherever he goes, the Imperial escort clears the road before him.

His residence occupies nearly half a street.

But forty-one years ago—

In the first years of Yuanyou, I was fourteen, memorizing texts in Meishan. Father would occasionally return from court and mention, almost casually:

> “The Commandant's household has sent another clever clerk asking for a recommendation.”

We never even stopped eating.

Just another clerk.

Every day, dozens of clerks, painters, musicians, monks, Taoists, disgraced officials, failed examination candidates, and petitioners passed through Father's hands.

He wrote recommendation letters the way a man might give away a bowl of porridge:

Warm it.

Hand it over.

Forget it.

He could never have imagined that one bowl of porridge would grow into a Grand Marshal's mansion.

---

For those three months when Father was gravely ill, I kept watch beside his bed.

The cabin of the boat at Jiahe was narrow.

Father had grown so thin that he seemed almost weightless. The bones of his wrists protruded like the roots of an ancient tree.

Once, he emerged from his feverish stupor and grasped my fingers.

His grip was unexpectedly strong.

“Guo'er,” he said, “your father has lived his life without failing his writing, without failing the people…”

He paused to breathe.

Then he said:

> “But there are some things, my son, that I do not know whether I did rightly.”

At the time, I thought he meant Wutai.

The *Huzhou Thank-You Memorial*.

Those lines of his writing that had been intercepted, pulled apart, and placed before the knife-sharp pens of the clerks.

All his life, Father had struggled with his own writing.

He wrote too truthfully.

He offended too deeply.

He was exiled too far.

I thought what he regretted was simply this:

*I should not have written those things.*

Tonight, sitting before this copied letter, I finally understand the other meaning.

He did not regret **what he had written**.

He regretted **whom he had written it for**.

---

## Thirteenth Day of the Twelfth Month · Clear

I went to see Li Sicheng today.

Vice Director of the Ministry of Personnel.

One of Father's students from the third year of Yuanfeng.

When I was a child, he used to come to our house for meals. Father would laugh and scold him:

> “You eat more than I do, you wretch.”

Li would clasp his hands dramatically and beg:

> “Master, spare me!”

His chopsticks, however, never stopped reaching for the braised pork knuckle.

He still clasps his hands in exactly the same way.

Only now his sleeves are Sichuan brocade.

An official tablet hangs from his belt.

And when he smiles, a layer of caution has settled into the wrinkles beside his eyes.

We exchanged a few pleasantries.

Tea arrived.

I asked directly:

“Tell me about Lin Chong's case. How was it actually concluded?”

The hand holding his cup stopped in midair.

“…Which Lin Chong?”

“The instructor of the eighty-thousand Imperial Guards. Cangzhou. The fodder depot.”

I watched him.

He put down the cup.

The porcelain struck the tabletop with a sharp little sound.

Like something had cracked.

“Shudang,” he said, lowering his voice until it became almost a thread, “you have been away all these years. There are some things…”

He stopped.

“Do not ask.”

“I am not asking about *things*.”

I looked at him.

“I am asking about the case file.”

He said nothing.

Outside the window, sunlight broke through a gap in the clouds.

It was painfully bright.

Half his face turned white beneath it.

After a long silence, he finally forced the words through his teeth:

> “This was personally ordered by Grand Marshal Gao.”

There was nothing more to say.

There did not need to be.

---

I rose to leave.

He walked me as far as the second gate.

Then, suddenly, he called my name from behind.

I turned.

His lips moved.

For a moment I thought he was going to say something important.

In the end, he said only:

> “…It is cold. Wear another layer.”

---

Beyond the alley was the Imperial Avenue.

Yesterday's snow had melted by half an inch.

This morning it froze into a thin sheet of ice.

Horse hooves clicked and cracked across it.

The vendors selling steamed cakes.

The men selling sour-plum drinks—

in winter, no less, though there are always people selling them.

The men renting donkey carts.

The Imperial patrols carrying sabers.

Everything was as lively as it had been forty years ago.

The same Vermilion Bird Gate.

The same Rainbow Bridge.

The same people kneeling in the dust raised by the hooves of great horses.

Father once wrote in Huangzhou:

> *“What is life wherever we go?*
> *Perhaps it is like a wild goose*
> *stepping across snow and mud.”*

At the time, he truly believed it.

We arrive.

We leave.

Our footprints remain briefly upon the snow.

The snow melts.

And the footprints disappear.

It does not matter.

The world is so vast.

What can one person's trace possibly mean?

But Lin Chong's footprints were not made in snow and mud.

They were knife marks.

Branding irons.

The red that seeped from the snow after the fire at the fodder depot.

And Gao Qiu's footprints were different still.

He walked upon those red stains.

Stone by stone.

Tile by tile.

Until they became the nine steps leading to the gates of his Grand Marshal's residence.

*The wild goose crosses the snow.*

But who ever asked what was buried beneath the mud?

---

## Seventeenth Day of the Twelfth Month · Snow

The case file came through Second Brother Chen.

Not from the Ministry of Personnel.

The paper there is the kind that leaves a stench on your hands simply by touching it.

Chen's connection is in the old archives of the Three Offices.

He paid a night watchman to copy the documents.

When the copying was finished, the papers were wrapped in oilcloth, stuffed inside the belly of a fish, and delivered to me.

Thick.

And yet thin.

What does that mean?

Every page is filled with words.

And yet everything that truly matters lies in the spaces between them.

Like water seeping downward through cracks in ice.

You can see the damp stain.

You cannot scoop out a single drop.

---

The language of the exile order is perfectly official:

> “Assigned to service in the Cangzhou prison compound; twenty strokes to the back; caged and displayed to the public…”

When I reached the line:

> “His wife, Zhang, shall be transferred by the Grand Marshal's office to her family for remarriage at their discretion…”

my fingers unconsciously tightened around the edge of the paper.

*For remarriage at their discretion.*

Four characters in the official language.

Clean.

That is what such wording is supposed to be.

Clean.

A person no longer needs to be called a person.

She becomes:

*the wife.*

*the assigned prisoner.*

*an auxiliary item under the disposition of household assets.*

---

The account of the fire was even more concise:

> “Fire arose at the fodder depot. Guard Lin Chong disappeared and was presumed burned to death therein. Subsequently, villagers reported three bodies near the Mountain God Temple, identified as Lu Qian and others. Death was caused by spear wounds, the weapon being consistent with the injuries. It is therefore inferred that Lin Chong, while fleeing, killed them in revenge.”

**Inferred.**

One word.

And an entire night was sealed inside an official coffin.

But I know the coffin was never completely nailed shut.

Because on the final page of the appendix, someone had drawn a thin circle in vermilion ink beneath the words:

**Mountain God Temple.**

It was not the usual mark of an official review.

It did not look like the notation of a superior.

It looked more like—

someone had reached this point while reading,

stopped,

and without realizing it,

let the tip of his brush follow the pause,

drawing a small circle.

Who drew it?

I do not know.

But that circle suddenly made me hear the ruined temple.

Not the fighting.

History cannot write fighting.

And whatever it writes will almost certainly be false.

No.

I heard the wind.

I heard the fine grains of snow striking the broken wooden door with a dry, relentless sound, like teeth grinding.

I saw the clay statue of the god with half its face collapsed.

Mold growing through the cracks in its earthen body.

I smelled pinewood smoke mixed with the metallic scent of blood.

Lin Chong was crouching beneath the altar table—

No.

Not crouching.

**Curled up.**

His spear lay across his knees.

The cloth wrapped around his legs was soaked through with melted snow.

His feet had already gone numb.

Then came firelight outside.

And voices.

He knew that voice.

That was the cruelest part.

**He knew it.**

Lu Qian.

The man who had trained with him since childhood.

The man who had warmed his wine.

Who had taken the whip for him.

Who, when Lin Chong was being sent into exile, had secretly slipped him a packet of wound medicine.

And now—

he was standing outside the temple,

telling the prison warden:

> “Go inside and search.”

---

Father once said something in the eighth year of Yuanyou.

Gao Qiu was still serving in the household of Prince Duan.

He did not even possess a proper official rank.

Father came home from court that day.

The porridge had gone cold.

He did not hurry to eat.

Instead, he traced several invisible characters across the tabletop with the tip of his chopsticks.

Then he said:

> “Guo'er, the higher a man climbs, the more people he has stepped on beneath him.
>
> You cannot see those people because they have already—
>
> not died—
>
> but sunk.
>
> Sunk so deeply into the mud that even their bubbles no longer rise.”

At the time, I nodded.

I thought he was explaining a principle.

Now, turning these pages of the case file, I understand.

He was not explaining a principle.

**He was trembling.**

A scholar who spent his entire life writing on behalf of the people of the world eventually discovered that, without meaning to, he had handed the first brick to a man who climbed by stepping on others.

He knew what that brick weighed.

---

When I closed the case file, dawn was almost here.

The oil in the lamp had burned dry.

The wick had curled into a short blackened stub.

Like an eye that refused to go completely dark.

---

## Twentieth Day of the Twelfth Month · Heavy Snow

Mother said last autumn:

> “Those old papers of your father's—choose some and burn them. They're only taking up space. What if someone comes looking again one day…”

She never finished.

She knew she could not.

*What if someone comes looking again one day…*

Looking for what?

The scar of the Wutai Poetry Case had long since formed a scab.

But beneath the scab, the flesh was still soft.

Touch it, and it would bleed.

That was the fate of all Father's writing.

When he wrote, it was blood from his heart.

Once preserved, it became evidence.

---

But that recommendation letter—

it had long since ceased to exist merely on paper.

It lives in Gao Qiu's official tablet.

In the height of the threshold of his Grand Marshal's residence.

In the tattoo burned into Lin Chong's back.

In the bent neck of an old pear seller on the streets of Bianjing as he stoops to retrieve fruit from the mud.

Yes.

Today I walked along the Imperial Avenue again.

Grand Marshal Gao's sedan came from the direction of the Great Xiangguo Temple.

Eight bearers.

Silver roof.

Vermilion curtains.

Imperial guards marching before it.

An old pear seller was half a step too slow to move aside.

One of the attendants kicked over his basket.

Pears rolled into the mud.

The old man dropped to his knees to gather them.

His fingers were red from the cold, the color of carrots.

He picked up one pear.

Wiped it.

Then another.

But he did not dare put them back into the basket.

Another attendant had already crushed the basket beneath his foot.

Everyone nearby looked for a moment.

Then, as one, they looked away.

They did it with astonishing ease.

Like muscle memory.

---

Father once wrote:

> “Under Heaven, all bustle is driven by profit.”

But the bustle of profit has never been meant for the old man.

---

Tonight, while searching through the trunks, I found a scrap of paper at the very bottom of a blue cloth bundle.

It was older than the recommendation letter.

Its edges had frayed.

The ink had faded into little more than a thread of ochre-gray.

I had to hold it above the flame to make out the words:

> “To save one person is to harm ten.
> To assist one man in power is to wound a hundred.
> At the moment we put brush to paper, we must remember:
> what lies beneath the brush is not ink—
> it is a blade.
>
> A blade recognizes no person.
> It recognizes only the strength of the hand that wields it.”

There was no signature.

I do not know in which year of Yuanfeng it was written.

Nor in what room.

Perhaps it was written after wine, in a moment of sobriety.

Perhaps during some lonely night when Father sat alone and imposed a warning upon himself.

But the place where I found it was strange.

It lay directly beneath the copied recommendation letter.

As if someone had deliberately placed it there as a sheet of waterproof paper.

He knew what he had done.

Not afterward.

**At the time.**

He knew.

The warning lay beneath his wrist as he wrote.

And still—

he finished the letter.

That is the heaviest part.

It was not a mistake born of ignorance.

It was a mistake made **with knowledge**.

The deepest abyss a good man can fall into is not becoming evil.

It is seeing exactly where the piece of paper will lead—

and telling himself:

> *It is only a recommendation letter.*

And then sending it anyway.

---

## Twenty-Third Day of the Twelfth Month · Snow

One more thing.

Today I was sorting through Father's exchange poems and letters with his friends.

I found a line from his reply to the monk Canliao in the fifth year of Yuanyou:

> “Though I have stumbled many times, I remain without shame when I look upward or downward; Heaven and the spirits may bear witness.”

He truly believed that then.

He believed that if a man could live without shame before Heaven and earth, that was enough.

He believed that if his writing could stand, then he himself could stand.

Later, when the Wutai authorities dragged him into prison,

when they dragged him to the edge of life and death,

he never entirely abandoned that belief.

He merely narrowed the meaning of *without shame*.

Narrowed it further.

And further.

Until, in the end, it became a corner so small that even one recommendation letter could no longer fit inside it.

Yet the recommendation letter was there.

Right where it should not have been.

---

I cannot defend him.

Nor do I want to.

To defend him would only make the burden lighter.

What I can do is this:

Gather these papers.

Arrange them by year.

Tie them together.

Return them to the camphorwood chest.

Place two packets of camphor inside.

Cover them with blue cloth.

When I told Mother, “Keep them,” she did not ask me to burn them again.

She only looked at me.

There was a kind of understanding in that look that perhaps only an old woman can possess.

The understanding that what I was keeping was not paper.

It was a lesson growing out of bone.

Growing until her son, too, had to feel its pain.

---

The snow is still falling outside.

One flake at a time.

Each carrying its own weight.

Father believed all his life in the wild goose crossing snow and mud.

The snow would melt.

The mud would dry.

The footprints would disappear.

But he forgot to ask one question:

**What happens to what lies beneath the mud after the mud has dried?**

Enough.

I turned the wick up a little higher.

I took the copied recommendation letter for Gao Qiu from the stack.

I did not burn it.

I did not hide it.

I placed it on top.

Let it remain visible.

Let him remain visible.

The snow melted.

Spring came.

---

## Postscript · Colophon to the Meishan Su Family Shrine Copy

This manuscript was preserved in the same envelope as Su Shi's original handwriting, inside a lacquered box in the western pavilion of the Su family ancestral shrine.

During the restoration of the shrine in the forty-second year of the Wanli reign, the box was opened for airing.

Between the pages, a later hand had written, in characters no larger than flies:

> “Master Guo never went to Liangshan, nor did he pursue Lin Chong.
> He remained in the old residence, ordered his father's manuscripts, and died in the early years of Shaoxing.
>
> From this entry onward, the diary contains not a single word concerning Gao or Lin.
>
> Only seven characters appear in the blank space of the final page:
>
> **The snow melted. Spring had come.**
>
> The ink was faint, like a swallowed cry.”

— *Respectfully inscribed in the seventh year of the Republic, by Dezhang, a descendant of the Su family of Meishan.*
