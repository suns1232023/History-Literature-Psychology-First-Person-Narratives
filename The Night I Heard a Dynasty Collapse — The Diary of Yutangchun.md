# **The Night I Heard a Dynasty Collapse — The Diary of Yutangchun**

> *“Su San has left Hongdong County,*
> *and now walks alone into the street.*
>
> *Before I have even spoken, my heart is already broken.*
> *Gentlemen passing by, listen to what I have to say…”*
>
> — *The Peking Opera The Rescue of Su San*

Before I began writing this diary, there was one question I could not stop asking myself:

**How many times, in all the centuries of our lives, have women ever truly been allowed to decide for themselves?**

My name is Su San.

In the capital, I was known as Yutangchun, a famous courtesan of the Imperial Brothel Quarter.

It was during the Zhengde Emperor's reign. A eunuch named Liu Jin still held enormous power at court, and to ordinary eyes, the empire seemed to be carrying on as usual.

But after I passed through the prison at Hongdong, escaped a road that should have ended in death, watched Mr. Fang Mizhi write poetry by lamplight, and later heard that the capital had changed emperors, that its walls had grown lower, and that the Jurchens had come—

only then did I begin to understand something.

**You cannot rely on emperors' men. You cannot rely on scholars' passion. In the end, the only thing you can rely on is the life in your own hands, and that small piece of pain you carry inside your chest.**

It is just past the Mid-Autumn Festival.

Tonight, Wang and I returned to his old residence in the capital. I lay awake beneath the covers, unable to sleep. Mr. Fang's words kept returning to me.

He once said:

**“A scholar may survive without reading. But if he reads and still cannot see where the world hurts, then he has wasted his life.”**

I never learned how to read.

But my entire life has been pain.

So let me tell you what I know.

---

## **Night One: When I Look Back, All I See Are Pits in the Road**

I had neither father nor mother.

When I was five, a pair of hands pushed me away.

**That was a human life. It was also a transaction.**

Only much later did I understand that the day I was sold into the Suhuai establishment, I ceased to be treated as a child and became something to be priced, displayed, and consumed.

When I was little, I understood nothing. I only knew how to cry.

When I grew older, I realized something much worse:

**I had not chosen this road. The road had chosen my life for me.**

The madam of the Suhuai establishment was not entirely cruel. Nor was she kind.

She looked at my face and said, “This girl has good features. She'll make us a fortune.”

So I learned.

I learned songs.

I learned the pipa.

I learned how to smile at men.

I learned how to cry for them.

Eventually, I could no longer tell which smiles were false and which tears were real.

At fifteen, I was given the name **Yutangchun**.

That was when I began receiving guests.

People like to speak of the pleasure quarters of the Ming dynasty in beautiful words: flower streets, embroidered chambers, painted towers, lanterns reflected in wine.

But once you entered one, you learned the truth.

**You were a piece of porcelain waiting to be opened.**

Those who knew your value might hold you carefully for a few days, then move on to another.

Those who did not might break your body and complain that you were not beautiful enough.

There were rules outsiders never saw.

Inside the courtesan houses, there were coded expressions for everything—being summoned, staying the night, receiving a guest. The books record these things in elegant language.

But underneath the elegance were wounds.

What did a famous courtesan live on?

Talent.

Beauty.

And a carefully measured illusion of personality.

Along the Qinhuai River, scholars could throw away fortunes for a song. You played the pipa, and they called you sincere. You recited a sentimental poem, and they praised your spirit.

But say one thing they did not want to hear, and they would turn around and leave.

**Do you know what my greatest talent was?**

Not music.

Not poetry.

Not painting.

It was making a man believe that I cared.

**The truth was that I cared about almost none of it.**

Later I read somewhere that the courtesan culture of the Ming dynasty represented the highest expression of women's culture in China, an organic union of the ancient tradition of female entertainers and the registered entertainment system.

The highest?

Perhaps.

**When so many women were thrown from the mountain, the people at the bottom simply learned to catch them, one after another.**

Who cared how badly they had fallen?

That was when I met Wang.

I cannot remember whether I was fifteen or sixteen.

He wore a blue scholar's robe. His face was pale and clean. He carried a folding fan. When he entered the room, he lowered his head slightly, like a scholar who had never learned how to turn sharply.

That night I sang.

He listened.

He was the only man in the room who did not look at my body.

**He looked into my eyes.**

I told myself:

Something is wrong.

Something is very wrong.

But I could not stop myself.

**Even if it was a road to death, I wanted to walk it once.**

Wang took thirty thousand taels that had been entrusted to Wang Lin. He borrowed travel money from Wang Ding. Then he spent everything on me.

I was frightened by how much money disappeared.

But I was greedy for those days, too.

**When a person's heart has lost its balance, who thinks about money?**

Eventually the money was gone.

And Wang was gone with it.

Not dead.

Thrown out.

The madam expelled him.

I was furious. Terrified.

That night I slipped out to find him.

I found the man who had once lived like a young nobleman curled up beside the wall of a ruined Guandi Temple.

He had wrapped himself in a torn quilt he had found somewhere.

The cold wind came straight through it.

His whole body was shaking.

I crouched down and held him.

He whispered:

“San'er. I'm sorry.”

I told him:

“Don't say you're sorry. Go back. Take the examinations. Pass them. Then come back and marry me.”

He bit his lip and nodded hard.

I gave him everything I had saved.

Even the silver hairpin.

I remembered every gift I had ever received from a guest.

The hairpin had been given to me by a tea merchant from Henan. He had drunk three cups of wine and tried to force himself on me. I refused him.

He threw down some silver and cursed me for being ungrateful.

Then he slammed the hairpin onto the table.

I returned the money.

I kept the hairpin.

At the time I believed there were two kinds of men in this world:

Those who gave me gold.

And those who gave me devotion.

I thought I would never forget the second kind.

**Only later did I learn that devotion, like gold, can also be spent.**

---

## **Night Two: Mr. Fang Told Me the Great House Was Already Falling**

After Wang returned to the capital, I entered the Shen household.

And with it, I entered the prison at Hongdong.

I do not want to speak much about those years.

There is only one thing I remember clearly:

**The farther down you went, the darker the world became.**

The magistrate accepted money from the Pi family and decided that I had murdered my husband.

They strapped me to the wooden frame.

The first blow struck my skin.

Then my flesh.

Then my bones.

Eventually, I lost consciousness.

They said that once the torture began, confession was no longer a matter of truth.

**“Confessed under torture” sounds like four simple words.**

But those four words are heavy enough to crush a life.

I thought about dying.

Many times.

The prison at Hongdong was dark.

Rats crawled over my body. Eventually I stopped trying to chase them away.

At night I was afraid even to cry.

Because I knew that if I began, I might never be able to stop.

When I was locked inside the place that later generations would call **Su San Prison**, I truly believed I would never see another spring.

But the reason I kept living became simpler and simpler:

**I wanted to see him one more time.**

Just once.

The day I finally climbed out of that darkness, climbed into the prison cart, and was taken away under guard, I understood why people later said:

“Hongdong County has no good people.”

It was not because I hated the county itself.

I hated the people who had turned truth upside down.

But hatred does not give you power.

Fear does not give you power either.

What could I do?

Could I take the imperial examinations?

Could I become an official?

No.

At most, I could curse them from inside a prison cart.

Then I saw Wang again.

He was no longer the young scholar I remembered.

He was an imperial inspector overseeing eight prefectures.

He wore a red official robe.

His face had hardly changed.

His eyes were still gentle.

But I did not dare believe it was him.

The first sentence—I could not recognize him.

The second—I was afraid to.

Surrounded by all those eyes, I was terrified that I was merely another case file on his desk.

**It had come down to a thread.**

If he had never passed the examinations—

if he had never become an inspector—

if he had never been sent to Shanxi—

then all the blood I had shed would have been for nothing.

There is an old saying:

*“We are both wanderers at the edge of the world; why ask whether we have met before?”*

It is true.

But we were more than two fallen people.

We were two lives that had reached out in the middle of the road, caught each other for a moment, and almost let go.

Fortunately, this time,

**we held on.**

I met Mr. Fang Mizhi later.

By then he had already left official life.

He wore a gray monk's robe and lived in a simple earthen house in the countryside near Tongcheng.

Beside the inkstone on his desk lay a short sword.

The scabbard was rusted.

The night wind moved through his hair.

Half of it was black.

Half was white.

Wang took me there and told me that Mr. Fang's learning reached from heaven to earth.

That night, Fang said something I would remember for the rest of my life.

He said:

**“I am a man who has watched the sky collapse. You do not know how the scholars of the late Ming slowly walked themselves into a dead end.”**

I did not understand what it meant for a great house to collapse.

I did not understand the decline of the scholar-officials.

But I knew something of his story.

He came from the Fang family of Tongcheng. His father had served as a high official of the Ming court.

When Li Zicheng's forces entered Beijing, Fang wept before the spirit of the Chongzhen Emperor.

Then he was captured.

Imprisoned.

He escaped during the chaos and eventually reached Nanjing.

There, he was falsely accused again and escaped only by disguising himself.

Under the Southern Ming, he obtained another official position.

It did not last.

He was dismissed after another accusation.

He eventually fled to Lingnan.

He changed his name.

He set up a small stall in the marketplace.

He sold medicine.

He treated the sick.

He pushed his cart through the streets, carrying herbs and needles from house to house.

No one knew that this quiet, white-haired medicine seller was Fang Mizhi of Tongcheng—

the former Vice Director of the Ministry of Works,

the former Director of the Ministry of Rites,

the man who had once stood beside the emperor and read countless memorials.

Then the Southern Ming fell too.

As Qing forces moved south, he fled with his family.

He could not always protect even his wife and children.

Eventually he became a monk.

His Buddhist name was Dazhi.

People called him the Medicine Monk, *Yaodi*.

But in his heart he carried one conviction:

**He owed a life to the fallen dynasty.**

Mr. Fang knew he could no longer change the world.

Yet he continued to do what he could.

He called it nothing grand.

He simply called it:

**remaining clean.**

I once asked him:

“Master, don't you ever get tired?”

He smiled.

“Yes.”

Then he said:

**“But is that not what it means to be human?”**

When he said that, I suddenly thought of my own years in Hongdong.

Being beaten was painful.

Being imprisoned was painful.

But I never admitted to a crime I had not committed.

Behind iron bars, with rats crawling through the darkness, my bones aching and sweat pouring down my face—

I still refused.

**Then I realized that men and women are not so different after all.**

The kind of strength that allows a person to endure does not live in the bones.

**It lives somewhere no one can see.**

That night Mr. Fang showed us a manuscript called *The Compendium of Physics*.

The pages were crowded with notes on astronomy, geography, plants, anatomy—everything he believed worth understanding.

He drew the human skeleton with his brush, measuring every part.

He believed that the world should be observed.

Measured.

Calculated.

I wanted to ask:

Master, can you measure the human heart?

Can you calculate a person's fate?

Can all the things in this world that make no sense be reduced to numbers?

I said nothing.

But I knew this:

While I was fighting a legal battle in Hongdong, he was fighting a far larger lawsuit—the lawsuit of an entire collapsing world.

If I died in prison, perhaps my name would survive in a few unofficial stories.

But the dynasty he had lost would remain in history for centuries.

**The coffin on his shoulders was heavier than the prison I had carried.**

So he did not call himself tired.

And perhaps neither could I.

---

## **Night Three: Madam Li Shishi, Can You Hear Me?**

Mr. Fang told me another story.

It kept me awake for many nights.

He said that in the final years of the Northern Song, there had been a woman whose fate was perhaps even harsher than mine.

Her name was **Li Shishi**.

I used to think she was the lucky one among women like us.

She had seen an emperor.

She had received imperial gifts.

She could sing a song and make Emperor Huizong leave the palace secretly at night to visit her.

A single word from her could carry the weight of half a court.

Mr. Fang said:

She was not lucky.

She lost her father when she was four and was adopted by a courtesan known as Madam Li.

From that moment on, she belonged to the pleasure quarters.

She became famous.

But every glittering banquet has another side.

Every feast may hide a knife.

She knew the poet Chao Chongzhi.

Later she was associated, in various accounts, with Qin Guan and Zhou Bangyan.

The official histories barely mention her.

Unofficial histories say much more.

Some claim Emperor Huizong summoned her to court and granted her a title.

Others say the stories were inventions.

But one date cannot be invented:

**1127.**

The second year of the Jingkang era.

The Jin forces entered Bianjing.

The capital was surrounded.

Burned.

Looted.

Emperor Huizong and his son, Emperor Qinzong, were captured and taken north.

The imperial family was dragged away.

The women of the palace were taken with them.

The city that had once seemed eternal was broken almost overnight.

During the Chongning and Daguan eras, Li Shishi may not have been a true imperial consort.

But if she wanted to survive, she still had to live by the emperor's favor.

Then the emperor was gone.

There was no palace left for her.

She gave away her possessions.

Some accounts say she became a Daoist nun.

Others say she swallowed a golden hairpin and died.

Still others say she wandered south into the regions of Hunan and Hubei, where nobody recognized her.

Mr. Fang stopped speaking.

It was as if something bitter had caught in his throat.

Then he said quietly:

“She was a friend.”

He called her *the Flying General*.

And then he said:

**“In the end, Li Shishi could not even piece together a complete life for herself. Once you leave the capital, you become yesterday's newspaper.”**

I felt cold all over.

**Women like us survive on a face and a song.**

But what happens when the emperor disappears?

Who are you singing for?

When the dynasty changes its name, who remembers your beautiful verses?

Li Shishi lived during the Northern Song.

I lived during the Zhengde reign of the Ming.

Centuries separated us.

But we walked the same road.

**We were, in the end, the same kind of woman.**

We stood on the same barbed wire and sang for men.

Some of those men were poets.

Some were fools.

Some commanded armies.

Some begged in the streets.

But Li Shishi had it worse than I did.

I had Wang.

Someone came back for me.

And her Emperor Huizong?

He was a captive in the Five-Nation City.

The emperor himself had been taken.

Who was left to redeem anyone?

That was when I finally understood:

**What women like us fear is not the cruelty of the men who come through the door.**

**What we fear is discovering that the shelter we trusted is sinking into the mud too.**

---

## **Epilogue: At Last, I Understand**

The days passed.

Autumn deepened.

Today Wang walked with me through the garden.

He pointed toward an old locust tree, slightly crooked with age.

“That was the tree beside the Guandi Temple where you once slept,” he said.

“Look.

It survived.”

I looked at the tree.

Its leaves were yellow and green.

Its trunk was dry.

But it was still standing.

And suddenly I thought:

**So did I.**

Mr. Fang once read my fortune.

I did not listen to all of it.

But I remember his final words.

He said:

**“When people tear down their old lives and begin again, only then do they discover what it means to live. Let the dynasty rise or fall. Let the emperor remain or disappear. In the end, your life is still in your own hands.”**

I do not believe in fortune-telling.

But I believe those words.

He carried the Ming dynasty through his hands.

I carried prison through my body.

The fact that either of us survived long enough to speak about it was already a kind of stubbornness.

Li Shishi.

Zhou Bangyan.

The Zhengde Emperor.

The Chongzhen Emperor.

Dynasties rise and fall.

The dead do not return.

But those of us who are still breathing must somehow finish living the lives that history interrupted.

Tonight the wind is strong.

I am writing beneath the lamp.

Wang is already asleep.

His breathing is steady.

There are more wrinkles on his face than when we first met, but his eyes are still gentle.

I put down my brush and quietly pull the blanket over his shoulder.

Across half the street, Mr. Fang's lamp is still burning.

He is standing by the window, looking at a chart of the stars.

He looks down.

Then up.

The night wind moves through his hair.

It is almost entirely white now.

And suddenly I wonder:

Did Li Shishi ever stand beside such a window and look outside?

In the year she died, was there anyone who pulled a blanket over her shoulders?

**We all walk from one end of history toward the other.**

One step at a time.

When we have no strength left, we stop for a while.

Then tomorrow comes.

And we walk again.

The wind of Hongdong has long since passed.

A new northern wind is now slipping through the cracks of the capital.

And tonight, at last, I understand one thing:

**The most reliable thing in this world is not the sweet words spoken by men.**

**It is not a fortune of gold.**

**It is not even the face that once made men turn their heads.**

It is this:

**When everything around you is falling apart, you must still know who you are.**

Whether the world belongs to an emperor or to a beggar, whether one dynasty replaces another, if that bone inside you remains unbroken, then your life can remain your own.

Mr. Fang taught me that.

Li Shishi taught me that.

And perhaps all those women who disappeared into history taught me the same lesson.

**I am Su San.**

**And this time, I will not betray that truth.**
