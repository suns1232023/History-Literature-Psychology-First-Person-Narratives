### A Plain-Spoken Monologue by Jingwen

*— Midnight on the Xiang River, While Guarding the Scriptures*

The wind is cold tonight.

The river keeps striking the side of the boat, again and again, like someone knocking on my heart.

I know they are coming.

There are footsteps on the shore. I can see firelight moving in the darkness.

I do not move. I do not make a sound.

I only hold the chest containing the scriptures a little tighter.

Inside is the *Lotus Sutra*.

It represents twenty years.

Not paper.

My blood. My fingers. My nights.

When I first entered the monastery, I was only an ordinary monk. I recited sutras, swept the floor, ate, slept.

Then my master asked me to copy the scriptures.

He said, “Your mind is quiet. Your characters are steady.”

So I copied.

At first, I did not think much of it.

But the more I copied, the less willing I became to do it carelessly.

When I pierced my finger, it hurt.

The first time hurt terribly.

After that, every time still hurt.

Blood would run onto the paper, darkening the characters. I would write while reciting the Buddha's name. Sometimes there was not enough blood, so I would wait.

I would wait for more.

Some people said I was mad.

“Why not simply use ink?”

I never explained.

It was not because I believed blood made the scriptures more sacred.

It was because I did not trust myself.

People become lazy. People cut corners. People write a character badly and think, *I can always rewrite it.*

But once you write with blood, there is no easy way back.

Every character costs pain.

And pain makes you remember.

I had always intended to take the completed scripture to Jizu Mountain, the sacred place associated with Mahākāśyapa.

It was clean there.

Safe.

The scriptures would not simply be thrown away. They would not be soaked by rain or burned because some later generation found them inconvenient.

I had no other child.

This was what I had to leave behind.

That day, when the bandits boarded the boat, everyone else jumped into the river.

I watched them swimming, bare-chested, struggling against the current.

I did not move.

The scriptures were on the boat.

They rushed toward me.

I saw the flash of their knives.

I smelled the smoke from the torches.

I stood there with the chest behind me.

One of them shouted,

“Monk, aren't you going to run?”

I said,

“I can't.”

They thought I was stupid.

One of them came over and reached for the bundle of scripture pages.

The moment his hand touched them, I threw myself at him.

I do not know where that strength came from.

My body moved before I could think, as if someone else were pushing me forward.

At that moment, I was not thinking about the Buddha.

I was not thinking about the next life.

I was thinking only:

**You cannot touch them.**

They kicked me.

They cursed me.

I heard something crack inside my body. I do not know which bone it was.

Blood came into my mouth.

I tasted it.

The taste was strangely familiar.

It tasted like the blood I had shed while copying the scriptures.

One of them laughed.

“What good are these scriptures? Can you eat them?”

I said nothing.

I knew they would not understand.

And I did not blame them.

Not everyone has to understand.

Eventually, they found things worth more.

Gold.

Silver.

Camels.

Anything that could be traded for wine.

They took those things.

Before leaving, they set the boat on fire.

I watched the flames rise.

Then I rushed toward them.

Bucket after bucket.

Cold river water against the heat of the fire.

By then, I could no longer feel the pain.

They left.

One of them turned around and looked at me.

There was something strange in his eyes.

He was not looking at me as a monk.

Nor was he looking at me as a madman.

It was almost as if he were looking at someone he did not want to become.

The boat was almost completely burned when dawn began to break.

That was when Xu, my benefactor, returned.

He was soaked through.

His face was pale.

He saw me sitting on the deck, holding the scripture chest.

For a long time, he said nothing.

I wanted to speak, but no sound came from my throat.

He crouched down.

Very gently, he touched the chest.

Then he asked:

“Is everything there?”

I nodded.

He let out a breath of relief.

And suddenly, I felt tired.

Not the kind of tiredness that comes from wounds or exhaustion.

It was something deeper.

The tiredness of a person who has finally done what he was supposed to do.

Later, when we reached Nanning, I became very ill.

I knew I would never make it to Jizu Mountain.

One night, I woke up.

The moon outside the window was perfectly round.

I called Xu to my side.

“I won't make it,” I told him.

“But you must take the scriptures there.”

He said,

“I will.”

I shook my head.

“Not for me.”

“Take them for the scriptures.”

He nodded.

Then I was at peace.

Before I died, I thought for a long time.

It seemed that I had never done anything particularly great in my life.

I had not saved many people.

I had left no famous name behind.

I had never even seen Jizu Mountain with my own eyes.

And yet—

I did not feel cheated.

Because the scripture would go.

Once it arrived, my life would have arrived with it.

If someone asked me:

Why didn't you jump into the river that day?

Why would you risk your life for a few sheets of paper?

I would tell them:

**They were not a few sheets of paper.**

**They were the one thing in my entire life that I could not afford to lose.**

If I lost them,

I would lose myself.

If I protected them,

my life would be complete.

That is enough.
