# **Shun’s Diary: The Epic Rise of a Phoenix Man**

## **Diary I: Beneath Mount Li, A Midnight Reverie**

**Youyu Clan · Age Thirty · A Moonlit Night**

Fire.

That fire again.

I woke from the dream drenched in cold sweat. Four thousand days and nights have passed, yet the flames beneath the granary still return to my dreams at midnight, as punctual as ever.

I touched my arm. The skin was intact. There were no scars.

But I know now that some wounds do not live in the flesh. They are carved into the bones, written into the blood, embedded in that extraordinary vigilance that seizes me whenever danger approaches.

The wind from Mount Li slipped through the thatch, carrying the smell of earth and wild beasts.

I rose and walked to the doorway.

Far in the distance, scattered points of light flickered across the darkness. That was my settlement—a place that had grown from nothing, from wilderness into prosperity. In three years, one man had become hundreds, then thousands; barren land had become what people now called a miracle:

**“In one year, a settlement; in two, a town; in three, a city.”**

They all call me Shun.

They say I am virtuous, benevolent, patient, and kind.

But only I know what I am writing tonight.

Not the legend of the sage-king that later generations will sing.

I am writing this so that I will not forget the boy who crawled out of fire and yellow earth, and how he climbed, one step at a time, to where I stand today.

Perhaps this is the confession of a **phoenix man**.

## **Diary II: Childhood — The First Mark in My Blood**

**A Memory · Thirty Years Ago**

My name is Chonghua.

They say I was given that name because my eyes contained two pupils.

But what was the first world those unusual eyes saw?

When I was five, my mother died.

My memories of my birth mother are little more than fragments—a warm embrace, a gentle hand, and the desperate grip of her fingers around mine as she lay dying, her eyes filled with a worry she could not take with her.

I did not understand it then.

By the time I did, she was already beneath the earth.

The day my stepmother entered our house, my father smiled for the first time in a long while.

I stood at the doorway and watched this unfamiliar woman walk into my home with an air of arrogance. Her shadow slowly swallowed the threshold where I stood.

She glanced at me.

I remembered that look for the rest of my life.

It was the look one gives a stray dog.

Or a piece of furniture that no longer serves any purpose.

Then my half-brother was born.

His name was Xiang.

Xiang was my stepmother’s treasure.

He was the opposite of my existence.

He could play all day while I worked from dawn until dark. He ate the best food; I waited until everyone else had finished and gathered whatever scraps remained.

And still, in my stepmother’s eyes, I was an eyesore.

And my father?

His name was Gusou.

He was blind.

Not merely blind in his eyes.

Blind in his heart.

Whenever my stepmother complained about me, my father would sit there in silence.

Eventually, he learned to agree with her.

Then he learned to strike me.

She said I was unfilial.

He beat me.

She said I was lazy.

He cursed me.

Then she said:

“This son will bring disaster if we keep him alive.”

He believed her.

Do you know what that feels like?

Your own father—the man who is supposed to protect you—turns his face away every time you call for him.

His ears hear only one person.

His heart has no room left for you.

And yet I still called him Father.

I still worked.

I still fed the family.

I farmed the fields of Mount Li.

I fished in Lake Lei.

I made pottery by the riverbank.

I traded in Fuxia.

Whatever grain I earned, I brought home.

Whatever fish I caught, I brought home.

Everything I could give, I gave to them.

And then?

**They tried to kill me.**

# **Diary III: The Fire and the Darkness Beneath the Well**

**A Memory · Twenty Years Ago**

The first time was the granary.

It was raining lightly that day.

My stepmother spoke to me with unusual gentleness.

“The roof is leaking. Go up and repair it.”

For a moment, I was even happy.

Perhaps she had finally accepted me.

Perhaps there was still a place for me in this family.

I climbed onto the roof.

Then I smelled smoke.

I looked down.

The ground beneath the granary was already piled with firewood.

Flames were climbing upward with the wind.

My father stood nearby, holding a torch.

He could not see me.

But he knew I was up there.

He knew.

He had lit the fire himself.

At that moment, I understood something.

A man’s death could be made extraordinarily simple.

Burn him alive beneath a collapsing granary.

Then tell everyone it was an accident.

But I did not want to die.

I looked around.

I had only two straw hats.

Without thinking, I spread them like wings and jumped.

The wind screamed past my ears.

When I hit the ground, pain exploded through my knees and palms.

But I was alive.

My father heard the noise.

His face changed.

My stepmother rushed out.

She saw me standing in the courtyard, covered in mud.

For a moment she froze.

Then she forced a smile.

“Oh, Shun! Why did you come down? Your father started the fire by accident. You mustn't blame him…”

I said nothing.

I looked at them.

Then I turned and walked away.

There was a second attempt.

They told me to dig a well.

When I reached a depth of ten meters, the light above suddenly disappeared.

Earth and stones came crashing down.

They struck my head and shoulders.

Then I heard Xiang shouting from above:

“Bury him! Bury him! Now he's finally dead!”

At that moment, I felt neither anger nor grief.

Only recognition.

**They had done it again.**

But they did not know that while digging the well, I had secretly carved a narrow passage into the wall—an escape route leading to another well.

I crawled out through it.

And just as I emerged, I heard Xiang shouting:

“Shun’s house belongs to me! His qin belongs to me! His two wives—”

I stood behind him.

Silently.

He turned.

He saw me.

The expression on his face was extraordinary.

Fear.

Embarrassment.

Disbelief.

Then, finally, a smile more painful than tears.

“Brother… you're back? I—I was just thinking about you.”

I looked at my half-brother.

We shared the same father.

We shared the same blood.

Yet his heart seemed colder than the earth at the bottom of that well.

Still, I nodded.

“Yes,” I said.

“I’m back.”

Then I returned home.

I continued working.

I continued serving my parents.

As if nothing had happened.

People said I was stupid.

They said I was weak.

They said I was not a man.

But they did not understand.

I was enduring.

**Endurance is not weakness.**

**Endurance is carrying your life and your hope through the darkest environment until an opportunity appears.**

# **Diary IV: The Secret in My Blood — The “Pretraining Advantage” of a Phoenix Man**

**Tonight · Reflections**

Some people would call a man like me a **phoenix man**.

Clever.

Patient.

Able to endure hardship.

A man who climbs from a small village into the great world, from mud to the imperial palace.

But they do not understand that a phoenix man is not forged in a single day.

Every person who climbs from the bottom carries three marks:

**early adversity, psychological resilience, and extraordinary vigilance toward danger.**

Recently, I heard of something called **epigenetics**.

They say childhood experiences can alter the way genes are expressed.

Children exposed to severe adversity may develop changes in DNA methylation around genes associated with the glucocorticoid stress response, potentially altering how they respond to stress later in life.

I do not know whether those foreign scholars are entirely correct.

But I know this:

Ever since my mother died when I was five,

ever since my stepmother first beat me bloody,

I became different from other children.

I could read the next insult from my father’s expression.

I could hear from my stepmother’s tone whether she intended to strike me.

I could tell from Xiang’s eyes whether he was planning something.

And when everyone else believed they were safe,

I could feel danger approaching.

That ability kept me alive.

Before the granary caught fire, I smelled smoke.

Before the well collapsed, I had already prepared an escape route.

Every time they tried to harm me, my body reacted before my conscious mind did.

My heart accelerated.

My muscles tightened.

My mind rapidly calculated escape routes.

This was not a gift.

It was the price of survival.

People later said that I understood when to avoid the heavy blows and when to endure the lighter ones.

They said I knew how to protect myself.

But they never understood where that knowledge came from.

It came from every experience in which I nearly died.

It came from a survival instinct carved deep into me.

**The “pretraining advantage” of a phoenix man is paid for with his life.**

And that training shaped everything that came afterward.

When I farmed, I knew what to plant and where.

When I made pottery, I knew how far to raise the temperature.

When I governed people, I knew whom to trust and whom to avoid.

When I faced Yao, I knew what he wanted to see.

I knew what he needed to hear.

None of this was innate.

It had been forged through countless encounters with the edge of the abyss.

# **Diary V: The Day Yao Came**

**A Memory · Fifteen Years Ago**

I was thirty that year.

My name had already traveled far beyond Mount Li.

Not because I was exceptionally clever.

But because I had done something people thought impossible.

After my father and brother had repeatedly tried to kill me,

I continued to honor them.

I continued to treat them well.

“There's a filial son at Mount Li,” people said.

“His own parents tried to kill him, yet he bears them no grudge.”

The story traveled from one mouth to another.

From Mount Li to Lake Lei.

From Lake Lei to Yao’s capital.

And then Yao came.

The first time I saw him, I had just returned from the fields.

I was covered in mud.

He stood outside my hut wearing ordinary clothes, looking like an old farmer.

But I immediately knew he was not ordinary.

There was a light in his eyes.

The kind of light possessed only by a man who has known power.

He asked me:

“I want peace throughout the realm. What do you think?”

I thought for a moment.

Then I said:

“Do not be partial.

Do not deceive.

Do not break your promises.”

He stared at me.

Then he laughed.

Years later, I learned that he remembered those words for the rest of his life.

But what truly changed my destiny was what came next.

He married his two daughters to me.

Ehuang and Nüying.

People later said Yao was testing me.

He was using his daughters to examine whether my virtue was genuine.

But I knew it was more than that.

**It was a political marriage.**

---

# **Diary VI: Marriage — Surveillance and Launchpad**

**A Memory · Fifteen Years Ago**

On the day I married Ehuang and Nüying, I understood perfectly.

They were not merely my wives.

They were Yao’s eyes.

His ears.

His informants.

Yao wanted them to watch me.

To see whether I truly possessed the virtue people attributed to me.

He wanted them to observe me in private.

To see whether I remained righteous when no one else was watching.

He wanted them to report my every movement.

To determine whether I crossed the invisible boundaries of power.

But Yao forgot one thing.

**Human hearts are alive.**

After Ehuang and Nüying came to live with me, I did not treat them as agents sent from above.

I treated them as wives.

My kindness toward them was not an act.

It was genuine.

I taught them farming.

I listened to them.

I protected them when they were wronged.

I stayed beside them when they were afraid.

Slowly, they began speaking on my behalf.

Slowly, they began planning for me.

Slowly, they stopped being merely Yao’s daughters.

They became **Shun’s wives**.

Later, Yao’s nine sons came as well.

Officially, they were there to accompany and assist me.

But what were they really?

Another surveillance network.

I treated them exactly as I had treated their sisters.

With sincerity.

With trust.

With an open heart.

And what happened?

Yao’s nine sons became my allies.

His two daughters became my advisers.

Once, Yao sent someone to inquire about me.

Ehuang stopped the messenger at the door.

Later she quietly told me:

“Some people around my father are dissatisfied with you.

Be careful.”

That was the moment I understood.

My marriage had transformed itself.

What began as a **surveillance network** had become an **information network**.

Years later, someone asked me how I managed to marry Yao’s daughters.

I told him:

“I did not marry them.

I turned them into my people.”

**That is the survival wisdom of a phoenix man.**

You want me to marry into your family?

Then I will turn marriage into roots.

You want to monitor me?

Then I will turn your surveillance network into my information network.

# **Diary VII: Twenty-Eight Years of Patience — Boiling Yao Slowly**

**A Memory · During the Regency**

Yao did not hand power to me overnight.

First, he made me **Situ**, responsible for social order and education.

Then **Sima**, responsible for military affairs.

Finally, he made me regent, allowing me to exercise imperial authority as he grew old.

The process lasted twenty-eight years.

Twenty-eight years.

Long enough for a tree to become a forest.

Long enough for a young man to grow old.

And long enough for me to accomplish three things.

### **First: Dismantle the Old Guard**

Yao had four powerful men:

**Gong Gong. Huan Dou. San Miao. Gun.**

Gong Gong controlled water management.

Huan Dou was one of Yao’s trusted men.

San Miao commanded military power.

Gun had spent nine years attempting to control the floods.

They were Yao’s right and left hands.

The real power brokers of the empire.

I removed them one by one under the banner of incompetence.

Gong Gong was exiled to Youzhou.

Huan Dou to Chongshan.

San Miao to Sanwei.

Gun died at Mount Yu.

Some said I was ruthless.

They asked:

“How could you treat Yao’s old ministers this way?”

But I understood something they did not.

On the chessboard of power, there are no “old ministers” and “new ministers.”

There are only:

**your people**

and

**someone else’s people.**

Yao had left these men behind not merely to help me.

He had left them to watch me.

I could not allow them to watch me.

So I acted.

One by one.

I cleared the board.

### **Second: Promote New People**

There were two groups of capable men who had been neglected during Yao’s reign.

They were called the **Eight Gentlemen** and the **Eight Worthies**.

They were talented.

They were capable.

But Yao had not made full use of them because they belonged to other tribes and networks.

I did.

I brought in the Eight Gentlemen to administer education and social affairs.

I appointed the Eight Worthies to manage agriculture and land.

They trusted me.

They owed their opportunities to me.

They became the foundation of my administration.

**A ruler cannot govern an empire forever through his father-in-law’s people.**

You need people of your own.

### **Third: Test the Hearts of the People**

When Yao died, I did not immediately take the throne.

Instead, I made a decision that confused everyone.

I “yielded” the throne to Yao’s son, Danzhu.

Then I withdrew.

And waited.

I wanted to see what would happen.

What happened?

The feudal lords did not go to Danzhu.

They came to me.

The people did not cheer for Danzhu.

They cheered for me.

Those seeking justice did not go to Danzhu.

They came to me.

Then I knew.

I had won.

This was not usurpation.

It was the will of the people.

I had not seized the throne.

The realm had insisted on placing it in my hands.

Danzhu later rebelled.

I suppressed him.

How will history describe him?

It will call him unfilial.

It will call him vicious.

It will say he deserved to lose the realm.

**History, after all, is written by the victors.**

# **Diary VIII: Why I Am Writing This**

**Tonight · Epilogue**

The wind at Mount Li is the same as it was thirty years ago.

But I am no longer the homeless boy who was hunted through the wilderness.

I am Shun.

Yao’s son-in-law.

The husband of Ehuang and Nüying.

The ruler chosen by the realm.

Yet inside me still lives the child who once stood beneath a collapsing well.

He taught me vigilance.

He taught me patience.

He taught me how to dig an escape route through darkness.

Some people call me a phoenix man.

A man who rose through his wife’s family.

A man who climbed because of his father-in-law.

I admit it.

Without Yao’s daughters, I might never have reached where I am.

But I have one question for those people:

If I were merely an opportunist, why did Yao’s daughters become so completely devoted to me?

Why did his nine sons stand on my side?

Why did the feudal lords choose me even when doing so meant defying Yao’s apparent wishes?

I did not reach this position merely because my father-in-law promoted me.

I reached it because I had the ability to hold it.

I farmed at Mount Li.

Three years, and a settlement emerged.

I fished at Lake Lei.

The fishermen began yielding their best fishing grounds to one another.

I made pottery by the river.

The pottery became strong and reliable.

For twenty-eight years, I replaced the foundations of an empire, piece by piece, with people I could trust.

I appointed Yu to control the floods.

Not because I was generous enough to forgive his father, Gun.

But because Yu was genuinely capable.

Would another man have been able to do what I did?

I do not know.

I do not know how later generations will remember me.

Perhaps they will make me a sage king.

Perhaps a model son.

Perhaps the perfect symbol of abdication and virtuous rule.

They will say Yao gave me the realm because of my morality.

They will say the people followed me because of my benevolence.

But I know the truth.

**Power is never simply given.**

**Power must be earned, seized, consolidated, and defended.**

**Everyone who climbs from the bottom carries blood and mud on his clothes, and somewhere in his eyes there remains a blade and a flame.**

**A phoenix man is not forged in a day.**

He is forced into existence.

He is tempered by endurance.

He pays for his ascent with his life.

If there were another life, I would want only to be an ordinary man.

Parents who truly loved me.

Brothers who stood beside me.

A life without collapsing wells.

A life without burning granaries.

A life without waking every midnight to the memory of flames.

But there is no other life.

And if I must be Shun,

then let me finish the story of this phoenix man.

Outside, dawn is approaching.

I hear Ehuang rising.

I hear Nüying speaking softly.

I hear the roosters crowing from the settlements beneath Mount Li.

Another day has begun.

And I still have a road to walk.

**—End—**
