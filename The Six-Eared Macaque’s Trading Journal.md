# The Six-Eared Macaque’s Trading Journal

### On the Eve of the Final Battle at Spirit Mountain, I Put My Entire Position—My Very Existence—on the Tathāgata



> *Recorded on a drifting cumulus cloud southeast of the Western Heaven, somewhere between the sacred ground of Vīrāmbā and Spirit Mountain. Winter of the Renyin year. The moonlight unusually white.*

The night I put my entire position on the table, the moon over Flower-Fruit Mountain was just as white as it is tonight.

Before that cup of water struck Tang Sanzang across the back, I had **run the numbers seven times**.

The first time, I calculated the probability of winning.
The second, the odds.
The third, the possible exits.
The fourth, the probability that the Tathāgata would intervene.
The fifth, whether that intervention would mean suppression or enlightenment.
The sixth, the probability of enlightenment.

I never finished the seventh calculation.

Halfway through, I suddenly laughed.

I laughed at myself.

A creature who was supposed to **hear everything**, who was supposed to understand the hidden logic of all things, was sitting there calculating **how the Tathāgata would judge me**.

If the Tathāgata could be calculated, he would no longer be the Tathāgata.

And yet I still placed the bet.

**Fully invested. No leverage.**

I didn't need leverage.

This fur, this iron staff, these ears that had heard the voices of the Three Realms—those were my capital.

I put my entire self on the gambling table.

On the other side sat Spirit Mountain.

Others would call that madness.

I called it:

**The odds were thick enough to justify betting my very existence.**

---

## (I) Pre-Market: I Read the Prospectus Three Times

By “prospectus,” I mean Tang Sanzang's travel credential.

**The first time, I read the words.**

The Great Tang of Jambudvīpa. Tang Sanzang. Journeying west to seek the Buddhist scriptures.

I had heard all of this before.

But touching that yellow paper with my own fingers was different. When I turned the pages, I could feel traces of ink beneath my fingertips.

Page by page:

Guanyin's seal.
The Jade Emperor's seal.
Judge Cui's seal.

Each seal looked like a chip placed on the table.

I counted them.

Eleven immortals had already placed their bets on this pilgrimage.

**The second time, I read the three characters: Sun Wukong.**

I stared at them for a long while.

Those characters weren't merely written.

They had been **pressed into the paper**.

The ink had seeped into the fibers, leaving the edges ragged.

Suddenly I thought:

That isn't a name.

It's a wound.

Wukong had paid for that wound with five hundred years beneath a mountain, with the tightening of the golden fillet around his head, with exile after the three encounters with the White Bone Demon.

If he could carve such a wound into the world, why couldn't I carve one of my own?

**The third time, I read the blank space.**

The back of the document was empty.

There were no four characters saying **Six-Eared Macaque**.

I ran my finger across that blank space. The paper roughened beneath my touch, but no ink appeared.

I called toward the mouth of the cave:

“Someone come here.”

A false Zhu Bajie ran in.

“Bring me a brush.”

He froze.

I repeated myself.

My voice was calm.

The iron staff turned once in my hand.

He ran.

When the brush came, I put it back.

I never wrote those four characters.

Because I **knew what came before and what came after**.

I knew that once those words were written there, I would no longer be the Six-Eared Macaque.

I would merely be **another Sun Wukong**.

That wasn't what I wanted.

What I wanted was to go to Spirit Mountain myself, return with the scriptures myself, and have the people of Jambudvīpa recognize me as their patriarch, my name carried down through ten thousand generations.

That prospectus taught me one thing:

**There is only one seat in the pilgrimage-to-the-West market.**

But a seat like that is not something you wait for.

**It is something you fight for.**

---

## (II) Building the Position: My Logic of Exposure

I built my position in three tranches.

### **First tranche: Strike Tang Sanzang.**

To an outsider, this was the stupidest move.

Strike the master, and you declare war on the entire pilgrimage project.

But I had calculated it.

Before Tang Sanzang fainted, he shouted:

> “You insolent monkey!”

Not:

> “Demon!”

He called the wrong person.

His eyes could not recognize me.

His mouth did.

That word—*insolent*—was the product of years of practice.

It told me something important.

In Tang Sanzang's mind, **Sun Wukong** had already become synonymous with disobedience, defiance, and violence.

So when I struck him, I wasn't destroying Wukong's identity.

I was **reinforcing the label**.

I wanted Tang Sanzang to understand:

Wukong can strike you.

I can strike you too.

If the behavior is identical, why is one called “disciple” and the other “demon”?

---

### **Second tranche: Seize the travel credential.**

This was my core holding.

The credential was the only official proof of the pilgrimage.

Whoever possessed it was, at least on paper, the pilgrim.

I sat on the stone platform in Water-Curtain Cave and read it word by word.

By the third reading, I had already begun arranging a false Zhu Bajie, a false Sha Wujing, and a false Tang Sanzang.

I wasn't trying to fool anyone.

I was trying to **run the entire process from beginning to end**.

I wanted this false pilgrimage party to leave Flower-Fruit Mountain, travel a certain distance, arrive at the next station, and have the officials stamp the document.

Once the first stamp appeared, the distinction between real and false would begin to blur.

Once the process worked, the logic would become self-consistent.

---

### **Third tranche: Fight Wukong in front of every judge.**

This was my heavy position.

I calculated the probabilities.

Guanyin.

The Jade Emperor.

The King of Hell.

And Di Ting.

The probability that all four would be unable to distinguish between the two of us, taken together, exceeded ninety percent.

Every time someone said,

> “I cannot tell which one is real,”

it became another piece of external validation for my proposition:

**I am real too.**

I didn't need to defeat Wukong.

I knew I couldn't.

And I knew I couldn't kill him.

What I needed was for as many judges as possible to publicly acknowledge:

**The two cannot be distinguished.**

Every such acknowledgment gave my position another layer of safety margin.

When the three tranches were fully established, my position was **fully invested**.

---

## (III) Noise and Confirmation Bias During the Holding Period

I admit it.

I **listened selectively**.

Along the road, I heard many voices.

When Guanyin said,

> “I cannot distinguish you,”

I stored the sentence.

When she said,

> “You two, go on your way,”

I stored that too.

When the Jade Emperor frowned, I recorded the exact angle of his brow.

When the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King turned his magic mirror, I noticed the knuckles of his fingers turning white.

When Di Ting lowered himself to the ground, I recorded the tiny tremor at the tip of his tail.

That was fear.

Fear meant he knew something.

But he dared not say it.

And if he dared not say it, then surely that meant consent.

Or so I told myself.

But I didn't store other signals.

I didn't store the way Guanyin's hand relaxed when she lowered the Pure Bottle.

That wasn't:

> “I cannot distinguish you.”

It was:

**“I no longer want to distinguish you.”**

I didn't store the first word Tang Sanzang spoke after waking, his hand pressed against the back of his head.

He didn't say:

> “Wukong.”

Nor:

> “Demon.”

He said:

**“It hurts.”**

Just two words.

I replayed them many times afterward.

There was no hatred in that voice.

Only pain.

And I didn't store what Wukong said when we traveled side by side on our clouds outside Mount Luojia.

He said:

> “I don't know.”

When he said it, his voice was soft.

And inside that softness was something I did not want to acknowledge.

He didn't know why he was still chasing me.

He didn't know.

But he chased me anyway.

Which meant there were things that **fell outside the range of “knowing what comes before and what comes after.”**

I filtered all those noises out.

I called it:

**“Focusing on the core logic.”**

In reality, it was simply **confirmation bias**.

I collected evidence that supported my narrative.

Anything that contradicted it was dismissed as:

“emotional disturbance,”
“short-term volatility,”
“irrational selling.”

Every unfavorable signal I dismissed quietly strengthened the walls of my own cognitive prison.

But I didn't see it that way at the time.

I only thought:

Look.

Everything points in the same direction.

**I am right.**

---

## (IV) The Most Dangerous Moment: Di Ting's Look

At the Kṣitigarbha Hall, Di Ting lay flat on the ground and listened.

The instant his ear touched the earth, I heard my own heartbeat.

No.

I had no heart.

I was the Six-Eared Macaque.

I belonged to none of the ten categories of beings.

Neither heaven nor earth.

Neither god nor human.

Neither ghost nor demon.

Theoretically, I should have had no heartbeat at all.

And yet, at that moment, something was pounding inside my chest.

Its rhythm resonated with the golden fillet around Wukong's head.

I could hear it.

Di Ting finished listening.

He didn't raise his head.

First he looked at Kṣitigarbha.

Then he glanced sideways at me.

**In that single glance, my ability to know what came before and what came after flickered.**

I saw a branch of the future.

Di Ting would speak.

He would reveal my true identity.

Kṣitigarbha would act.

I would be subdued.

The story would end.

Then the branch disappeared.

Because Di Ting did not speak.

Instead, he said:

> “You two, go to the Thunderclap Monastery. Only the Buddha can tell you apart.”

At that moment, my position had actually revealed its **risk exposure**.

Di Ting could distinguish us.

That meant the chain of “indistinguishability” had already broken.

But he didn't say so.

Which meant that, in his judgment, the cost of exposing me publicly was greater than the cost of sending us directly to Spirit Mountain.

In other words:

My existence had now been internally classified as something requiring **the highest level of adjudication**.

That wasn't bad news.

It was a **restructuring expectation**.

The junior judges couldn't decide.

So the case was being sent directly to the highest review committee.

That's how I interpreted it.

And I held my position even tighter.

---

## (V) The Road to Spirit Mountain: My Take-Profit and Stop-Loss Lines

On the way to Spirit Mountain, I set two lines for myself.

### **Take-profit line**

The Tathāgata would stand before all the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas and say:

> “Six-Eared Macaque, you too may undertake the pilgrimage.”

The moment those words left his mouth, I would have won.

I would kneel.

I would give thanks.

I would carry the scriptures back east.

Jambudvīpa would establish me as patriarch.

My name would endure for ten thousand generations.

My valuation would no longer be **“Equal to Heaven.”**

It would be **“Equal to Buddha.”**

One level above Wukong.

Because there would still be a golden fillet around his head.

There would be none around mine.

---

### **Stop-loss line**

The Tathāgata would expose my true identity.

The golden alms bowl would descend.

That too would count as a kind of victory.

At least I would have been explicitly judged a “false one” by the Tathāgata himself.

That was better than remaining suspended forever as a ghostly question because Guanyin “could not distinguish” us.

A definite loss is better than an indefinite position with no settlement.

But I never established a third line.

What if the Tathāgata neither recognized me nor exposed me?

What if he simply...

**remained silent?**

My ability to know what came before and what came after could not calculate that possibility.

In all my branching simulations, there was no scenario called:

**“The Tathāgata remains silent.”**

I simply skipped that risk exposure.

I called it:

**“The core assumption is unfalsifiable.”**

In reality, I was simply afraid to imagine it.

---

## (VI) On the Act of Betting Itself

Everyone said I was gambling.

I disagreed.

Gambling meant guessing blindly.

I wasn't guessing.

I calculated everything.

The probability of victory.

The odds.

The judges' reactions.

The Tathāgata's style.

I could **hear the voices others could not hear**.

I could **see the logic others could not see**.

I could **see causal chains others could not see**.

My system was far more sophisticated than that demon-revealing mirror in Heaven.

But I had failed to calculate one thing:

**When the abacus clicks too loudly, the beads begin to resonate with one another.**

I treated every “cannot distinguish” as bullish.

But I never calculated what kind of **bubble** those bullish signals would create when accumulated together.

I drew every support line on the chart.

But I never drew the line showing what I would do if all of them broke at once.

I established take-profit and stop-loss levels.

But I never created a response to **Tathāgata silence**.

Because my core assumption was:

**The Tathāgata must make a judgment.**

Once that assumption failed, the entire system collapsed.

What do you call that?

**Model risk.**

The more sophisticated your model becomes, the more absolute your trust in it becomes.

And reality has a habit of entering through exactly the crack your model failed to cover.

---

## (VII) The Gate Was Near. I Rebalanced One Last Time.

I could see the golden light.

The tiles of Thunderclap Monastery had been warmed by a thousand years of sunlight.

The light was old.

But the brightness was new.

From the top of my cloud, I reviewed my entire position one final time.

**The strike against Tang Sanzang:**

Behavioral equivalence implies identity equivalence.

That support still held.

**The seizure of the travel credential:**

Procedural legitimacy implies legitimate participation in the pilgrimage.

That support still held.

**The fight with Wukong before every judge:**

Indistinguishability implies equivalence with the real thing.

That line...

**was weakening.**

Because Di Ting's glance had proved that someone *could* distinguish us.

He simply didn't dare say so.

Three support lines.

The last one had begun to crack.

I looked toward the gates of Thunderclap Monastery.

And suddenly I wondered:

What if I turned back now?

What if I returned to Flower-Fruit Mountain?

Dismissed the false pilgrimage party?

Returned the credential?

And from that day forward simply lived as a wild monkey belonging to none of the ten categories—

neither heaven nor earth,

neither god nor human,

neither ghost nor demon—

existing forever inside that strange emptiness?

Would that be enough?

I thought about it for one moment.

That moment lasted longer than five hundred years.

Then I said:

**No.**

Not because I still had a winning probability.

But because if I turned back, then all those days of judgment, analysis, calculation, and every moment when I believed I had “gotten it right” would become nothing more than **a misunderstanding**.

I could accept losing.

But I could not accept **being cancelled**.

To be cancelled meant this:

The Six-Eared Macaque had never truly operated anything.

He had merely been playing the role of something that could.

A shadow pretending to be an operator.

Wukong gathered his cloud and looked back at me.

I looked at him.

Suddenly I remembered those five hundred years beneath the Five Elements Mountain.

He had been beneath the mountain.

I had been above the clouds.

He had waited for a pilgrim to come and rescue him.

I had waited for evidence that **I, too, was worth rescuing**.

Five hundred years.

We had been waiting for the same thing.

“Let's go,” he said.

“Let's go,” I answered.

I moved the Ruyi Iron Staff into my left hand and straightened my tiger-skin skirt with my right.

I knew what came before and what came after.

I could see what would happen over the next half-hour:

The Tathāgata would speak those fourteen words.

Di Ting would bow to the ground.

The golden bowl would fly.

I would transform into a bee.

And then—

**then I could no longer see what came next.**

For the first time in five hundred years,

my ability to know what came before and what came after had failed.

And it was good that it failed.

Because only when it failed did my model finally encounter reality.

Until that moment, I had never actually been betting on:

**“How will the Tathāgata judge me?”**

I had been betting on:

**“Can I correctly calculate how the Tathāgata will judge me?”**

Those are two completely different games.

The first bets on the Buddha.

The second bets on whether I have moved the beads of my own abacus loudly enough.

Only now did I understand.

All those days before Spirit Mountain, I had been betting against my own model.

Every position I added was a purchase of the proposition:

**“My model is correct.”**

But at the foot of Spirit Mountain, the model cracked.

And in the instant it cracked, I finally saw what the real gambling table looked like.

There were no odds.

No judges.

No support lines.

There was only one question:

**Dare you walk in without knowing what comes next?**

I took one step forward.

**Winter of Renyin.
The Six-Eared Macaque, three steps outside the gates of Spirit Mountain.
I closed every position built on logic.
I entered with nothing but myself.**

*(As for what happened next—that is not written in this journal. When I wrote these pages, the golden alms bowl had not yet descended. I know only one thing: the moment I stepped through the gates, the abacus inside my head suddenly fell silent.*

*And an analysis made before the market opens is only truly a pre-market analysis once the abacus stops making noise.)*
