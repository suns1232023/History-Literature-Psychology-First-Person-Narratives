# When the Sacrificial Meat Never Came: The Night Confucius Left Lu

### Spring, 496 BCE — Three Days After the Suburban Sacrifice — Night — Confucius's Residence, Qufu



I sat alone in the empty main hall.

It had been three days.

The flame of the oil lamp on my desk flickered back and forth, like the fire in my own heart these past three days—

**a fire that refused to die, yet could never burn brightly.**

During the day, **Zilu** burst through the door, his face flushed with barely contained anger.

> “Master, **the sacrificial meat has already been distributed by the Ji family retainers!** Duke Huan accepted the music and dancing girls from Qi and has neglected court for three days. Even the suburban sacrifice was nothing more than a formality!”

As he spoke, I was holding a bamboo slip.

My fingers tightened around it until the knuckles turned pale.

When he finished, I merely nodded.

“I know.”

Zilu stood there, staring at me for a long moment.

In the end, he said nothing more.

He bowed and walked out.

I looked back at the bamboo slip.

I could no longer read a single character.

The densely packed seal script suddenly seemed distant and alien, as though it belonged to another world.

**Meng Yizi** once asked me:

> “What is filial piety?”

I answered:

> **“Do not violate the proper order.”**

Three words.

How heavy they seem now.

I have spent my entire life teaching people to **observe ritual, follow ritual, restore ritual**.

And in the end, I have become a witness to the collapse of ritual institutions—

No.

**A victim of it.**

The *fàn* meat—the sacrificial meat distributed by the ruler to his senior ministers after the suburban sacrifice—

It was never merely meat.

It was **a symbol of ritual order, a tangible expression of the relationship between ruler and subject.**

When Duke Ding of Lu and Ji Huanzi performed the suburban sacrifice and failed to send the sacrificial meat to the ministers, they were not merely slighting me, Kong Qiu.

They were announcing to the realm:

**Lu no longer takes ritual seriously.**

Mencius would later put it plainly:

> **“Those who do not understand think it was about the meat. Those who understand know it was about the failure of ritual.”**

Among all my disciples, perhaps only **Yan Hui** would understand that I had never been waiting for a few pounds of cooked meat.

I was waiting for the smallest remaining sign that a state still respected **ritual order**.

And even knowing this, I waited three days.

**Three days. Seventy-two hours.**

Every passing moment felt like a tiny knife slowly cutting through the string I called **hope**.

---

### The First Day

I said to Zilu:

> **“Lu is performing the suburban sacrifice. If the sacrificial meat is delivered to the ministers, perhaps I can still remain.”**

Zilu looked at me.

He did not argue.

But I knew what he was thinking.

*Master. My Master.*

*The people of Qi have sent eighty dancing girls and a hundred and twenty magnificent horses. Ji Huanzi has gone in disguise to watch them again and again. The ruler of Lu spends his days in amusement and neglects affairs of state.*

*What are you still waiting for?*

---

### The Second Day

Nothing came.

---

### The Third Day

Zilu came to tell me:

The Ji family's retainers had already distributed the meat.

I had been waiting for a miracle.

A miracle proving that **ritual had not yet completely died.**

I am fifty-five years old.

The age when, as I once said, one begins to understand Heaven's decree.

I know the weight of destiny.

I know the limits of human effort.

And yet I stubbornly believed that as long as Lu still distributed the sacrificial meat after the suburban rites, it meant that **the seed of ritual was still alive—and that my Way still had soil in which to take root.**

But in that moment, everything I had done over more than fifty years suddenly felt like—

a dream.

From the day I first established a school at Que Li,

to my appointment as magistrate of Zhongdu,

to my elevation to Minister of Works,

to the time I was entrusted with the affairs of state—

it all felt like a dream I had taken very seriously.

A dream into which I had poured everything.

Only to wake and discover that **everyone had already left the theater.**

I remembered myself at thirty-five, when I first left Lu for Qi.

That departure had been full of hope.

Duke Jing of Qi had asked me about government.

I told him:

> **“Let the ruler be a ruler, the minister a minister, the father a father, and the son a son.”**

He replied:

> **“Excellent! If the ruler is not a ruler, the minister not a minister, the father not a father, and the son not a son, then even if there is grain, how could I eat it?”**

I thought I had found someone who understood.

But later, Yan Ying opposed me.

The ministers of Qi wanted me dead.

I had no choice but to leave.

That departure was **“leaving while the rice was still being washed”**—*jie xi er xing*.

I had not even finished preparing the rice before I had to flee.

But this time—

before I had even left Lu,

**my heart was already breaking.**

**Lu was my ancestral homeland.**

The land of the Zhou Duke.

The land of ritual and music.

Everything I had learned,

everything I had pursued,

everything I had hoped for—

was tied to this place.

I once believed I could realize my Way here.

**Rectify names. Restore ritual. Cultivate virtue. Bring peace to the people.**

At the **Meeting at Jiagu**, I had secured the return of the lands of Wenyang, Yun, and Guoyin for Lu.

Through the campaign to **destroy the three fortified cities**, I had tried to weaken the Three Huan families and restore power to the Duke.

I thought I was slowly pulling Lu back onto the right path.

But I had forgotten something.

**The harder you pull on a rope, the more desperately the person on the other end wants to let go.**

Qi sent **eighty beautiful women and one hundred and twenty splendid horses**.

And the ruler and ministers of Lu truly became intoxicated by them.

The benevolence and righteousness I preached could not compete with seductive music.

The self-restraint and restoration of ritual I advocated could not compete with silk robes and imperial dances.

It reminded me of a poem.

When **Shi Ji** comes to see me off, I may sing it to him:

> **“A woman's words may drive a man from his home;
> her influence may bring ruin and death.
> How pleasant life becomes when one simply drifts along,
> content to let the years pass.”**

*How pleasant. How leisurely.*

Easy to say.

But how could my heart possibly be at ease?

---

Today, **Zigong** came to see me.

He asked a question very carefully, as though afraid of stepping on something fragile.

> “Master, the sacrificial meat never came. If people ask why you left, what should I tell them?”

I looked at this most intelligent of my disciples.

I remained silent for a long time.

Then I said slowly:

> **“Tell them that Confucius left because he did not receive the sacrificial meat.”**

Zigong's eyes widened.

> “Master—”

“I know what you are going to say.”

I raised my hand.

> **“But this is the best explanation.”**

Mencius would later explain my reasoning:

> **“Confucius wished to leave on account of a minor fault; he did not wish to leave merely for the sake of leaving.”**

If I publicly accused the ruler of Lu of violating ritual, or Ji Huanzi of abusing political power, I would be exposing the failures of the ruler and his ministers before the entire world.

I could leave.

But **Lu's dignity must not be shattered.**

Let the world believe that I left because I was offended over a piece of sacrificial meat.

It was a small enough “crime” to permit me to leave with dignity.

I would not expose the failures of the ruler and his ministers.

**That was loyalty.**

Zigong was silent after I finished.

After a long while, he said quietly:

> “Master, I understand.”

Then he paused.

> “But…”

“But what?”

> “But the world will never understand how much this hurts you.”

I did not answer.

Because I did not know how to answer.

Does it hurt?

Of course.

But not because I failed to receive a piece of meat.

**I am not so shallow.**

What hurts is that everything I have spent more than fifty years building has been quietly pushed to the ground in a single moment.

**Can ritual truly be restored?**

**Can the Way truly be practiced?**

But I cannot allow myself to drown in that pain.

Because I know this:

If I remain here, watching Lu descend day by day into the collapse of ritual while I can do nothing—

**that would be the greater suffering.**

If I stay, I will either become an appendage of the Ji family,

or I will watch silently as my Way is slowly worn away.

**Either fate is more terrifying than leaving.**

> **“When there is harmony, remain. When there is no harmony, depart.”**

I have said those words many times.

To my students.

To my friends.

To scholars from other states who came seeking advice.

Today, it is time to say them to myself.

Zilu asked where I would go.

I answered:

> **“Wei.”**

Wei is a state bound to Lu by kinship.

Its ruler, Duke Ling, is hardly a model sovereign.

But Wei still has worthy ministers such as **Qu Boyu**, and upright men such as **Shi Yu and Gongsun Fa**.

At least in Wei, scholars are still respected.

At least someone may still be willing to listen when I speak of **ritual**.

And besides, Zigong is from Wei.

Zilu is also strongly in favor of going there.

**Where one's disciples are, one is not entirely a rootless wanderer.**

Yet I know—

better than anyone—

that there may be no ruler anywhere in this world willing truly to practice my Way.

When Duke Ling of Wei asks me about government, perhaps he will care only about the size of the payroll.

Other rulers may want nothing more than to decorate their courts with my reputation.

My Way is too impractical.

Too idealistic.

Too out of step with the times.

This is an age in which states compete through force.

Who cares about **benevolence**?

Who cares about **ritual**?

**And yet I will go.**

Not for office.

Not for wealth.

I am fifty-five.

I am long past the age of chasing either.

I go because if I do not even try, then I will have betrayed the mission Heaven placed upon me.

I go because:

> **“If the world were already governed according to the Way, I would have no need to change it.”**

Precisely because the world has lost its Way,

**I must try.**

Even if I ultimately become nothing more than a **dog abandoned by its master**, so be it.

Tomorrow I leave.

---

This afternoon, I went alone to the site of the suburban sacrifice.

There was no one there.

Only the ashes of the incense remained, scattered by the wind.

I stood before the empty altar.

And suddenly I remembered a line from the *Book of Songs*:

> **“Wind and rain darken the sky, yet the rooster does not cease to crow.”**

No matter how violent the storm,

the rooster still crows.

No matter how chaotic the world,

**the Way must still be practiced.**

That is my fate.

From the age of thirty, when I began teaching,

to fifty, when I entered public service,

to today, at fifty-five, when I leave Lu—

every step has been part of the same undertaking:

**to carve the character “ritual” into the world.**

If the world refuses it, I will keep carving.

If it cannot be carved here,

I will find another place.

Some people say I am stubborn.

Some say I do not understand the times.

But they do not understand one thing:

**If even I stop being stubborn, who will remember how to write the word “ritual”?**

I returned to the main hall.

I picked up my brush and thought about leaving a few words for my disciples.

But the brush stopped in midair.

A drop of ink fell onto the bamboo slip, spreading slowly into a black circle.

I wanted to write:

> **“If the Way cannot be practiced, I shall build a raft and drift upon the sea.”**

But I put the brush down.

Drift upon the sea?

No.

I will still walk upon this land.

I will walk until I can walk no more.

Until no one is willing to listen.

Until my Way has been completely buried by the age in which I live.

Because this is the destiny of Kong Qiu.

**To know that something cannot be done—and still do it.**

---

The night has grown deep.

The oil lamp is nearly extinguished.

Outside, the watch drums sound again, dull and distant, like a farewell arriving from another world.

Tomorrow, the sun will rise as it always does.

And I will step onto a road whose end I cannot see.

**The road is called: “Traveling among the States.”**

Fourteen years from now, I will return to Lu.

I will be sixty-eight.

My hair will be white.

My body will be gaunt.

My face will bear the marks of a lifetime of wandering.

But my heart will be no different from the one writing these words tonight.

Because the Way has never been a road that can be completed.

**It is a road that can only be walked.**

---

### The Following Morning — Outside the Gates of Qufu

My disciples had already packed their belongings.

They were waiting outside.

I took one last look at the city in which I had lived for fifty-five years.

The old house at Que Li.

The teaching platform at the Apricot Altar.

And the suburban altar where I would **wait forever for sacrificial meat that would never come.**

Zilu called from ahead:

> **“Master, let us go.”**

I nodded.

And took my first step.

But my feet were heavy.

Every step seemed to fall upon a memory—

upon my mother's teachings,

upon the courtyard where I had learned ritual as a boy,

upon the glory of the Jiagu alliance,

and upon the humiliation of the sacrificial meat that never came.

> **“Slowly I walked, reluctant to leave the land of my parents.”**

I walked slowly.

Not because I was uncertain.

**Because I could not bear to leave.**

I could not bear to leave this land.

These people.

The dream I had once dreamed here.

But no matter how painful it was—

**I had to go.**

Because there are roads that must be walked.

Otherwise, you will never know how far you could have gone.

I turned and looked once more at the gates of Qufu.

In the faint morning light, the city gate looked like a silent mouth—

as though it wanted to speak,

but could not.

I turned away.

This time, I did not look back.

The wind came from the west, stirring the hem of my robe.

My disciples followed behind me.

No one spoke.

We simply walked.

Quietly.

Toward an unknown future—

long,

difficult,

filled with wandering and uncertainty.

And inside my heart, one sentence kept repeating:

> **“If I hear the Way in the morning, I can die content in the evening.”**

Where is the Way?

I do not know.

But I believe this:

**As long as I keep walking, I will come a little closer.**
