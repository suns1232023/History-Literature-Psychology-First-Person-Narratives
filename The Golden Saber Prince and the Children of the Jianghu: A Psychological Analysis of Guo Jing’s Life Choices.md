# The Golden Saber Prince and the Children of the Jianghu: A Psychological Analysis of Guo Jing’s Life Choices

**February 17, 2026**

**Keywords:** Cultural Media; *The Legend of the Condor Heroes*; Identity; Moral Development; Decision-Making; Existential Psychology

## I. Introduction: The Double Bind of Choice

Among the most psychologically complex episodes in Jin Yong’s *The Legend of the Condor Heroes* is Guo Jing’s choice to relinquish his status and privileges as the Mongolian Golden Saber Prince and instead choose Huang Rong, the woman he genuinely loves, as his life partner.

This decision is not merely a matter of romantic preference. It involves multiple psychological dimensions, including **identity formation, moral development, cultural belonging, existential responsibility, and decision-making under conflict**.

The choice may be understood as a conflict between two competing life narratives. One path offers Guo Jing status, political power, social security, and the fulfillment of obligations toward Genghis Khan and the Mongolian world in which he was raised. The other offers emotional authenticity, cultural identification, moral integrity, and a life consistent with his emerging sense of personal meaning.

This essay examines Guo Jing’s decision through the perspectives of developmental psychology, existential psychology, and cognitive decision theory. It also draws upon the tragic narrative structure of Xu Xingye’s *The Golden Coffer in Ruins* (*Jin'ou Que*) and the historical figure Ma Kuo to explore the psychological significance of individual choice in periods of historical upheaval.

---

# II. Guo Jing’s Psychological Development

## 2.1 Early Attachment and Identity Formation

Guo Jing’s childhood experiences contributed to a distinctive psychological structure.

### His mother, Li Ping

As a Han Chinese mother living on the Mongolian steppe, Li Ping continued to transmit Han cultural values to her son. Through her, Guo Jing developed a strong sense of **Han cultural identity**, which later became an important component of his moral framework.

### The Mongolian environment

Growing up among the Mongols, Guo Jing was also immersed in a culture that emphasized courage, martial strength, loyalty, and practical action. This environment cultivated both a **pragmatic behavioral orientation** and a strong respect for authority and personal commitments.

### The Seven Masters

The Seven Freaks of Jiangnan reinforced Guo Jing’s Han cultural education and, above all, introduced him to the moral ideal of **xia**—the chivalric commitment to justice and the protection of others.

At the same time, their relatively straightforward moral framework contributed to a tendency toward relatively **categorical moral judgments**.

### Psychological interpretation

Guo Jing therefore developed a form of **dual cultural identity**. He was culturally connected to the Han Chinese world while simultaneously being deeply embedded in Mongolian society.

From the perspective of Erikson’s psychosocial theory, adolescence involves the central developmental crisis of **identity versus role confusion** (Erikson, 1968). Guo Jing’s development can be interpreted as an unusually complex attempt to integrate two cultural identities into a coherent sense of self.

His later decision is therefore not simply a choice between two women.

It is, at a deeper level, a choice between two possible selves.

---

## 2.2 The Unusual Development of Moral Judgment

Guo Jing’s moral development also follows a distinctive trajectory.

At an early stage, aspects of his behavior resemble what Kohlberg would describe as the **preconventional level**, characterized by sensitivity to punishment and obedience to authority.

Within Mongolian society, he gradually develops a conventional commitment to **loyalty, promises, and social obligations**.

Within the Han Chinese and jianghu moral framework, however, he increasingly embraces ideals of **justice, righteousness, and protection of the vulnerable**.

After meeting Huang Rong, these different moral commitments begin to converge into a more universal ethical orientation—eventually expressed in the famous ideal of becoming a person who acts **“for the country and for the people.”**

Importantly, this development is not entirely linear.

Guo Jing’s moral reasoning evolves through different cultural contexts, sometimes producing tension rather than simple progression. His eventual decision therefore represents not merely a higher stage of moral reasoning but an attempt to **integrate competing moral obligations into a coherent ethical identity**.

---

# III. The Psychological Dynamics of the Choice

## 3.1 What Choosing the Golden Saber Princess Would Mean

Choosing Hua Zheng would entail several psychological benefits:

* **Identity confirmation:** acceptance of his Mongolian social identity.
* **Reciprocity and gratitude:** repayment of Genghis Khan’s protection and upbringing, as well as his bond with Tolui.
* **Political power:** entry into the center of the Mongol political order.
* **Social security:** a stable position for himself and potentially for his mother, Li Ping.

Yet the psychological costs would be considerable:

* **Self-betrayal:** conflict with the Han identity transmitted by his mother.
* **Moral conflict:** participation in Mongol military campaigns against the Song.
* **Emotional alienation:** marriage without the depth of emotional attachment he experiences with Huang Rong.

Thus, the Golden Saber Prince represents **security, obligation, and power**, but potentially at the cost of authenticity.

---

## 3.2 What Choosing Huang Rong Would Mean

Choosing Huang Rong represents a different psychological configuration:

* **Emotional authenticity:** a relationship based on genuine attachment rather than political obligation.
* **Cultural identification:** continued commitment to the Han cultural world and the moral values of the jianghu.
* **Moral integrity:** avoidance of direct participation in the Mongol conquest of the Song.
* **Self-actualization:** living according to his own deeply held values.

But this choice also carries substantial costs:

* **Violation of obligations:** disappointing Genghis Khan and Tolui.
* **Loss of political power:** relinquishing the opportunity to become part of the Mongol ruling elite.
* **Insecurity:** choosing an uncertain life in the jianghu rather than a predictable political future.

The choice is therefore not between an obviously good option and an obviously bad one.

It is a genuine **value conflict**.

---

## 3.3 The Cognitive Mechanisms of Decision-Making

From the perspective of Kahneman and Tversky’s prospect theory, Guo Jing’s decision can be interpreted through several mechanisms.

### Loss aversion

The psychological cost of betraying those who had raised and trusted him may weigh more heavily than the potential gains of political power.

### Framing effects

The decision is psychologically framed not as a simple question of marriage, but as a conflict between **moral integrity and political interest**.

### Affect heuristic

His emotional attachment to Huang Rong provides a powerful affective signal that influences the evaluation of otherwise competing alternatives.

### Cognitive dissonance

After choosing Huang Rong, Guo Jing must reconcile his decision with his obligations toward Hua Zheng, Genghis Khan, and Tolui.

One way to reduce this dissonance is to place greater psychological weight on the moral consequences of Mongol expansion and on the higher-order principle of acting “for the country and for the people.”

The decision therefore becomes psychologically sustainable because it is incorporated into a broader moral narrative.

# IV. The Tragic Pattern of *The Golden Coffer in Ruins*

## 4.1 The Psychological Structure of Ma Kuo’s Tragedy

Xu Xingye’s *The Golden Coffer in Ruins* presents the tragic figure Ma Kuo as a man caught between competing obligations.

His tragedy lies in:

* the rupture between idealism and historical reality;
* the paradox of loyalty;
* the helplessness of individuals caught within historical forces;
* and the impossibility of making a choice without sacrifice.

Ma Kuo represents a fundamental tragic dilemma:

**sometimes every available choice carries an irreversible loss.**

---

## 4.2 The Tragic Counterfactual in Guo Jing’s Dream

In Guo Jing’s imagined alternative life, choosing Hua Zheng produces a trajectory resembling Ma Kuo’s tragedy.

### Identity alienation

He becomes a Mongolian military commander and gradually experiences a division between his political identity and his cultural identity.

### Historical guilt

He participates in the conquest of the Song and becomes, from the perspective of the conquered, a historical enemy.

### Emotional emptiness

Political authority cannot compensate for the emotional loss created by separation from Huang Rong.

### Fatalism

Despite his personal efforts, he becomes increasingly trapped within a historical process much larger than himself.

Psychologically, the dream functions as a **counterfactual simulation**. By experiencing the consequences of an alternative decision within the dream, Guo Jing symbolically rehearses the costs of the path he did not take.

At the same time, this imagined future introduces a form of **survivor guilt and historical guilt** into his psychological narrative.

---

# V. The Psychological Meaning of Guo Jing’s Choice

## 5.1 Completion of Self-Identity

By choosing Huang Rong, Guo Jing accomplishes several forms of psychological integration:

**Cultural identity:** he clarifies his sense of belonging to the Han cultural world.

**Moral identity:** he transforms personal chivalry into the broader ethical principle of responsibility toward the nation and its people.

**Emotional authenticity:** he chooses a relationship grounded in genuine attachment rather than political calculation.

His decision therefore represents the consolidation of a coherent self.

---

## 5.2 The Existential Meaning of Choice

From the perspective of existential psychology, Guo Jing’s decision illustrates several fundamental concepts.

### Authenticity

He chooses to live according to his deeply held values rather than according to external expectations.

### Responsibility

He accepts that every meaningful choice carries consequences and that freedom cannot be separated from responsibility.

### Meaning-making

In the midst of historical chaos, he creates meaning through the commitments he chooses to uphold.

### Freedom

Even within severe historical constraints, he exercises personal agency by choosing the life for which he is willing to accept responsibility.

---

## 5.3 Psychological Adaptation in a Historical Context

Against the backdrop of the Song–Yuan transition, Guo Jing’s decision can also be interpreted as a psychological strategy for preserving internal coherence.

It allows him to:

* avoid direct participation in the conquest of the Song;
* reduce moral and cognitive conflict;
* preserve a stable ethical identity;
* and establish a meaningful value anchor amid historical instability.

His decision is therefore simultaneously **personal, moral, cultural, and existential**.

---

# VI. Conclusion: What Guo Jing’s Choice Teaches Us

Guo Jing’s decision illustrates several broader psychological principles.

### 1. Identity can take precedence over instrumental gain

When cultural identity conflicts with material benefits, individuals may prioritize psychological integrity over external rewards.

### 2. Moral development shapes the framing of decisions

The level and structure of moral development influence how a person defines the alternatives available to them.

### 3. Emotional attachment can function as a decision heuristic

A powerful emotional bond can become more influential than a purely instrumental calculation of costs and benefits.

### 4. Counterfactual simulation can reduce regret

Imagining the consequences of an alternative life can help individuals psychologically process the road not taken and strengthen commitment to their actual choice.

Ultimately, Guo Jing’s decision shapes not only his personal fate but also reflects a broader psychological response to individual agency during periods of historical transformation.

The deepest question is not simply:

**“Which choice is objectively correct?”**

It is:

**“Which consequences am I willing to accept as the price of becoming the person I choose to be?”**

# A Strange Dream on the Lantern Festival: Guo Jing’s Psychological Diary

**15th year of Jiading, Lantern Festival — Outside Xiangyang**

## The Fifteenth Night of the First Month

Tonight is the Lantern Festival.

The lanterns in Xiangyang should already be glowing.

Rong'er said this afternoon that she wanted to hang a hundred lotus lanterns on the city wall. She said it was a custom from Lin'an—women by West Lake would release lanterns during the festival and make wishes for the safety of their families.

I should have been there beside her, watching the lanterns.

But now I am thirty li outside the city.

There is a letter in my robe.

It arrived three days ago.

Sheepskin paper. Golden saber seal.

A Mongolian courier rode day and night from the banks of the Onon River to the gates of Xiangyang.

It was from Hua Zheng.

Twenty years have passed, yet I recognized her handwriting at once.

Every character was written carefully—almost awkwardly—just as she used to write Chinese characters when we were children, drawing every horizontal and vertical stroke thick and deliberate.

The letter was short.

> **“Guo Jing,**
>
> **The ice on the Onon River is especially thick this year. Father has grown old. He needs someone to help him walk. He says he wants to see you once more.**
>
> **Hua Zheng.”**

She did not mention the Golden Saber Prince.

She did not mention the marriage promise.

She did not even ask, “When will you return?”

She merely told me that the ice had formed, and that her father had grown old.

Yet I know the weight of this letter.

It lies against my chest as though the entire winter of the Onon River were pressing down upon my heart.

The wind comes from the north, carrying the smell of the great steppe.

I close my eyes, and I can see her.

Hua Zheng at sixteen, standing outside the golden tent, watching me mount my horse and ride south.

She did not cry.

Mongolian women are not taught to cry easily.

But I remember her eyes.

Those eyes were heavier than tears.

They were waiting.

Waiting for someone who might never return.

And that someone was me.

“Jing-gege, if one day you had to choose between Hua Zheng and me, whom would you choose?”

Rong'er asked me that many years ago.

We were on Peach Blossom Island. The sea wind smelled of salt, lifting her long hair.

I did not answer.

Not because I did not know.

But because I was afraid to say it.

Because the answer would hurt two people.

It would hurt the Mongolian princess who had waited for me for twenty years.

It would hurt the brilliant yet vulnerable woman who had asked me the question.

And it would hurt me.

Tonight the Lantern Festival moon is perfectly round.

It is as round as the moon over the steppe during the White Moon Festival.

As round as the silver bracelet on Hua Zheng's wrist when I first saw her dance.

As round as a promise that can never be made whole.

I lean against the wall of an abandoned hunter's hut.

The wall is made of mud brick. A hole has opened in it, and the wind whistles through.

It sounds like crying.

Like many people crying.

Like—

the entire Southern Song crying.

---

# The Dream Begins

I do not know when I fell asleep.

Then I heard horses.

Not one.

Thousands.

The earth trembled beneath them.

I opened my eyes.

The Golden Tent.

The golden roof of the Twelve Ordos blazed beneath the sunlight, almost painful to look at.

Genghis Khan sat upon a tiger-skin seat.

Tolui was on his left.

Jochi, Chagatai, and Ögedei on his right.

Princes, nobles, commanders, and khans knelt in dark rows across the tent.

Hua Zheng stood beside me.

She was wearing a red bridal robe.

The red was like blood.

Like the blood flowing through the cities of the Central Plain beneath Mongol cavalry.

“Guo Jing.”

Temüjin's voice sounded like rocks rolling down Burkhan Khaldun.

“From this day onward, you are the Golden Saber Prince. You are granted command of fifty thousand men.”

He placed the golden saber in my hands.

It was heavy.

So heavy that I could barely hold it.

I turned.

Hua Zheng was looking at me.

Her eyes were still the eyes of twenty years ago.

Waiting.

She was waiting for me to say:

“I accept.”

But my throat felt blocked.

I thought of Jiangnan.

I thought of Peach Blossom Island.

I thought of a woman who once told me:

“Jing-gege, if you choose Hua Zheng, I will return to Peach Blossom Island and never see you again.”

It had been said in anger.

But I knew she meant it.

“Guo Jing.”

Temüjin called my name again.

Two thousand eyes were watching me.

Tolui was watching too.

There was expectation in his eyes.

Brotherly affection.

And something else.

Pity.

He pitied me.

Because he knew what this choice meant.

I knelt.

My knees touched the cold golden bricks.

“Thank you, Great Khan.”

Three words.

When they left my mouth, I heard something inside me break.

Completely.

Forever.

---

# Year One — Xi Xia

Seven days after the wedding, the Great Khan ordered me to march against Xi Xia.

When the army departed, Hua Zheng did not come out of the tent to see me off.

Tolui told me she had knelt beneath the Eternal Blue Sky for an entire night.

What was she praying for?

That I would return safely?

Or that I would finally become her husband?

I do not know.

When Heishui City fell, I stood upon its walls and watched the envoy of Xi Xia crawl before us.

The sunset was red as blood.

Suddenly I remembered something from years ago.

In Zhangjiakou, a little beggar once asked me:

“Jing-gege, if you become a great general one day, will you also conquer cities and make all these people kneel before you?”

I said:

“I won't.”

She replied:

“You will. Once a person occupies a position, he must fulfill its responsibilities.”

At the time, I did not understand.

Tonight, I do.

---

# Year Three — Jin

The Great Khan attacked Jin.

I led the right wing, broke through Juyong Pass, and advanced toward Zhongdu.

The Jin emperor offered the Princess of Qi in exchange for peace.

An envoy knelt before my tent.

“Prince Consort, Great Jin is willing to surrender three prefectures in Hebei…”

I waved him away.

Late that night, I walked alone outside the camp.

The northern stars looked exactly as they had twenty years ago.

But something had changed.

I was no longer the boy who hunted eagles on the steppe.

I was the Mongolian Golden Saber Prince.

A general who had broken the cities of Jin.

I was—

the enemy of the Han Chinese.

---

# Year Five — The Return

The army returned to the Onon River.

Hua Zheng stood outside the Golden Tent holding our son.

The child was two years old.

He had never seen his father.

I dismounted and walked toward them.

Hua Zheng handed me the child.

“Call him An,” she said.

“An?”

“Peace. Safety. I only hope he will live a peaceful life. I do not want him to become like his father, forever at war.”

Her voice was calm.

But I heard what lay beneath it.

Resentment?

No.

Something worse.

Resignation.

She had accepted her fate.

The fate of being married to a man whose heart belonged elsewhere.

The fate of waiting for a man who was always leaving.

---

# Year Ten — The Western Campaign

The Great Khan marched west against Khwarazm.

I went with him.

Three years.

When Samarkand fell, I stood upon its walls and watched Mongol soldiers burn, kill, and plunder.

An old woman emerged from the flames carrying the body of a child.

She looked at me.

She said something in a language I could not understand.

Then she threw herself against the wall and died.

That night I dreamed of Rong'er.

She stood on the shore of Peach Blossom Island, her back toward me.

The sea wind lifted her white robes.

She said:

“Jing-gege, how many people have you killed?”

“I don't know.”

“I counted.”

“How many?”

“Seventy-six thousand four hundred and thirty-one.”

I woke.

Outside the tent, the moon over Central Asia was cold as a blade.

---

# Year Fifteen — The Southern Campaign

The Great Khan was old.

Before his death, he summoned me.

“Guo Jing.”

His hand was thin as a branch, but still powerful.

“In my entire life, the thing I am most proud of is not how much land I conquered.”

He paused.

“It is that I gained you—a Han Chinese son.”

I knelt silently.

“But I know your heart has never belonged to Mongolia.”

“Your heart is in Jiangnan.”

“With that woman named Huang Rong.”

I raised my head.

The Great Khan's eyes were cloudy, yet still piercing.

“I give you one final choice.”

“Remain in Mongolia. Serve Ögedei. When the Mongol armies move south to destroy the Song, you may become ruler of the Central Plain.”

“Or leave now. Return to Jiangnan.”

“But from this day onward, your bond with Mongolia will be broken.”

I remained silent for a long time.

So long that the butter lamp in the tent flickered three times.

“I…”

I lowered my head.

“I choose to stay.”

Why?

I do not know.

Perhaps after fifteen years, I had become accustomed to being the Golden Saber Prince.

Perhaps I was afraid of returning to Jiangnan and facing Rong'er's eyes.

Perhaps—

I was afraid.

Afraid of choosing.

Afraid of freedom.

---

# Year Twenty — The Fall of the Song

Ögedei Khan ordered me to lead the southern campaign.

Two hundred thousand Mongol cavalry crossed the Yellow River, captured Bianjing, and advanced toward the Yangtze.

Xiangyang.

I stood before the central command tent and looked at the familiar city.

I had once defended this city beside Rong'er.

I had once sworn here to live and fight “for the country and for the people.”

Tonight, I was going to destroy it.

“Report!”

A scout came galloping in.

“Prince Consort—the commander of Xiangyang is…”

He hesitated.

“…Huang Rong.”

My heart stopped for one beat.

---

# The Night Before the Siege

I rode alone to the walls of Xiangyang.

A woman in white stood upon them, sword in hand.

Moonlight illuminated her face.

It was Rong'er.

Twenty years had passed.

She had grown old.

There was gray in her hair.

But her eyes were still clear.

Still stubborn.

Still—

her eyes.

“Guo Jing.”

She called my name.

Her voice crossed the night wind like ice.

“You finally came.”

“Rong'er…”

“Do not call me Rong'er.”

She interrupted me.

“From the day you chose to become the Mongolian Prince Consort, I ceased to be your Rong'er.”

I said nothing.

“Do you know something?”

She suddenly smiled.

The smile was unbearably sad.

“For twenty years, I have asked myself what would have happened if you had chosen me.”

“I imagined a thousand possibilities.”

“I imagined us defending Xiangyang together.”

“Teaching our children martial arts.”

“Growing old together.”

“But I never imagined this.”

“That one day you would bring the Mongolian army to attack my city.”

Tears finally fell from her eyes.

“Guo Jing, tell me.”

“Was it worth it?”

“Was the wealth and glory worth the price of the Song?”

“Was a marriage to a woman you never loved worth everything between us?”

I opened my mouth.

I wanted to say:

“No.”

I wanted to say:

“I regret it.”

But when the words reached my lips, what came out was:

“Military orders are absolute.”

Four words.

Four blades.

They cut into my heart.

And into hers.

---

# The Fall of Xiangyang

The siege lasted three months.

It was not because the Mongols could not take the city.

It was because I—

delayed.

Every day, I hoped for a miracle.

That reinforcements would arrive.

That Ögedei would suddenly order a retreat.

That—

Rong'er would surrender.

But no miracle came.

The day Xiangyang fell, Rong'er stood upon the highest tower.

Her white robes were stained with blood.

She looked at me.

With the last of her strength, she shouted:

“Guo Jing!”

“I hate you!”

Then she leapt.

Like a white bird falling into the flames.

I did not cry.

I did not move.

I simply stood there and watched the fire consume her.

And consume me.

---

# Year Twenty-Five — Jiangnan

The Mongols conquered the Song.

I was granted the title of Prince of the South and given authority over Jiangnan.

Lin'an.

I walked beside West Lake.

Lotuses were blooming.

Pink and white.

Just as Rong'er had once described them.

She had said:

“Jing-gege, when the world is finally at peace, let's build a little house beside West Lake and watch the lotus flowers every day.”

I had said:

“All right.”

The world became peaceful.

The lotuses bloomed.

But she was gone.

---

# Year Thirty — Awakening

I was old.

My hair had turned completely white.

Every day I sat in the prince's residence and watched the rain over Jiangnan.

Sometimes it lasted all day.

My son came to see me.

“Father,” he asked, “you achieved everything a man could achieve. Why are you never happy?”

I said:

“Because I traded something I should never have traded for something I should never have valued so highly.”

“What do you mean?”

“I traded sincerity for reputation.”

“Morality for power.”

“And…”

I stopped.

“…a woman who loved me for a woman I did not love.”

My son did not understand.

He never would.

Because he was a Mongolian prince.

His world had no pain of choosing.

Only the pleasure of possessing.

Last night, I dreamed of Rong'er again.

She stood among the peach blossoms on Peach Blossom Island.

She smiled.

“Jing-gege, come catch me.”

I ran toward her.

But I could never catch up.

Peach petals covered my body.

Like snow.

Like the snow of Zhangjiakou.

---

# Awakening

“Jing-gege!”

“Jing-gege!”

Someone was shaking me.

I opened my eyes.

It was Rong'er.

She was kneeling beside me, tears covering her face.

“You were having a nightmare,” she said.

“You kept saying, ‘Rong'er, don't jump.’”

I looked at her.

For a long time.

Then I held her tightly.

So tightly that it seemed that if I loosened my arms, she would disappear.

“Rong'er…”

“I am here.”

“I dreamed…”

I struggled to speak.

“I dreamed that I married Hua Zheng.”

“And then?”

“And then…”

I looked away.

“I destroyed Xiangyang.”

“You…”

I could not finish.

She understood.

“You saw me jump.”

She was silent.

Then she gently patted my back.

“It was only a dream.”

“But it felt real.”

“No matter how real it felt, it was still a dream.”

She looked at me.

“The reality is that you chose me.”

“We are together.”

“We defended Xiangyang together.”

I released her and looked into her eyes.

They were still clear.

Still stubborn.

And still—

full of me.

“Rong'er.”

“If I had really chosen Hua Zheng back then, what would you have done?”

She thought for a moment.

“I would have returned to Peach Blossom Island.”

“And never seen you again.”

“And then?”

She smiled.

There were tears in her smile.

“Then I would have cursed you a thousand times a day.”

“I would have cursed you until we grew old.”

“Until we died.”

I laughed.

Then, while laughing—

I cried.

---

# Lantern Festival Morning

Dawn came.

The morning of the Lantern Festival was covered in a thin mist.

Rong'er and I stood upon the walls of Xiangyang and watched the sun rise in the east.

She leaned against my shoulder.

“Jing-gege.”

“Yes?”

“Do you know something?”

“Last night, while you were gone, I thought about many things.”

“What things?”

“What would have happened if you had really chosen Hua Zheng.”

“What would have happened?”

“Perhaps the Mongols would have conquered the Song more quickly.”

“Perhaps you would have become a traitor in the history books.”

“Perhaps I would have opened a little tavern somewhere in Jiangnan and spent my days listening to travelers tell stories about ‘the traitor Guo Jing.’”

She paused.

“But none of that matters anymore.”

“What matters is that you chose me.”

“What matters is that you are here.”

I tightened my grip on her hand.

Her hand was small and warm.

Like the spring wind of Jiangnan.

---

# Afterword: *The Golden Coffer in Ruins*

Many years later, I read a book.

It was called *The Golden Coffer in Ruins*.

It told the story of a Song general named Ma Kuo and his attempt to save the Northern Song as the Jurchen armies swept south.

He tried to turn back history.

He failed.

When I finished the book, I cried.

Because I saw myself in Ma Kuo.

No.

I saw—

the Guo Jing who existed only in my dream.

The Guo Jing who chose Hua Zheng.

The Guo Jing who eventually destroyed Xiangyang.

The Guo Jing who watched Rong'er leap from the city wall.

Ma Kuo's tragedy was that every available choice seemed wrong.

Loyalty to the court could contribute to greater disaster.

Resistance to the court could violate his moral obligations.

It was a tragedy of impossible choice.

It was like the choice in my dream.

Choose Hua Zheng, and I betray Rong'er and the Song.

Choose Rong'er, and I betray Hua Zheng and the Great Khan.

**The Golden Coffer in Ruins.**

A golden vessel missing one corner.

No matter how carefully one repairs it, it can never become completely whole again.

But tonight I finally understand.

Life is not about finding a choice that leaves everything intact.

It is about finding, within inevitable loss,

**the part that is worth protecting.**

My part is Rong'er.

It is Xiangyang.

It is the oath to act **for the country and for the people**.

Even if that oath means betraying another woman who waited twenty years for me.

Even if it means carrying guilt for the rest of my life.

I accept it.

Because **I chose it**.

Not because fate imposed it upon me.

Not because the Great Khan granted it to me.

But because I, Guo Jing, chose this path among countless possible futures.

This path led to the walls of Xiangyang.

To Rong'er's side.

To the city a Han Chinese warrior had sworn to defend.

And it also led—

into the pages of history.

Perhaps my name will be condemned.

But at least it will be a name that was **authentically mine**.

**Guo Jing**

**Recorded at Xiangyang on the Lantern Festival, in the fifteenth year of Jiading**

# Psychological Afterword

Guo Jing's dream can be understood psychologically as an extreme form of **counterfactual thinking**.

By mentally simulating an alternative life trajectory, an individual may:

### 1. Reduce decision regret

Experiencing the imagined costs of the road not taken can reinforce commitment to the decision actually made.

### 2. Complete emotional mourning

Counterfactual simulation allows a person to symbolically say goodbye to an unrealized future.

### 3. Integrate the self-narrative

The decision can be reconstructed as an intentional and meaningful life event rather than merely an accidental consequence of circumstance.

The dream also creates a psychological parallel with the tragedy of Ma Kuo in *The Golden Coffer in Ruins*.

Both narratives reveal the helplessness of individuals confronted by historical forces and the unavoidable losses embedded in meaningful choices.

Yet Guo Jing ultimately achieves psychological integration through **acceptance of the cost of choice**.

This resembles a central insight of existential psychology:

> Once we recognize that human existence is inevitably incomplete, meaningful living does not require eliminating loss. It requires accepting responsibility and continuing to choose.

This may be one of Jin Yong's deepest psychological insights into the human condition.

The crucial question is not simply:

**Which path is objectively perfect?**

It is:

**After choosing a path, how do we continue living with everything we have gained—and everything we have lost?**

## Author's Note

This essay follows an academic-literary approach. The dream sequence is a **literary reconstruction informed by psychological theories of counterfactual thinking, identity formation, moral development, and existential choice**. It does not alter the narrative trajectory of Jin Yong's original *The Legend of the Condor Heroes*.

The diary sections are fictional constructions inspired by the tragic narrative structure of *The Golden Coffer in Ruins*. They are intended not as historical or textual claims about Guo Jing, but as a psychological thought experiment designed to illuminate the meaning of choice, responsibility, identity, and loss.

The central argument is therefore not that Guo Jing literally experienced this dream, but that **counterfactual reconstruction provides a useful psychological lens through which to examine what his alternative choice might have meant**.

In this sense, the imagined Guo Jing who becomes the Golden Saber Prince is not merely an alternative character.

He is the **shadow self of the Guo Jing who chose Huang Rong**—the embodiment of the road not taken.

And perhaps that is why the dream is so painful.

Because every meaningful choice creates, somewhere in the imagination, another self who lived the life we did not.
