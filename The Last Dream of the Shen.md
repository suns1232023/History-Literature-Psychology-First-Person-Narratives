# The Last Dream of the Shen

### A Dying Sea-Mirage Spirit’s Chronicle

**Taiping Xingguo, Year 3 (978 CE), Second Month, Ninth Day — Fog**

I am a **Shen**.

I have lived for thirteen hundred years.

**Today, I am going to die.**

There is, in truth, little to fear.

My kind has never been entirely flesh. We are a breath of the world given temporary form—gathered, we become mist; dispersed, we return to it.

Death is only another kind of dispersal.

I simply never imagined that I would die **in a cooking pot**.

---

## I. The Shen Caught in the Mist

The ones who caught me were little demons under the command of **Zhong Kui**.

They dragged me from a crevice between the rocks along the Eastern Sea, stuffed me into a cloth sack, tied it shut, and carried me all the way to **Bianjing**.

“For the kitchen,” one of them said.

“**The cook wants it.**”

I did not know who the cook was.

Not until they emptied me into a clay basin and I peered through a crack in its rim.

A woman stood beside the stove.

She wore coarse cloth and a stained apron. Her hair was loosely tied behind her head, and the firelight painted her face red.

On the chopping board lay **five knives**, arranged neatly from largest to smallest.

Beside them was a basin of lively yellow fish, their tails beating against the water.

There was also a plate of freshly fried **crispy rice**, golden and fragrant.

She looked at me.

Only once.

Then she picked up the smallest knife and walked toward me.

“**Don't be afraid,**” she said.

“**It will be quick.**”

I believed her.

Her eyes were clean.

They did not look like the eyes of someone about to kill. They looked like the eyes of someone cooking.

A killer's eyes contain violence.

A cook's eyes contain only **timing**.

---

## II. Memories of the Shen: The Eastern Sea and Mount Wuliang

I closed my eyes.

As the knife descended, memories came rushing back.

### The Sunrise Over the Eastern Sea

When I was young, I used to lie on the rocks and watch the sun rise from the water.

The entire sea would turn gold.

My mother once told me:

> “We Shen live too long. You must learn how to forget, or one day there will be no room left in your heart.”

I did not understand her then.

I do now.

### The Island for a Lost Sailor

The first time I exhaled a phantom landscape was for a sailor who had been lost at sea for seven days.

He was close to death.

So I gave him an island.

There were houses on it.

Smoke rose from chimneys.

A woman stood on the shore, waving.

He survived because he believed the island was real.

Eventually, he found the mainland.

I never learned whether he discovered that the island had been my illusion.

It did not matter.

**What mattered was that he lived.**

### The Illusion at Mount Wuliang

Three hundred years ago, I wandered from the Eastern Sea into the southwest and discovered a secluded valley deep within **Mount Wuliang**.

Inside a stone cavern lived a man named **Wuyazi**.

He rarely spoke.

Most of the time, he simply stared at the camellias outside the cave.

I knew he was waiting for someone who would never return.

One evening, I created an illusion for him.

Mist rolled through the camellia grove.

A woman in white appeared among the flowers.

She smiled at him.

He froze.

Then he smiled.

It was the most beautiful smile I had ever seen.

It was the smile of someone who had finally been reunited with the person he had been waiting for all his life.

The illusion lasted only one incense stick.

Then it vanished.

He became the same silent man again.

But for that single incense stick, **he was alive**.

Before I left, I looked at the statue he had carved.

It stood in the deepest part of the cave.

Its face was identical to the woman in my illusion.

But I knew something he did not.

**The illusion had been alive.

The statue was not.**

---

## III. When the Knives Fell

The knife descended.

The smallest one was thin, delicate, almost elegant.

Its edge was razor-sharp.

### The First Knife — Scaling

It passed beneath my gills and lifted away a single scale.

The scale was thin as a cicada's wing, shimmering blue beneath the candlelight.

We Shen do not feel pain.

Only something like an itch.

As though ants were crawling beneath the skin.

### The Second Knife — The Heart

The belly opened.

My organs were exposed.

My heart was still beating.

The cook removed it and placed it in a bowl.

It beat once.

Then stopped.

And suddenly I remembered **Wang Yuyan**.

The granddaughter of Wuyazi.

The girl from Mantuo Villa.

### Wang Yuyan's Illusion

I first saw her in spring, outside Gusu.

She was perhaps fifteen or sixteen, wearing a pale green dress, sitting among the camellias with a martial arts manual open on her knees.

She was reading about the **Dog-Beating Staff Technique**.

I drifted closer.

“Do you understand any of that?”

She looked around.

“Who's there?”

“A Shen.”

“A friend of your grandfather's.”

She blinked.

She wasn't frightened.

“Then make me a building.”

So I did.

I filled the air above **Yanziwu** with mist.

Moonlight appeared.

And in the moonlight stood **Murong Fu**, reaching out his hand toward her.

Wang Yuyan stood by the window and cried.

“It's fake,” she whispered.

“I know it's fake.”

“You still want to see it.”

She nodded.

She watched until dawn.

### The Third Knife — Bone

The third knife was heavier.

It struck the bone.

The sound was clean.

Transparent liquid seeped from the broken marrow.

That liquid contained everything I had accumulated during thirteen hundred years:

landscapes,

faces,

wind,

dreams,

and moments I had never told anyone about.

The cook mixed it with herbs.

I wondered what she was making.

### The Fourth Knife — Flesh

The fourth knife was long and narrow.

It sliced my flesh into thin pieces.

Each slice contained a dream.

The sunrise over the Eastern Sea.

The camellias of Mount Wuliang.

Wang Yuyan crying beside the window.

And one place I had never visited.

**Jinling.**

### The Illusion of Jinling

I had never been there.

But I had heard people speak of it.

The green waters of the Qinhuai River.

The city seen from Phoenix Terrace.

Old plum trees beside painted halls.

A young woman walking beneath the eaves in golden shoes.

For a hundred years I had carried Jinling inside my mind.

I had imagined it countless times.

That was the illusion I created **for myself**.

### The Fifth Knife — The Head

The largest knife had the thickest spine and the broadest blade.

The cook raised it.

She brought it down toward my head.

I did not close my eyes.

I saw the fire.

The yellow fish soup.

The five knives stained with my essence.

The calm face of the cook.

Then—

**the knife fell.**

I dispersed.

My flesh went into the pot.

My heart remained in the bowl.

My essence dissolved into the spices.

I was still there.

Just no longer **in one piece**.

---

# IV. What the Soup Remembered

The fire burned brightly.

The fish soup simmered.

The cook added the fish, the crispy rice, the herbs.

The broth turned golden.

Steam rose.

And suddenly, I realized—

**I could still see.**

I could see the kitchen from inside the soup.

The cook.

The knives.

The stove.

The night over Bianjing.

The mansions glowing in the distance.

And—

**Jinling.**

Someone opened the door.

An old woman entered.

Her hair was completely white.

Her face was lined with wrinkles deep as knife cuts.

But her eyes were extraordinarily clear.

It was **Wang Yuyan**.

She was old now.

An old woman everyone called **Grandmother Wang**.

The cook ladled the soup into a bowl and pushed it toward her.

She took a sip.

Then froze.

Her eyes changed.

They became distant.

Hollow.

Dreamlike.

I knew that expression.

It was the same expression Wuyazi had worn.

The same expression Wang Yuyan had worn when she watched the illusion of Murong Fu.

She was seeing something.

She was seeing **Langhuan Blessed Land**.

The place where she had been born.

The place where she had grown up.

A hidden cavern deep within Mount Wuliang.

Mist-covered cliffs.

Shelves filled with martial arts manuals.

Books she had read as a child.

Camellias outside the cave.

A waterfall running endlessly through the valley.

She saw herself at five or six years old.

Two little buns tied into her hair.

Sitting at the entrance of the cave with a book almost larger than her face.

Sunlight filtered through the flowers.

Her mother was there.

Young again.

Wearing pale blue.

Standing among the camellias.

Calling her.

The little girl tried to run toward her, but her legs were too short.

Her mother laughed.

Then bent down and lifted her into her arms.

She kissed her cheek.

Wang Yuyan saw the white jade statue.

It stood in the deepest part of the cave.

Beautiful.

Serene.

She had once thought it was the statue of some celestial maiden.

Only later did she learn—

It was her grandmother.

She saw Murong Fu.

The first time he came to Langhuan Blessed Land, he was still young.

Only a few years older than she was.

But he already carried himself like a grown man.

He stood before the shelves of books for a long time.

His eyes were shining.

She had not understood that look then.

Later, she learned its name.

**Ambition.**

She saw herself leaving Langhuan Blessed Land with him.

Leaving Mount Wuliang.

Leaving the books.

Leaving the camellias.

She had believed she would follow him forever.

Wherever he wanted to go.

She did not yet know that some places, once abandoned,

**can never be returned to.**

She saw the dry well.

The well in which she had once died—

and somehow lived again.

When she jumped into it, her heart had been empty.

When she woke, she saw Duan Yu's face.

Her heart was still empty.

But slowly, something began to grow there.

Not love.

At least, not the love she had once felt for Murong Fu.

Something else.

Gratitude.

Warmth.

The quiet security of being held in someone's hands.

She saw the royal palace of Dali.

Red walls.

Green tiles.

Carved beams.

She was empress.

She wore gold and jade.

Servants followed wherever she went.

And yet she often sat alone in the garden, staring north.

She knew what lay in that direction.

Gusu.

Mantuo Villa.

Mount Wuliang.

Langhuan Blessed Land.

She saw Duan Yu grow old.

She saw him die.

She saw her son ascend the throne.

Then her grandson.

She watched the people around her disappear, one by one.

She watched her own hair turn white.

Her face grow older.

And finally—

she left the palace.

Her son asked where she was going.

She answered:

> “To a place I should have returned to long ago.”

She went to Mount Wuliang.

But Langhuan Blessed Land was empty.

The shelves remained.

The books were gone.

The books she had read and memorized as a child—

gone.

The cave remained.

The white jade statue was gone.

It had been knocked down and shattered.

She knelt.

She picked up the fragments one by one.

She tried to put them back together.

She could not.

She sat at the entrance of the cave for an entire day.

The camellias still bloomed.

The waterfall still flowed.

Mist still drifted through the valley.

But she knew.

**This was no longer her Langhuan Blessed Land.**

In truth, the place inside her had disappeared long ago.

Perhaps the moment she first walked away.

Tears rolled down her face.

One.

Then another.

They fell into the bowl.

Yet she smiled.

A faint smile.

Like wind passing through camellias and carrying away a single petal.

It was exactly the same smile I had seen on Wuyazi's face.

“I saw it,” she whispered.

The cook said nothing.

She simply added another piece of wood to the fire.

“I saw Langhuan Blessed Land,” Wang Yuyan said.

“I saw myself as a little girl.

I saw my mother.

I saw the jade statue.

And I saw the books.”

The cook nodded.

“What is this?”

She pointed at the soup.

“**A Shen.**”

Wang Yuyan paused.

Then smiled.

“A Shen,” she murmured.

“I saw one when I was young.

In Langhuan Blessed Land.

It once showed me an illusion of my cousin standing beneath the moon, reaching out his hand.

I cried all night.”

She looked down at the remaining soup.

“It was fake,” she said.

“**But I wanted to see it.**”

“And now?” the cook asked.

Wang Yuyan was silent for a long time.

“Now…”

She smiled.

“**Now I don't want to see my cousin anymore.**”

“What do you want to see?”

She touched her chest.

“**That little girl.**”

“The girl who sat at the entrance of the cave reading books.”

“The girl who knew nothing.”

“The girl who had never had to make a choice.”

“The girl who had never loved anyone.”

“The girl who had never left home.”

She lifted the bowl.

And drank the rest.

Slowly.

Every last drop.

“Thank you,” she said.

The cook looked at her.

“Where will you go now?”

Wang Yuyan steadied herself against the stove.

“To the place I should go.”

She walked toward the door.

At the threshold, she turned around.

She looked at the kitchen.

The cook.

The five knives.

The fire.

One last time.

Then she said:

> **“This soup is better than Meng Po's.”**

And she left.

---

# V. Better Than the Soup of Forgetting

The night was deep.

Her figure disappeared into the darkness.

And suddenly I understood.

I knew where she was going.

**The Bridge of the Underworld.**

Everyone goes there eventually.

Cross the Gates of Hell.

Walk the Yellow Springs.

Reach the River of Forgetfulness.

Then cross the bridge.

There sits Meng Po.

She gives every traveler a bowl of soup.

Drink it,

and everything is forgotten.

Whom you loved.

Whom you hated.

Whom you waited for.

Whom you lost.

Everything disappears.

You enter the next life clean,

like a blank sheet of paper.

But Wang Yuyan had said:

> **“This soup is better than Meng Po's.”**

Because Meng Po's soup makes you **forget**.

But my soup—

**makes you remember.**

It gave her back the little girl.

It returned Langhuan Blessed Land.

The camellias.

The books.

Her mother.

The sunlight.

The girl she had once been.

She could carry all of that with her to the Bridge of the Underworld.

And therefore she would not need Meng Po's soup.

Not because she refused it.

Because she **did not need to forget anymore**.

Her heart was already full.

Full of everything that had mattered.

Full of a place that no longer existed.

Full of a little girl who could never return.

**Meng Po's soup makes people forget.

But what cannot be forgotten does not need to be forgotten.**

---

# VI. The Shen Disperses

The sky outside was beginning to pale.

The fog was lifting.

My consciousness was fading.

I could feel myself dissolving.

Returning to the mist.

In my final moment, I saw Wang Yuyan standing on the Bridge of the Underworld.

Meng Po offered her the bowl.

She shook her head.

Then poured the soup into the River of Forgetfulness.

She stepped onto the bridge.

One step.

Then another.

Her face was peaceful.

She was smiling.

It was the same smile I had seen on Wuyazi.

The same smile I had seen on everyone who had ever looked into one of my illusions and found, for a brief moment, something they thought they had lost forever.

The stove fire went out.

Dawn came.

And—

**I dispersed.**

But the warmth of that bowl of soup remained inside her.

**Creative Note**

This is a work of mythological and literary fiction.

The Shen is an imagined supernatural being whose ability to create mirages draws upon traditional Chinese associations between the Shen, mist, atmospheric phenomena, and maritime illusions. The story combines elements from *The Demi-Gods and Semi-Devils*, traditional legends of Zhong Kui, Chinese underworld folklore, and the literary motif of the illusion as a vessel of memory.

Wang Yuyan's later life, the history of Langhuan Blessed Land, the Naihe Bridge, Meng Po, and the Shen's relationship with Wuyazi are deliberately reconstructed as fictional elements.

The central question of the story is not whether an illusion is **real**.

It is whether something false can nevertheless carry a **true human experience**.

A mirage can be unreal.

A memory can be incomplete.

A lost home can no longer exist.

And yet, for the person who sees it—

for one brief moment—

**it may be enough to make a life feel whole again.**
