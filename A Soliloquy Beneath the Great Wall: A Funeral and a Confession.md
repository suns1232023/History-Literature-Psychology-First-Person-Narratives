
## *A Soliloquy Beneath the Great Wall: A Funeral and a Confession*

### *— The Private Diary of Qin Shi Huang*

### A Psychological Introduction

This essay is a **fictional thought experiment** inspired by *The Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*), the legend of Meng Jiangnü, and modern theories of decision-making psychology.

It imagines Qin Shi Huang attending the funeral of Meng Jiangnü and asks several enduring questions:

**How does the architect of a monumental historical project confront individual suffering?**

**Can a ruler at the absolute summit of power still possess the capacity for empathy?**

**When political logic demands that “the greater good” come first, how does a powerful decision-maker perceive, justify, or suppress the suffering of individuals?**

From a psychological perspective, Qin Shi Huang faces a classic form of **cognitive dissonance**: he believes that the Great Wall is necessary for the security of the empire, yet he is confronted with the human tragedy associated with its construction. These two beliefs cannot easily coexist without psychological discomfort.

The fictional diary below explores several possible psychological defenses: **rationalization** (“the sacrifice was unavoidable”), **moral disengagement** (“this was simply the price of the age”), and **emotional distancing** (“an emperor cannot afford weakness”), followed by a brief moment in which empathy breaks through those defenses.

---

# The Diary

### *The Thirty-Seventh Year of the First Emperor, Tenth Month, Twenty-Third Day*

*Winter. Overcast.*

Today, I attended the funeral of that woman.

Her name was Meng Jiangnü—or at least that is what people call her now.

Her true name may have been Meng Zhongzi. It may have been something else entirely.

It no longer matters.

What matters is that she is dead.

She threw herself into the sea after I had agreed to every condition she placed before me.

I stood before her coffin dressed in mourning clothes, wearing white hemp, looking for all the world like a genuine mourner.

Behind me, the ministers knelt in silence, a dark mass of bowed heads.

None dared look up.

They were probably wondering how the First Emperor could possibly wear mourning clothes for a common woman.

It violated ritual.

It violated hierarchy.

It violated the image they had constructed of me—a ruler without mercy, without hesitation, without weakness.

But they do not understand.

There are things that can only be seen from the very summit of power.

---

## I. The Truth Behind the Legend

People in later generations will probably tell a magnificent story.

They will say that Meng Jiangnü wept until eight hundred *li* of the Great Wall collapsed.

They will say that I, the First Emperor, desired her beauty and attempted to take her as a concubine.

They will say that she refused me and eventually threw herself into the sea to die with her husband.

They will turn the story into songs and ballads and sing it across the length and breadth of the realm.

And I will become the villain.

A tyrant.

A lustful emperor.

A monster in human form.

Let them.

History has always been written by those who survive.

And for now, I happen to be the one who has survived.

But today I do not want to talk about victory.

I want to talk about failure.

I want to talk about how the death of one woman can illuminate the darkness inside an entire empire.

In truth, the original story of Meng Jiangnü had nothing to do with me.

According to the old records, her legendary prototype was the wife of Qi Liang, a man of the Spring and Autumn period.

At that time, my ancestors were still tending horses on the western frontier.

Qin had not yet become the empire it would one day be.

Qi Liang died in battle at Ju.

His wife went outside the city to meet his funeral procession and mourned with such extraordinary grief that, according to the *Zuo Zhuan*, the city wall collapsed.

It was originally a story about mourning, ritual, and human emotion.

Nothing about the Great Wall.

Nothing about tyranny.

Nothing about me.

But stories grow.

Like vines climbing a tree, they eventually find the strongest branch around which to wrap themselves.

By the Western Han dynasty, Liu Xiang had added the episode of her throwing herself into the water.

Centuries later, during the Tang dynasty, the poet Guanxiu placed her directly into my era and connected her story with my Great Wall.

By the Ming dynasty, resentment toward massive wall-building projects had become deeply embedded in popular memory.

Qi Liang became Fan Xiliang—or Wan Xiliang.

His wife became Meng Jiangnü.

And I became the tyrant who supposedly tried to seize a grieving woman for himself.

Strange, isn't it?

A virtuous woman from the Spring and Autumn period was dragged across centuries and placed into the Qin dynasty, transformed into a symbol of resistance against my rule.

What does that tell us?

Perhaps it tells us that the Great Wall itself carried a burden of guilt from the beginning.

No matter how magnificent it became.

No matter how many northern raids it prevented.

In the memory of ordinary people, it was always built upon bones and watered with tears.

And the woman lying here today—

Meng Jiangnü—

has become the embodiment of all those tears.

---

# II. The Funeral

I ordered a mourning tent to be erected beside the sea.

I gave her husband the finest coffin available.

That was her first condition:

I was to erect a monument and build a proper tomb for Fan Xiliang.

I did so.

And I did more than she asked.

The monument bears words written in my own hand, although Li Si has always mocked my calligraphy for being “forceful but lacking elegance.”

Her second condition was more outrageous.

She demanded that I wear mourning clothes and personally lead the civil and military officials in a funeral ceremony worthy of a state burial.

When the ministers heard this, their faces turned pale.

Li Si knelt before me, his forehead pressed against the earth.

“Your Majesty,” he said, “this violates every principle of ritual. For the Son of Heaven to personally conduct the funeral of a commoner has no precedent. The world will laugh.”

I laughed.

The sound seemed strangely harsh against the empty coastline.

“Li Si,” I said, “do you think the world lacks reasons to laugh at me already?”

“Burning the books.”

“Burying the scholars.”

“Building a Great Wall across the empire.”

“Leaving countless skeletons beneath it.”

“In their eyes, I have already become a joke. What difference does one more story make?”

I stood.

With my own hands, I removed the black imperial robe.

Then I put on the white hemp garments.

The rough cloth scratched against my skin.

And strangely, it reminded me of my childhood.

Of the years I spent as a hostage in Zhao.

Back then I too wore coarse clothes.

I remember walking through the streets of Handan while people pointed at me and called me a bastard of Qin.

I had forgotten what that felt like.

Until today.

It turns out that even an emperor can recover a little of his humanity by putting on a piece of coarse cloth.

The funeral began.

I walked at the front.

Behind me came Li Si, Zhao Gao, Meng Yi, and hundreds of civil and military officials.

The sea wind pulled at the white funeral banners.

They snapped violently in the air.

The sound reminded me of countless dead voices whispering in my ears.

I ordered Fan Xiliang's remains to be recovered from beneath the Wall.

Yes.

He really had been buried beneath it.

Perhaps not exactly as the later legend would tell it, but the reality was brutal enough.

Twelve years of construction.

Hundreds of thousands of laborers, convicts, and soldiers.

Too many dead.

Too little time.

Sometimes the dead were simply buried where they fell.

Sometimes their remains became part of the earth beneath the wall.

It was a matter of efficiency.

A matter of cost.

I will not defend it.

When we reached the grave, I accepted the incense from an attendant.

I bowed three times.

I ordered the historians to record every detail.

I knew that later generations would write about this ceremony.

They would say I was pretending.

They would say I was acting.

They would say I was trying to win the woman over.

They would never believe that an autocratic ruler could experience a moment of genuine remorse.

But I did.

I was not ashamed for Fan Xiliang.

I was ashamed for her.

---

# III. The Moment I Faced Her

After the funeral, I ordered Meng Jiangnü brought before me.

It was the first time I truly saw her.

Not as a legend.

Not as a symbol.

But as a human being.

She was older than I had imagined.

The journey in search of her husband, the years beneath the open sky, the endless grief—perhaps the legends had exaggerated them, but the marks of hardship were real.

There were deep lines around her eyes.

Her hands were rough.

There was still dirt beneath her fingernails.

But her eyes—

Her eyes were astonishing.

They were like embers remaining after an entire lifetime had burned away.

Calm.

Exhausted.

Desperate.

“Do you hate me?” I asked.

She looked directly at me.

She did not kneel.

She simply raised her head slightly.

The gesture displeased me.

But more than that, it intrigued me.

It had been many years since anyone had looked directly into my eyes like that.

The last man who dared was Jing Ke.

“I would not dare hate Your Majesty,” she said.

Her voice was hoarse.

“Your Majesty is the Son of Heaven. Surely the Son of Heaven has his reasons.”

She paused.

“I simply do not understand.”

“Why build the Wall?”

“To stop the Xiongnu.”

She looked at me.

“The Wall may stop the Xiongnu.”

“But it cannot stop the tears of the people.”

Her voice was quiet.

“My husband and I had been married for only three days when he was taken away.”

“He was a scholar. He could barely hold a hoe.”

“Yet he was forced to carry stones.”

“He died at twenty-five.”

“His bones were buried beneath the Wall.”

“Not even a complete body remained.”

She looked directly at me.

“Your Majesty, what exactly did this Wall protect?”

I said nothing.

What did it protect?

The territory of Qin.

The northern frontier.

The empire I had built.

The dream of a dynasty that would last for ten thousand generations.

But standing before this woman, all those answers suddenly sounded hollow.

Her tears had supposedly brought down the Wall.

Perhaps that was only a legend.

But legends survive because they touch something true.

**The grandest historical narrative can become unbearably small when placed beside the suffering of one human being.**

---

“You made a third demand,” I said, changing the subject.

“You asked to spend three days at sea and then marry me.”

“I agreed.”

“But I knew you would never return.”

She smiled.

There was almost pity in that smile.

“Your Majesty is wise.”

“My body could belong to you.”

“But my heart has already gone with my husband.”

“What use would Your Majesty have for an empty shell?”

“Then why make such a demand?”

She pointed toward the sea.

“So that Your Majesty would look.”

“Look at how vast the sea is.”

“Look at how high the sky is.”

“And look at how many people live beneath it.”

“So many are suffering as I am.”

“You say you built the Wall to protect the people.”

“But what I have seen is a people unable to live.”

I followed the direction of her hand.

The winter sea was gray and restless.

The waves rose and fell as if something beneath them were struggling to breathe.

Far away, several fishing boats were pulling in their nets.

The fishermen were so small that they looked like insects against the horizon.

And for the first time in my life,

I felt completely alone.

---

# IV. A Ruler's Confession

When I returned to the palace, I dismissed everyone.

I sat alone beneath the lamp and opened my diary.

I have kept this habit for many years.

The historians record my public actions.

But some things can only be written for oneself.

Meng Jiangnü's words remained inside my mind like a thorn.

Was I wrong to build the Wall?

From a military perspective, no.

The Xiongnu cavalry moved like wolves across the northern plains.

Without defensive fortifications, the northern commanderies would never know peace.

From a political perspective, perhaps not.

The Six States had only recently been conquered.

The empire needed cohesion.

Large projects could bind the realm together—and absorb the energies of populations that might otherwise turn toward rebellion.

From the perspective of history, perhaps not.

Future generations will remember the Wall.

Just as they remember the pyramids.

Just as they remember Roman roads.

Monuments become symbols of civilization.

But from the perspective of humanity—

What then?

I calculated the numbers once.

Thousands upon thousands of *li* of walls.

Hundreds of thousands of laborers, convicts, and soldiers.

Years of forced labor.

Tens of thousands dead, perhaps more.

The numbers themselves become meaningless after a certain point.

Every stone represents a cost.

And beneath some stones there may be bones.

This is the price.

Every great undertaking has a price.

The unification of the Six States cost generations of war.

Standardizing writing and measurements destroyed old privileges and provoked resistance.

Building the Wall cost labor, hunger, exhaustion, and death.

And the tears of women like Meng Jiangnü.

But there is one question I had never asked.

**Who has the right to decide the price?**

Me?

Because I am the Son of Heaven?

Because I am the First Emperor?

Because I have achieved what no ruler before me achieved?

If political power gives one person the authority to decide who must suffer, then political power itself becomes the greatest form of violence.

Tonight, for the first time,

I began to doubt that logic.

Li Si says governing a country is like controlling water: one must contain it, direct it, discipline it.

Meng Tian says the Xiongnu are wolves; without the Wall, the central plains will never know peace.

They are both right.

And that is precisely what frightens me.

**When everyone is terrifyingly correct, perhaps the problem lies not with the individuals—but with the system itself.**

Meng Jiangnü struck me with her death.

She showed me that beyond every grand historical narrative are countless individual lives.

Her tears could not literally destroy the Wall.

But they could destroy something else.

They could destroy the legitimacy of the story we tell ourselves about why the Wall exists.

Tomorrow, another Meng Jiangnü may appear.

And another.

And another.

I can suppress rebellions.

I can burn books.

I can execute scholars.

I can build walls across mountains and deserts.

But I cannot command the human heart.

I cannot bury memory.

And I cannot build a wall high enough to keep out tears.

---

# V. Power and Loneliness

People say that in my later years I became increasingly superstitious.

They say I sent expeditions in search of immortality.

They say I sought the elixir of life because I was afraid of death.

They are wrong.

I was not afraid of death.

I was afraid of loneliness.

At the summit of power, I could no longer see anyone like myself.

The nobles of the Six States hated me.

The people feared me.

Even my own sons were afraid of me.

Fusu urged me toward benevolent government, so I sent him to the frontier.

Hu Hai was young and knew little beyond pleasing those around him.

Zhao Gao was calculating.

Li Si was brilliant and ambitious.

But none of them—

not one—

could look me in the eyes and say:

**You are wrong.**

Meng Jiangnü did.

Today.

With her life.

It reminds me of my mother.

Zhao Ji.

She brought humiliation and turmoil into my childhood, but she also taught me something I would never forget:

There is one thing in this world that power cannot conquer.

**The human heart.**

Meng Jiangnü's heart belonged to Fan Xiliang.

She crossed thousands of *li* to find him.

She mourned him.

She died for him.

None of it was ultimately about resisting me.

It was about remaining faithful to the person she loved.

That kind of loyalty is stronger than any wall.

Stronger than any law.

I possess the empire.

Yet I cannot possess the heart of one woman.

Perhaps that is not irony.

Perhaps it is destiny.

---

# VI. Epilogue: The Last Night by the Sea

Tonight I went to the sea alone.

Meng Jiangnü's body had been recovered.

According to her wishes, she was buried beside Fan Xiliang.

I stood before the new grave.

The waves struck the rocks with a deep, monotonous rhythm.

Like the heartbeat of the earth.

“Your Majesty.”

Zhao Gao's voice came from behind me.

“It is late. We should return to the palace. Tomorrow, Your Majesty must continue the eastern inspection tour.”

I did not turn around.

“Zhao Gao.”

“How will later generations judge me?”

He was silent for a moment.

Then he answered cautiously.

“Your Majesty's achievements surpass those of the Three Sovereigns. Your virtue exceeds the Five Emperors. Surely posterity will remember you as the greatest emperor of all.”

“Remember me?”

I laughed bitterly.

“They will call me a tyrant.”

“They will call the Wall a monument to oppression.”

“They will turn Meng Jiangnü's tears into a banner of resistance.”

“They will invent countless stories.”

“And in every one of them, I will be the villain.”

“That is merely the foolish talk of common people,” Zhao Gao said. “Why should Your Majesty care?”

“I do not care.”

I turned and looked at the eunuch who had followed me for so many years.

“But what concerns me is this.”

“Perhaps they are not entirely wrong.”

Zhao Gao lowered his head.

He said nothing.

I turned back toward the sea.

The dark water reflected the stars.

They looked like countless eyes watching me.

The laborers who died beneath the Wall.

The families driven from their homes.

The people crushed beneath the machinery of empire.

And perhaps all those who would die in the wars still to come.

Their eyes were everywhere.

Among the stars.

In the sea.

Inside the darkness.

“Send an order,” I said suddenly.

“Reduce next year's forced labor levies for the Wall.”

“Give the workers more grain.”

“Those who die must be properly buried.”

“No more bodies are to be incorporated into the foundations.”

Zhao Gao looked up in astonishment.

“Your Majesty?”

“Do it.”

I knew it would not change much.

The Wall still had to be built.

The northern frontier still had to be defended.

The machinery of empire, once set in motion, could not simply be stopped.

But at least tonight—

beside this woman's grave—

I wanted to make some small compensation.

Not for her.

For myself.

For the part of me that still remembered what it meant to be human.

Tomorrow I would put on the black imperial robes again.

I would wear the twelve-stringed crown.

I would once again become the First Emperor—the ruler whom everyone feared.

But tonight,

let me be a human being once more.

A human being who can regret.

A human being who can question himself.

A human being who can still shed tears.

### *The Thirty-Seventh Year of the First Emperor, Tenth Month, Twenty-Fourth Day*

*Clear.*

---

# Afterword

Today we departed for the eastern inspection tour.

I looked back once.

The new grave beside the sea had disappeared into the morning mist.

Last night I dreamed of Meng Jiangnü.

She was standing upon the Great Wall.

Her white robes moved in the wind.

She was not crying.

She simply looked at me.

Behind her stood countless shadowy figures.

The laborers who had died beneath the Wall.

They said nothing.

They only watched me.

I woke covered in sweat.

Perhaps this is my destiny.

To build one of the greatest monuments in human history.

To carry one of its heaviest burdens.

To become one of the loneliest rulers who ever lived.

Future generations will find fragments of bones between the stones of the Wall.

They will read Meng Jiangnü's tears between the lines of history.

They will tell stories, generation after generation, about the tyrant and the woman who refused him.

They may forget my unification of the realm.

They may forget the administrative systems I created.

They may forget my attempt to construct an empire that would last for ten thousand generations.

Perhaps they will remember only this:

There was once an emperor who built a great wall.

Countless people died building it.

And a woman named Meng Jiangnü died grieving for her husband.

Is that fair?

Perhaps not.

But history has never promised fairness.

History remembers.

And memory belongs to those who leave tears behind.

I have spent my entire life trying not to cry.

Perhaps I left all my tears beside that grave—

on that night when I wore mourning clothes.

Let later generations judge me as they wish.

The Wall will stand.

I will fall.

But the tears will continue to flow through the river of legend, reminding every person who ever holds power of one simple truth:

**No achievement is greater than the human heart.**

**No wall is longer than a tear.**

## Academic Note

This work is a **fictional historical narrative informed by historical psychology**.

The psychological interiority attributed to Qin Shi Huang is not presented as a recoverable historical fact. Rather, it is a literary reconstruction informed by surviving historical accounts, theories of decision-making, moral psychology, power and social isolation, cognitive dissonance, and the relationship between individual experience and collective historical memory.

The funeral of Meng Jiangnü, Qin Shi Huang's private reflections, and the dialogue between the two are **literary inventions**.

The purpose is not to establish that these events occurred.

It is to ask a different question:

**What happens psychologically when the person who believes he is building history is forced to confront the human beings who must pay for it?**

The legend of Meng Jiangnü provides an unusually powerful narrative vehicle for examining the tension between **historical necessity and individual suffering, political rationality and moral responsibility, institutional power and human empathy**.

The result is therefore best understood not as a conventional historical biography, but as an experiment in **historical imagination**—an attempt to place a ruler at the center of the very human tragedy that later generations would use to judge him.

And perhaps that is the question that remains after the Wall, the empire, and the emperor have all disappeared:

**When history calls something necessary, who gets to decide what the necessary sacrifice is—and who gets to remember the people who paid it?**
